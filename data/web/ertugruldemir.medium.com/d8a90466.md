# Reasoning Models at Home

**How to Train Your Own Specialized Reasoning Model at Home**

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*5EBaGphvbsLei58Z0TGQuA.jpeg)

Reasoning models are not entirely new, but the release of DeepSeek’s R1 model sparked significant excitement within the open-source community. After reading the **DeepSeek-R1** paper, I became particularly interested in their **post-training** technique, which reportedly leads to substantial performance improvements.

The traditional post-training method is **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**. In SFT, the model is provided with prompts and desired outputs to learn user preferences, structural alignment, safety protocols, and more. Essentially, SFT demonstrates what developers or users expect from the model, guiding it to align with these examples.

Since post-training has been mentioned, it’s worth briefly discussing **pre-training** before delving further into R1’s post-training approach, as the two are related. One of the initial steps in training Large Language Models (LLMs) is** Causal Language Modeling**. In this stage, the model learns to predict the next token in a sequence by attending to the preceding tokens. This process yields “**Base**” models, which are trained on vast amounts of public text data in a semi-supervised manner, without explicit labels, to predict subsequent tokens. Speaking of public text data, we are nearing its depletion (to some extent, disregarding other modalities in this context). This scarcity is one of the primary drivers for researchers to concentrate on the post-training phase of LLMs.

Returning to post-training, numerous techniques exist, such as **SFT, PPO, and DPO**, among others. While some methods perform better in specific domains, they often limit creative freedom for instruction alignment or become prohibitively expensive for extended runs. Conversely, base models lack these limitations but are unpredictable, difficult to instruct, and often produce outputs that are barely comprehensible to humans.

The authors of R1 discovered a method to achieve the best of both worlds: aligning raw models to instructions while preserving a degree of creative freedom. I won’t detail the entire process here, but fundamentally, they allow the base model to **“reason”** with considerable freedom, while setting simple objectives focused on accuracy and format. To achieve this, they employed **Reinforcement Learning**, implementing an optimization technique called **“GRPO” (Group Relative Policy Optimization)**. GRPO eliminates the need for a critic model — traditionally the same size as the policy model and used in methods like PPO/DPO** **— and instead calculates a baseline using aggregated group scores.

Applying this method directly to base models resulted in strong reasoning capabilities, leading to the first version of the R1 model: **DeepSeek-R1-Zero**. Although this model improved the reasoning capabilities of base models, its outputs were often difficult for humans to read or interpret. As mentioned, SFT methods help models adhere to human preferences and readability. However, we also discussed how SFT can stifle creative freedom and generalization, as demonstrated in the paper: “**SFT Memorizes, RL Generalizes**: A Comparative Study of Foundation Model Post-training.”

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*hZq8vVwGfwdkm2Ik_3MmNA.jpeg)

Therefore, the authors utilized a small, high-quality dataset to “**cold start**” their models before the **Reinforcement Learning** step. This approach was instrumental in the creation of the renowned DeepSeek-R1 model. In the next chapter, we will explore the implementation of some of these techniques to achieve similar results.

### Force to Reason

In their report, DeepSeek mentioned a new set of **distilled models**. These significantly smaller models were trained using a distillation technique with SFT, leveraging the outputs of DeepSeek-R1. Notably, these distilled models did not undergo any Reinforcement Learning steps, which is an interesting point and an area worth exploring for us. Therefore, instead of distilling models, **what if we implement GRPO directly on the original models?**

We could proceed with either base models or instruct models, assuming the instruct models have already been “cold started.” Given that this is an experiment conducted on my local gaming rig equipped with an RTX 3090, I have chosen the Qwen2.5–0.5B-Instruct model to begin with. It’s a model with a very small number of parameters for an LLM, and while its limitations are uncertain, it’s worth experimenting with.

### Setting Up the Rewards

In the original paper, the authors utilized a large **Mixture of Experts (MoE)** model with **671B parameters**. This substantial model size allowed them to provide the model with considerable latitude, fostering freedom, robustness, and creativity. However, in my case, working with a 0.5B parameter model (yes, you read that right!), I felt it necessary to implement **stricter rules** to guide the model effectively. The original DeepSeek-R1 model employed two primary rewards for reward modeling: **Accuracy and Format**. These two rewards were designed to assess the correctness of the final response and whether the formatting incorporated reasoning steps enclosed within <think> and </think> tags.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*TMLedvq5v-_OG7UbYyczqQ.png)

In my more constrained scenario, I incorporated a broader set of rules to reward or penalize the model:

- Is the answer correct?
- Does it follow the suggested format?
- Does it include reasoning tags?
- If reasoning tags are present, what is length within the thinking process?
- Does it include validation tags?
- If validation tags are present, what is the length of self-critique?
- Does it utilize an “Aha!” moment marker?

I recognize that some of these rewards are subjective and may not generalize broadly. However, as mentioned, I believed that several of these were essential for a smaller model and to facilitate faster training. Prepare yourself for the real arbitrary reward points in the code! Furthermore, it’s important to consider the dataset we are employing, as several of these rules are tailored to its specific characteristics. The dataset is **GSM8K** **(Grade School Math 8K)**, which comprises linguistically diverse grade school math word problems. This dataset was specifically created to support question answering tasks on basic mathematical problems that necessitate multi-step reasoning. Consequently, some of these rules are task-specific to this dataset and **may not generalize well**. Nevertheless, allow me to explain the rationale behind some of these reward choices.

- For accuracy reward we’re going to extract final answer between
**<answer></answer>**tags as predictions and check the accuracy of them with ground truth answers. - The formatting part is checking for
**tags for reasoning, criticizing and answer**parts to follow general structure.

These two are similar to original paper but rest is just up to your task and creativity.

- To encourage longer thinking processes I
**count the number of words**between reason and validate tags and add that as a reward in a scale up to some extent. (To prevent model exploiting useless long answers)

The original work mentioned **“Aha!”** moment happened by itself while reasoning. Although it’s possible for bigger models with longer training procedures, in my scenario I set a cheesy game rule to force this moment.

In a recent paper **s1: Simple test-time scaling**, authors inserted a “**Wait**” token at the point where models preparing to end their predicted sequence, to force them rethink. It sound similar to “Aha!” moment so I just **took this idea and inserted into my reward function**.

**Wait, but or rethink**” and followed by minimum of characters I set huge bonus for reward to encourage this kind of thinking, and also set that into system prompt.

## Code Time

Okay, I think we’ve covered enough of the theoretical background. Now, let’s dive into the actual code implementation. We’ll begin by creating utility functions for several key components: setting up the system prompt, extracting answers from the dataset, extracting content between specified tags, and loading the **GSM8K **dataset itself.

`import re`

import torch

from datasets import load_dataset, Dataset

from trl import GRPOConfig, GRPOTrainer


from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize


SYSTEM_PROMPT = """

Respond in the following format:

<reasoning>

[Break and reason your answer here]

</reasoning>

<validate>

[Criticize your reason and think about it. If you see somethin start with 'wait']

</validate>

<answer>

[Final integer answer justified by validate]

</answer>

"""


def extract_hash_answer(text: str) -> str | None:

if "####" not in text:

return None

return text.split("####")[1].strip()


def extract_xml_answer(response, tag="answer"):

import re

match = re.search(rf'<{tag}>(.*?)</{tag}>', response, re.DOTALL)

if match:

return match.group(1).strip()

return ""


def get_gsm8k_questions(split = "train") -> Dataset:

data = load_dataset('openai/gsm8k', 'main')[split]

data = data.map(lambda x: {

'prompt': [

{'role': 'system', 'content': SYSTEM_PROMPT},

{'role': 'user', 'content': x['question']}

],

'answer': extract_hash_answer(x['answer'])

})

return data


dataset = get_gsm8k_questions()

Next, we’ll implement one of the most important pieces: the **Reward Function**. I created this based on the points I mentioned in the previous chapter, but feel free to tinker or write your own.

`def custom_reward_func(prompts, completions, answer, min_reasoning_length=10, **kwargs) -> list[float]:`

responses = [completion[0]['content'] for completion in completions]

q = prompts[0][-1]['content']

extracted_responses_answer = [extract_xml_answer(r, tag="answer") for r in responses]

extracted_responses_reasoning = [extract_xml_answer(r, tag="reasoning") for r in responses]

extracted_responses_validate = [extract_xml_answer(r, tag="validate") for r in responses]


rewards = []

for original_response, extracted_answer, extracted_reasoning, extracted_validate in zip(

responses, extracted_responses_answer, extracted_responses_reasoning, extracted_responses_validate

):

is_correct = (extracted_answer == answer[0])

is_int = extracted_answer.isdigit()

has_answer_tags = "<answer>" in original_response and "</answer>" in original_response

has_reasoning_tags = "<reasoning>" in original_response and "</reasoning>" in original_response

has_validate_tags = "<validate>" in original_response and "</validate>" in original_response

reasoning_length = len(word_tokenize(extracted_reasoning.lower()))

validate_length = len(word_tokenize(extracted_validate.lower()))


reward = 0.0

reasoning_reward = 0.0


if is_correct:

reward += 5.0

if is_int:

reward += 0.5


if has_validate_tags:

reward *= 1.25

if validate_length >= 5:

min_validate_length = 5

max_validate_length = 256

max_validate_bonus = 3.0

if validate_length >= min_validate_length:

if validate_length >= max_validate_length:

validate_bonus = max_validate_bonus

else:

validate_bonus = ((validate_length - min_validate_length) / (max_validate_length - min_validate_length)) * max_validate_bonus

else:

validate_bonus = 0.0

else:

validate_bonus = 0.0

else:

validate_bonus = 0.0


if has_reasoning_tags:

reward *= 1.25

if reasoning_length >= 5:

min_scaling_length = 5

max_scaling_length = 1024

max_scaling_bonus = 10

if reasoning_length <= min_scaling_length:

reasoning_reward = 0.0

elif reasoning_length >= max_scaling_length:

reasoning_reward = 5.0

else:

reasoning_reward = ((reasoning_length - min_scaling_length) / (max_scaling_length - min_scaling_length)) * max_scaling_bonus

else:

reasoning_reward = 0.0

else:

reasoning_reward = 0.0


total_reward = reward + reasoning_reward + validate_bonus


if has_validate_tags:

validate_lower = extracted_validate.lower()

if re.search(r"(wait|but|rethink)(?=.{20,})", validate_lower, re.DOTALL):

total_reward *= 10.0


rewards.append(total_reward)


return rewards

Okay, we’re done with utility functions and the reward function. Now, let’s get into the training script. For efficient and faster training with limited compute power, we’re going to use some libraries and techniques such as **Unsloth, trl, vLLM, and LoRA**, etc.

I am not going into deep details about these in this article but will mention their role in this pipeline.

Basically Unsloth allows optimized models to train with a smaller memory footprint and faster iterations.

`from unsloth import FastLanguageModel, PatchFastRL`

PatchFastRL("GRPO", FastLanguageModel)



max_seq_length = 1024

lora_rank = 32


model, tokenizer = FastLanguageModel.from_pretrained(

model_name = "unsloth/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct-unsloth-bnb-4bit",

max_seq_length = max_seq_length,

load_in_4bit = False,

fast_inference = True, # Enable vLLM fast inference

max_lora_rank = lora_rank,

gpu_memory_utilization = 0.8,

)


model = FastLanguageModel.get_peft_model(

model,

r = lora_rank,

target_modules = ["q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj",

"gate_proj", "up_proj", "down_proj"],

lora_alpha = lora_rank*2,

use_gradient_checkpointing = "unsloth",

random_state = 1907,

)

By applying **LoRA we are training a small subset of model parameters**. Then we set** trl** training arguments and the trainer itself.

`output_dir="outputs/Qwen-.5B-GRPO"`


training_args = GRPOConfig(

output_dir=output_dir,

run_name=run_name,

learning_rate=3e-5,

adam_beta1 = 0.9,

adam_beta2 = 0.99,

weight_decay = 0.1,

)


trainer = GRPOTrainer(

model=model,

processing_class=tokenizer,

reward_funcs=[custom_reward_func],

args=training_args,

train_dataset=dataset,

)

trainer.train()

And let the magic happen! These hyperparameters are probably far from optimal (the same goes for other components too), but I believe the open-source community will find better approaches in no time. (My current setup is built upon GRPO Llama-1B).

As you can see above, the model consistently increased its output by lengthening its reasoning and validation steps. At some point, it stopped increasing though, and there are a couple of reasons for that:

**Lack of max_completion_length limit:**As the reasoning steps get longer, the model starts to cut out the final answer, therefore not getting the important accuracy reward. (The limit I set in trainer)**Lack of model capability:**This might sound like an excuse, but for a 0.5B parameter model, it really matters.**Reward function upper limits**for reasoning lengths. (Remember we set limit to max gain from length…)

## Conclusion: Reasoning at Home — Just the Beginning!

So, there you have it! We took a tiny 0.5B parameter model, threw some GRPO at it in our home lab (or, you know, gaming rig!), and managed to teach it some pretty decent reasoning skills, at least for math problems. Who would’ve thought, right? While our little Qwen-GRPO isn’t going to be replacing DeepSeek-R1 anytime soon, the jump in accuracy we saw is genuinely exciting. It proves, even with limited resources, we can start to play with these advanced techniques and push the boundaries of what smaller models can do.

This whole experiment really underscores a key point: **reasoning models aren’t just for massive labs with endless compute**. You can tinker with this stuff at home, learn a ton, and even get surprisingly good results. Sure, there’s still a long way to go — generalization, more robust rewards, maybe even figuring out how to make that “Aha!” moment less cheesy — but this is just the beginning. The open-source community is buzzing with ideas, and I can’t wait to see what amazing reasoning models we can all build together, brick by brick, or maybe, line of code by line of code! The future of reasoning at home? It’s looking surprisingly bright, and I, for one, am strapped in and ready for the ride!
