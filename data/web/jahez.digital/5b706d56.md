# From Craving to Action: Building an AI Powered Search Engine for Jahez

## Overview

Search is the entry point for food delivery customers. It affects multiple high-impact business metrics: conversion rates, session times, retention, and numerous other secondary metrics. Jahez serves millions of customers in the GCC and the vast majority of them rely on search to find their cravings. Given the high impact search functionality has on Jahez, we needed to make the product as optimal as it can be, in both performance and quality. Turns out we have areas to improve and huge business gains could come from these improvements. With the current advancements of AI and machine learning, we have the toolkit to uplift the quality of the results. In this article, we will go over both the business and the technical challenge of building a state of the art search engine that processes millions of queries daily.

## Gaps in The Previous Search

There are a lot of aspects of the previous search at Jahez that are providing a suboptimal experience for our users, below we’ll discuss the most impactful ones.

**Relevance:**The previous search accounted only for distance and basic match text ranking to sort the result, it doesn’t take into account how popular the restaurant is, or whether the user likes the restaurant or not.**No Alternatives:**Sometimes having richer options for what the user has in mind might help them discover new restaurants or meals.**Long Session Time:**Having a limited set of results when users don’t have a specific restaurant in mind increases time taken to order.**Empty Results:**After analyzing searches for a period of time, we found that around 50% of searches provide no results at all. This directly leads to a high probability of churn, if, for example, a user had a restaurant in mind, but was not able to find it.

## Understanding what users really want

Before we deep dive into building the product, we first need to understand the user’s intent and the prominent cases that we need to account for. Most search use cases are split into two parts, retrieving results and then ranking those results. For Retrieval we looked at the historical search queries that users searched for and we ended up with the following.

### Search cases

**Exact intent:**When the search is a correctly-spelled restaurant name. An example would be if a user wrote “Kudu” and they had Kudu Restaurant in mind.**Spelling mistakes:**Sometimes restaurant brand names might be tricky and have duplicated characters by design. This makes it harder for users to find those restaurants. A good example would be “برغراززير” (Burgerizzer). The name has 2 main challenges. In colloquial Arabic, it’s very common to use the character “غ”, “ق”, “ج” in the word “Burger”. Also, the brand has repetitive character “زز” (“zz”) which is a misspelled from a lexical perspective,This makes it tricky to easily find the restaurant through exact match search as most queries for this restaurants are “برقرازير” (Burgerizer).*but the brand wanted to convey trendiness.***Partial match:**To make it faster for users to find their best match, you want to be able to provide results with least possible characters, for example, to get “خطوة جمل” (Camel Step) a user should be able to find it by only “خطوة” (Camel ..).**Semantic meaning:**For a more meaningful output, the results must include a semantic (meaning) understanding of the query. If a user wanted to find restaurants through cuisine names or meal names, we want to provide restaurants closely clustered around these queries. For example, a query like “سوشي” (Sushi) should provide Japanese restaurants along with the restaurants that have “سوشي” (Sushi) in their name. We also want to provide similar cuisine restaurants, so if a user searches for “ماريز” (A shawerma place) instead of only showing the restaurant Mareez, we want to also provide: Mama Noura, Shawarma House, etc .. as they are similar in cuisines. This is a major upgrade which will account for many cases.**Unavailable Restaurants:**In some cases, users search for restaurants that are either not available in their city, or not available in Jahez at all. There’s an extremely high probability that this user will end their session after not finding the restaurant. To decrease the probability that they leave, we want to provide them with similar restaurants by cuisine or by craving. We can do this by leveraging what user ordered in the past after searching for a available restaurant. Two of the most common cases are “ساروجة” (Saruja — Grills Restaurant) and “WBJ” (burger joint). In these cases, showing Grills/Lebanese Restaurants and Burger Joints will increase the chance that the user will not churn, because we answered their crave.**Multi-level match:**Apart from finding restaurant matches, we wanted to take into account item or item characteristic searches. Some users search for popular items like “Whopper” or “بيج ماك” (Big Mac), in those cases, the result should be Burger King and McDonald’s respectively. Other users search for item adjectives like “Grilled”, “حراق” (Spicy), etc ..

## Making Sense of results

### Relevance

Aside from retrieving results, we wanted to bring more context to the outputs. This helps us clean up the big pool of results and sort them to be relevant. We relied on multiple factors to help us achieve relevance.

**Store Popularity:**We measure popularity by number of orders, taking into account recency of orders**User Preference:**User’s historical orders play a big role in user’s future selection, so we take into account their past orders with time decay to give more value for recency**Store Distance:**It’s important to take into account how far the restaurant is from the user for competing keywords. In order to help providing a better service, it makes sense to bias closer restaurants.**Search History:**Since Jahez has been operating for many years, we had a big chunk of search interactions history. So we relied on how many times did a query lead to an order from a restaurant. Each query restaurant combination was taken into account. For example “قهوة” (Coffee) might lead to multiple restaurants. The more the restaurant gets clicked, the higher the relevance to users.


“قهوة” → “Coffee Address”

“قهوة” → “Starbucks”

“قهوة” → “Dunkin Donuts”

**Result Type:**The relationship between the query and each restaurant in the pool of results is crucial for sorting. If we match a restaurant through exact match, it makes sense to show it above another restaurant that we matched through semantic match. For a query like “بريسكت” (Brisket) , we need to nudge restaurants named “Brisket” above restaurants like “7 Ribs”. The latter is a semantic match for the word “بريسكت” (Brisket).**Hierarchy:**An important distinction as well is whether the match is for a restaurant or an item. For example, if a user searched for the word “كودو” (Kudu), the Kudu Restaurant should be ranked higher than “ساندويتش كودو” (Kudu Sandwich) which is a common steak sandwich name for many restaurants.

### Ranking

To build a strong relevant search engine, we built a scoring algorithm that ranks the pool of results through the relevance components above (popularity, preference, distance, history, hierarchy and type). We wanted to account for as many common cases as possible, so we relied on a weighting algorithm to score each result. The final score is based on a two branch function, one for boosting based on which search stream the results is coming from and how strong is the text score (aka BM25), and the other is for relevance factors. Each factor has a different contribution to the overall score of the factors, depending on how important it is.

Let’s say a user searches for “شاورما” (Shawerma). At Jahez, we have hundreds of restaurants that contain the word “شاورما” (Shawerma) in their name and many others that offer “شاورما” (Shawerma) but don’t have the word in their brand name. The ranker first takes BM25 score, which biases restaurants containing the word, then looks at the popularity, user relevance, distance, and search history of the word, then boost or deboost restaurants accordingly. If the user likes a restaurant and it’s popular but doesn’t have the word “شاورما” (Shawerma) in its brand name, like Mama Noura, it is still likely to show as top 10 because it scores higher than others that have “شاورما” (Shawerma) in their brand name in most factors.

## AI Powered Search

Restaurant names are not expressive enough to match with all search queries. In order to account for this, we built a document for each restaurant that holds a bigger information set that better unpacks what the full meaning of a restaurant actually is. In the document we added the following metadata about the restaurant:

- Main cuisines
- Top 10 most sold items
- Restaurant Tagline
- Tags that describe the restaurant (“Local”, “Burgers”, etc ..)

Then we created embedding vectors for these documents and stored them in a vector database. At query time, we take the cosine similarity of the query and the document embeddings and rank them accordingly. This operation has greatly enriched the search pool and allowed us to capture more matching restaurants especially for long tail queries

## Software Deep Dive

Given the complexity of the problem, it had to be broken into three layers: analytical layer, search layer, and ranking layer. The analytical layer is where analytical information is computed using a variety of data points such as users preferences and trending restaurants, and this is also where we analyze the engagement of the users with the search results for further enhancement. The search layer is where we do query sanitization, caching the query heuristics, and concurrent search calls across different search streams. The ranking layer is where the ranking algorithm does its magic, based on mathematical formulae and preassigned weights for each search stream.

### Tech Stack

**Snowflake**: The foundation of our analytics is built on Snowflake, and that’s where we build our data use cases and models**Cohere**: Embeddings are generated through Cohere multilingual embedding model, which we found to be the most powerful in embedding Arabic and Multilingual queries and documents at scale.**ElasticSearch**: A search engine optimized for storing and fast retrieval of data including restaurants, vectors, user preferences, and other data points.**Golang**: Powerful as a backend with rich support for concurrency, making it ideal for high performance applications.**Amplitude**: Event aggregation for user behaviour and capturing how they interact with the search results.

### Query Heuristics

In order to understand the intent of the user, we need to enrich the query with metadata related to the cuisine the user is interested in, as well as generating an embedding of the query so we could implement cosine similarity search to enrich our results. Part of the problem is that this will lead to a sequential step that will cause performance degradation. In order to overcome that, we precomputed query heuristics for popular queries captured within the app. Example:

**Query**: هرفي (Herfy)**Lexical Cuisine**: Fast Food**Semantic Cuisine**:Traditional (Fun question: Can you guess why?)**Embedding Vector:**[0.0721435546875, … , -0.048553466796875]

### Performance Consideration

Initially when we first started developing the search engine, we came across so many hurdles that affected the performance, both within Elastic and the backend application. For optimization purposes we did the following:

- Precomputing query heuristics instead of doing it at runtime
- Caching the query heuristics in the backend
- Utilizing Elastic Multi-Search API for concurrent requests
- Limiting fuzzy match to restaurants
- Query timeouts within Elastic

## Business Impact

To properly assess the impact of the smart search engine, we relied on two primary metrics: Annual Run Rate Lift and Decrease in Searches Per Session. The first one communicates business value, while the latter measures experience impact. ARR lift looks at how much revenue was added to the daily average revenue coming for search orders in relation to the previous search engine. While Searches per Session focuses on the number of search attempts, in which the lower the ratio the better, because it entails that users are searching less to achieve their desired result.

The rollout of the service was gradual, staring with a random sample of 25% of the users, and a weekly increase of 25%. This helped us assess the impact of the above metrics. The smart search service is projected to generate tens of millions of riyals annually more than the previous service. This is mainly driven by the fact that smart search covers more use cases, is less sensitive to spelling mistakes, and provides a variety of options and alternatives. Smart search also achieved a reduction in searches per session, which sped up session to order times. Aside from direct business impact, we were able to utilize only 25% of the previous search resources to operate the smart search service, thus reducing the operating cost of the search engine.

## Future enhancements

A lot of the decisions that we made for the new ranking functionality is based on educated assumptions and our experience as users of the app. Therefore, there is bias in our choices that may result in suboptimal outcomes in long tailed queries. With that in mind, we’re planning to launch more enhancements in the coming weeks. After collecting enough user interactions with the new search, we will replace the ranking algorithm with a machine learning ranking model. We will also build a custom embedding model to improve semantic search.
