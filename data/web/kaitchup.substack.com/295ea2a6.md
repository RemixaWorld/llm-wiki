When running LLMs locally, GGUF is by far the most popular format. It’s compact, easy to distribute, and works seamlessly with inference frameworks like `llama.cpp` or user-friendly tools like `ollama`.

But it’s important to realize that GGUF models are not the same as the original releases. Unless you’re using a full 16-bit version, what you’re running is a quantized version of the model. That means the weights have been compressed, usually down to 8-bit, 4-bit, or even 2-bit precision, to reduce size and often speed up inference.

This kind of quantization tends to preserve accuracy quite well, especially at 4-bit, and for English tasks with short to medium-sized inputs. But past research shows that in certain settings, like [long sequences](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20276>) or [multilingual inputs](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03211>), the quality can drop noticeably.

There's also another problem that’s easy to overlook: the inference settings. When a model is released, the authors usually recommend a set of default values for temperature, top-p, and so on. These are tuned to get good results on standard benchmarks. But once the model is quantized, its internal probability distribution shifts. The original hyperparameters may no longer be optimal.

In this article, I show that this is especially true for very low-bit models, such as those quantized to 2-bit (e.g., Q2_K format). I found that quantized models tend to be much more sensitive to temperature and top-p. Even small changes can lead to noticeable drops in accuracy.

To explore this, I ran 300 different hyperparameter combinations across Qwen3 models, using several quantization methods such as AWQ, bitsandbytes 4-bit, and GGUF at both 2-bit and 4-bit levels. I used vLLM for inference and evaluated performance using IFEval’s accuracy.

## Temperature, Top P, and Min P: What Are They Controlling during LLM Inference?

At its core, LLM inference is just repeated sampling from a probability distribution over the next token. Each time the model runs, it gives you a vector of logits, which are raw scores for each possible next token. Before sampling, these logits go through a few transformation steps (mainly) controlled by `temperature`, `top_p`, and `min_p`.1 These settings shape how the model chooses words and let you steer the output toward more predictable or more creative results.

**Temperature** is the first step in most pipelines. It rescales the logits before softmax. A temperature of `1.0` means no change; what the model gives is what you sample from. Lowering it below 1 sharpens the differences between tokens: high-probability words become even more likely, and rare ones nearly disappear. This makes the output more deterministic. Raising the temperature above 1 flattens the distribution, increasing randomness. Tokens that normally wouldn't be chosen start to show up more often. At `temperature = 0`, only the highest logit survives. The model always picks the single most likely token, making the whole generation a greedy, deterministic path. This can cause problems in longer completions, like loops or broken structure, because the model loses its ability to back off to second-best options.

**Top-p** (nucleus sampling) works differently. Instead of rescaling, it cuts off the low-probability tail of the distribution. The model keeps just enough tokens to reach a total probability mass of `p`, then renormalizes. So, with `top_p = 0.9`, only the top few tokens (whose combined probability is at least 90%) are kept. In confident contexts, that might be 2–3 tokens. In uncertain ones, it could be dozens. Higher values allow the model to reach into less common word choices, rare names, or creative phrasing. At `top_p = 1`, nothing is filtered: the full distribution is kept. In that case, `top_p` has no effect, and only temperature (and possibly `min_p`) will control the output.

**Min-p** adds a final filter. After scaling and optional top-p trimming, any token with a probability below `min_p` is removed. This is a hard threshold: if a token is under the floor, it can’t be chosen at all. By default, `min_p = 0`, so nothing is blocked. But setting a small value like `1e−3` can help avoid strange outputs such as random control characters, odd punctuation, or malformed syntax. If you set it too high, though, the model might get stuck. For example, it may need a rare word to complete a sentence correctly, but that word’s been excluded.

Together, these settings form a spectrum of behavior:

  * **Low temperature + low top-p + non-zero min-p** → safe, repeatable output. Great for code, tables, or structured answers.

  * **Medium temperature + medium top-p + min_p = 0** → balanced, readable prose. Ideal for general-purpose chat or helpdesk tasks.

  * **High temperature + top_p close to 1 + min_p = 0** → creative, less predictable output. Best for brainstorming, fiction, or poetic writing, but also more likely to go off track.

However, these general rules are based on full-precision models. Once you quantize a model, its internal distribution changes. The same logits aren’t just compressed. They’re shifted in ways that make sampling less stable. You can no longer assume that your optimal settings will behave the same way.

In the rest of this article, we’ll look at how quantized models, especially those using aggressive formats like 2-bit Q2_K, react differently to sampling settings. We’ll see that quantized models are much more sensitive to temperature and top-p, and often require different tuning to stay accurate and stable.

No single combination works best for all prompts. If you're deploying quantized models, you'll want to log your settings and adjust carefully. Try changing temperature in small steps (e.g., by 0.2), or top-p in smaller ones (e.g., by 0.05), while watching for drops in quality.

## Inference Hyperparameters and Quantized Models: A Case Study with Qwen3-8B

I ran five versions of Qwen-3-8B across different sampling configurations with the IFEval benchmark, using vLLM as backend (strict prompt-level accuracy). 

The grid search included 6 `temperature T `(0.0 to 1.0), three `top_p` values (0.8, 0.9, 1.0), and four `min_p` settings (0 to 0.2), making over 300 runs in total. The models I tested were: full-precision, 4-bit AWQ, standard 4-bit and 2-bit GGUF versions, and Unsloth’s 4-bit variant. Since a temperature of zero disables randomness and makes generation fully deterministic, only one output per model was collected at T = 0 during these tests.

Clearly, **each quantization format/precision has a very different set of optimal hyperparameters**.

When working with different quantization levels, the most important thing to get right is temperature. We see that using temperature = 0 leads to a clear drop in performance for the AWQ, bitsandbytes, and original models. The model becomes too deterministic, which hurts accuracy. Even setting the temperature to 0.2 gives a noticeable improvement, and by 0.4, the model usually performs near its peak.

As temperature increases further, behavior starts to diverge more significantly depending on quantization. For base models (full precision), accuracy is mostly stable from 0.4 to 1.0. For the BnB model, which is quantized to 4-bit, the trend is similar. This model is very close to the base model2 in how it reacts to hyperparameters, albeit at a different accuracy.

The 4-bit model in `.gguf` format, Q4_K_M, shows more surprising behavior. It benefits from higher temperatures. Even 0.8 or 1.0 can improve accuracy.

Things change more drastically with the 2-bit model. The Q2_K model is very sensitive, as expected, but performs surprisingly well if you use the right hyperparameters. A low temperature helps, but going above 0.6 quickly hurts. It looks like Q2_K becomes unstable with too much randomness, perhaps due to how aggressively it's been compressed. In any case, if you have the budget and time, try tuning the hyperparameters. The 2-bit model’s performance varies a lot depending on settings, so there’s no one-size-fits-all here.

Top-p sampling behaves more predictably. Surprisingly, most models do well around top_p = 0.8. Increasing it to 0.9 or 1.0 doesn’t give consistent gains, and sometimes reduces performance. This is especially true for quantized models.

As for `min_p`, I found it barely moves the needle for this task, [which is somewhat confirmed by recent work.](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13681>) Across all experiments, varying it had no meaningful effect, except in a few edge cases, where it hurt. The best move might often be to set min_p = 0.0 and not think about it, but remember that for specific tasks, like coding, a very low `min_p` value might help.

## Conclusion

Overall, the original model and lightly quantized models tend to behave similarly, as expected. In these cases, the default inference hyperparameters recommended by the LLM provider generally require little to no adjustment.

However, with aggressively quantized models, the story changes. Hyperparameter tuning becomes important. As quantization becomes more extreme, performance trends shift and the range of effective configurations, 'safe zones', shrinks.

In hindsight, this experiment highlights a common oversight: quantized models are often evaluated improperly. Reusing the same inference hyperparameters from the original full-precision models, a common practice in many research papers and public benchmarks, can lead to misleading results. Quantized models do not necessarily behave like their original counterparts under the same sampling settings. With low-bit models, using suboptimal inference hyperparameters can easily leave a few accuracy points on the table.

Of course, to find the best set of hyperparameter values, we should try many different benchmarks. By using one single benchmark as we did in this article, we have no guarantee that the same set of hyparameter values will also perform the best for a different task.
