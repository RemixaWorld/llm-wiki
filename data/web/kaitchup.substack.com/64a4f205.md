Serving LLMs in production is mostly an infrastructure problem: latency, throughput, and reliability come down to GPU choice, memory, batching, and the serving runtime.

Most teams start with **serverless LLM:** you don’t manage servers, you just call an endpoint and pay per use. It’s great for spiky or low-volume traffic because it’s fast to set up and operationally simple. The downside is less control and often less predictable costs at scale (prompt/output lengths, concurrency, very long cold starts, and platform scheduling can all move the bill).

In contrast, self-hosting means you run the model yourself (on your own GPU or a dedicated rented GPU) and operate the serving stack end-to-end. Even if many platforms already serve most models, self-hosting gives you:

  * **Control:** pin exact model/version, custom model/adapters, manage rollouts, limits, updates.

  * **Clearer data boundaries:** you decide what leaves your system.

  * **Performance + cost ownership:** choose the GPU and weight format (bf16/fp16/fp8/fp4/int4), and tune batching/runtime to hit your speed/quality target at the lowest cost. Different model variants and GPUs behave differently, self-hosting lets you debug and optimize precisely.

In this article, I’ll show**** how to deploy your own LLM on a dedicated GPU using vLLM, an inference engine designed to **maximize throughput** , while **keeping costs under control**. We’ll use RunPod (clear per-GPU pricing and easy reproducibility) to deploy an LLM, and test the endpoint with AnythingLLM. I’ll also share practical guidance for choosing the right GPU based on model size, weight format, and whether your workload is likely compute-bound or memory-bandwidth-bound, so you can optimize for both speed and cost.

**Note:** I’m also working on a separate tutorial for serverless deployment. Maybe not with RunPod as I’m still exploring options.

## Decide Your Maximum Budget First

> #### Quick definition: what’s an “instance”?
> 
> An instance is a rented machine (a VM) with a specific GPU attached. When it’s running, you pay a fixed hourly rate for that GPU (and usually its CPU/RAM/storage). Think of it as “your own box” in the cloud.

#### Why instance pricing is simple

With instance deployment, cost is straightforward: once the instance is up, you pay the same hourly rate until you stop it. That’s usually more cost-efficient than serverless for steady usage, but less flexible because you can’t “pay only when requests arrive.”

The main ways to reduce cost are:

  * **Pause the instance**  
You stop paying for compute and only pay for storage (often a few cents per hour for ~100 GB). The trade-off: when you resume, you may not get the same GPU back immediately.

  * **Reserve for longer**  
Committing for weeks/months can bring significant discounts, at the cost of flexibility.

### Rule of thumb: start from budget, then optimize the stack

I like to start with a max daily budget for a deployed instance, then optimize the model variant + weight format + GPU to hit that number.

Let’s assume:

  * Budget we can commit: $24/day

That’s $1/hour or $720/month if you run it 24/7. That can sound high, but many internal/company deployments don’t need to run nonstop, you can shut it down after hours and on weekends if nothing critical needs to run.

## Choosing the GPU

Answer this question first: what is the size of your model in GB?

Example: Qwen3-8B in FP16/BF16 is roughly:

  * 8.19B params x 2 bytes ≈ 16.38 GB of weights

You also need room for:

  1. KV cache (the big one)

  2. Runtime overhead (allocator fragmentation, temporary buffers, CUDA kernels, etc.)

     * Rule of thumb: ~10% of weight size (≈ 1.6 GB here)

#### Why KV Cache is the Real VRAM Killer

During generation, transformers store Keys and Values for every token you’ve processed so far. This is the KV cache. Given a model, it grows with:

  * **context length** (prompt + generated tokens)

  * **number of concurrent requests**

  * **KV precision** (fp16/bf16 vs fp8)

A practical way to think about it:

> **KV cache memory scales ~linearly with generated tokens.**  
>  Double the context length → roughly double KV VRAM.

For Qwen3 8B (36 layers, 128 head dimension, GQA with 8 KV heads), the KV cache is roughly:

  * **~147 KB per token** (FP16/BF16)

That translates to:

  * **1k tokens** : ~16.38 GB + 0.137 GiB KV → ~**16.5 GB** (+ runtime)

  * **32k tokens** : ~16.38 GB + 4.395 GiB KV → ~**20.8–21.0 GB** (+ runtime)

  * **40,960 tokens** : ~16.38 GB + 5.625 GiB KV → ~**22.0–22.2 GB** (+ runtime)

So, a 24 GB can handle a single sequence of 32k tokens for Qwen3 8B. You will need a 32 GB GPU for something meaningfully longer.

Also remember: the KV-cache numbers above are per sequence. With multiple concurrent users, KV cache scales roughly linearly with the number of active sequences.

In practice, inference engines like vLLM load the model weights first, then reserve most of the remaining VRAM for KV cache. As more requests come in, or as prompts get longer, that KV pool fills up. When it can’t fit additional KV blocks, the server typically starts queueing work (instead of immediately failing), unless you explicitly configure stricter admission control.

Users don’t get errors. They just see responses slow down a lot as requests wait their turn.

A simple mitigation is to cap context at launch time (e.g., `--max-model-len` in vLLM) so a single long prompt can’t monopolize VRAM. The trade-off is that for tasks that genuinely need long context, this can be too restrictive, so you may need a bigger GPU, lower-precision KV, or a serving policy that limits concurrency for long-context requests.

You can also quantize the model weights to free up VRAM for the KV cache (which directly translates into longer context and/or more concurrent users).

For an 8B model:

  * FP16/BF16 weights are ~16 GB (8B params × 2 bytes).

  * 4-bit weights are 4 GB raw (8B × 0.5 bytes), plus some metadata (scales/zeros), so ~4–6 GB in practice depending on the scheme.  
That can easily free ~10–12 GB of VRAM for KV cache—e.g., going from ~16 GB → ~5 GB gives you ~11 GB extra headroom.

#### Hardware compatibility: not all quant formats run everywhere

Different “low precision” formats rely on different GPU features and kernels:

  * FP8 (W8A8 acceleration): Blackwell, Hopper, and Ada Lovelace GPUs.

  * NVFP4 and MXFP4: Blackwell GPUs.

  * INT8 / INT4: well supported on Ampere and newer (and widely used for inference).

#### Speed vs accuracy

Quantization often speeds up inference because smaller weights mean less memory traffic and better throughput. But you still need to validate: run your task evals (or at least golden prompts) and confirm the quantized model doesn’t meaningfully degrade quality for your workload.

## Deploy with One or Multiple GPUs?

It depends on three main factors:

  * **Model size** (parameter count/weight footprint)

  * **Maximum context length** (longer context → larger KV cache)

  * **Expected workload** (e.g., number of concurrent requests)

### Choosing between Data Parallelism (DP) and Tensor Parallelism (TP)

#### 1) If the model fits on a single GPU

You can run one replica per GPU using data parallelism:

  * The model is duplicated on each GPU.

  * Available KV cache memory per GPU is roughly:  
GPU VRAM − model weights − overhead (activations, fragmentation, runtime, etc.)

with vLLM, use:

  * `-dp $NUM_GPUS`

But: even if the model technically fits, it may leave too little free VRAM for the KV cache, especially with long context lengths or higher concurrency. In that case, you’ll be bottlenecked by KV cache capacity and will see more queuing (and latency).

#### 2) If the model does NOT fit on a single GPU (or leaves too little KV cache space)

Use tensor parallelism:

  * Model weights are sharded across GPUs, freeing VRAM per GPU relative to full replication.

  * Often the only viable option for larger models.

with vLLM, use:

  * `-tp $NUM_GPUS`

#### Important constraint: odd GPU counts

vLLM (or the underlying model/architecture assumptions) often refuses odd GPU counts greater than 1. In many cases, models won’t run on 3, 5, or 7 GPUs. They’ll require 2, 4, 6, 8… depending on architecture and tensor shapes. Reason: The total number of attention heads (X) must be divisible by the tensor parallel size (e.g., 3).

This matters for budgeting: you may be forced to pay for 4 GPUs instead of 3, even if 3 would otherwise be sufficient. In practice, there’s usually no clean workaround besides changing GPU count or model/config.

### Why RunPod?

A few (very practical) reasons:

  * Wide choice of GPU models across multiple generations, which makes it easier to adapt and test deployments.

  * Not the cheapest, but generally reasonable pricing and availability.

  * It’s the platform I have the most experience with, so I’m more confident documenting a reliable workflow.

  * Simple deployment from the user interface, including launching from existing Docker images, few platforms make this as smooth.

  * A referral program that doesn’t pay much, but helps a bit.

### RunPod isn’t perfect: caveats I often encounter

The community cloud can be significantly cheaper, but reliability can be an issue:

  * Some GPUs fail immediately (I’ve hit this when testing deployments for this article).

  * More annoyingly, failures can happen mid-run: the machine may restart. You usually don’t lose data, but you may need to resume your processes.

  * Worst case: the machine crashes and becomes unreachable, and you can lose data. This happens too often.

It sounds harsh, but in my experience, running community cloud instances regularly, I see roughly one issue per month.

Recommendation: Avoid the community cloud if you have the budget and need stability.

## Conclusion

VRAM dictates whether you can fit the weights and KV cache. Memory bandwidth dictates how well you handle concurrency. And your runtime (vLLM + max context) decides whether users experience “fast” or “queued.”

From here, you have a few clear levers to pull depending on your workload:

  * **Need more concurrent users?** Reduce max context, use lower-precision KV/weights, move to a higher-bandwidth GPU, or accept that they will experience higher latency.

  * **Need longer context?** Budget VRAM for KV cache first, then choose your model/quantization around that.

  * **Need lower cost?** Pause instances when idle, experiment with community GPUs, and quantify the trade-off in deployment time and reliability.

Most importantly: **measure, don’t guess.** Watch token/sec, GPU utilization, and queueing behavior under your real prompts and concurrency. Small changes, like capping context length or switching weight formats, often have bigger impact than switching models.

That’s it: you now have an endpoint you own, a UI to test it, and a framework to choose hardware. Once you’re comfortable with this workflow, scaling up to larger models is mostly the same process, just with more attention to KV cache, quantization, and GPU bandwidth.
