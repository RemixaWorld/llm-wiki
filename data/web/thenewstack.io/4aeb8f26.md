# IBM’s Mellea Tackles Open Source AI’s Hidden Weakness

![Featued image for: IBM’s Mellea Tackles Open Source AI’s Hidden Weakness](https://cdn.thenewstack.io/media/2025/10/c89a52fb-omar-ramadan-9jb4u2omx0g-unsplash-1-1024x683.jpg)

RALEIGH, N.C. — Nathan Fulton remembers the moment he realized open source AI had an infrastructure problem.

Senior engineers kept asking him the same question: When would IBM‘s AI models support structured outputs? The feature, which enables developers to control how language models format their responses, had just been added to OpenAI‘s API, and suddenly everyone wanted it.

Fulton, a researcher and engineering manager at IBM Research’s MIT-IBM AI Lab in Cambridge, Mass., was baffled, he told The New Stack. The capability had existed in open source libraries since 2021. And IBM’s models had supported it from the beginning.

“You’ve been able to do this for years,” he said. “But it gets added to the API endpoint, and suddenly they think that OpenAI is the only one that can do it.”

That disconnect between what open source AI could do and what people thought it could do was what led to Mellea, a new open source library from IBM Research that is trying to level a playing field most people didn’t realize was off kilter.

Fulton spoke with The New Stack in the IBM Generative Computing lounge at last week’s All Things Open 2025 event here.

## An Unfair Fight

The problem, Fulton realized, was not about AI model quality at all, he said.

When companies evaluate AI platforms, they’re not making an apples-to-apples comparison. They’re comparing OpenAI’s or Anthropic‘s complete, polished platforms — models plus proprietary software infrastructure — against open source models that are essentially just raw weights.

“What they’re doing is they’re hitting our raw model weights — they are shoving tokens in and getting tokens out, and then comparing that to a huge, sophisticated software stack that’s been codesigned together with the models,” Fulton explained

It’s like comparing a car engine sitting on a garage floor to a fully assembled vehicle and concluding the engine doesn’t work as well, he indicated.

“Oftentimes, the open models are, de facto, better,” Fulton said. “But we don’t have the standard software stack around those models.”

The gap became impossible to ignore with reasoning models, which are AI systems that show their work and correct themselves. Each open source implementation was rebuilding the same complex inference algorithms from scratch.

“From the 2024 structured outputs fiasco, I was like, ‘Oh, we need an open source solution to having that inference stack, or open models will never be able to compete with these vertically integrated closed providers.'”

## When Prompts Go Wrong

The other problem Fulton saw was closer to home as he watched how developers actually use AI in production.

He calls it “software anthropology.” The pattern repeats itself constantly. A developer writes a prompt. Adds some tools. It works. Sort of. Then feature requests start rolling in.

“Because the whole application is really just a prompt, some tools and a little bit of software around that, there’s nowhere — if you want to add a feature, the main place that you do that is in the prompt.”

So developers keep appending to the prompt. A bug appears. They update the prompt. The bug persists. They update it again, more emphatically.

“You go back and you say, in all caps, ‘PLEASE DON’T DO THE BUGGY THING,’ right?”

Fulton laughed, but it’s a real problem. “One is that people often put way too much stuff into their prompts.”

The absurdity is that much of what is crammed into these ever-growing prompts could be handled by traditional software. “A lot of what they’re doing in the prompt can be done in good old-fashioned software. They don’t need everything to be done by the LLM [large language model].”

But developers get trapped in prompt-thinking, “outsourcing everything to this statistical model that sometimes works and sometimes fails and fails in unpredictable ways.”

## Breaking Things Down

Mellea’s solution is almost radically simple: Stop doing everything in one giant prompt, Fulton said.

“Basically, do one thing at a time — decompose to the right amount of stuff to happen in each individual step,” he said. After each step, “think very carefully about what should be true after you take that step, and code those as post conditions. And then enforce those post conditions.”

It’s software engineering 101, applied to AI. The framework “nudges developers in the right direction in terms of how they should think about programming with LLMs. Don’t throw everything into the prompt.”

Inside IBM, teams are already seeing results. Multiple groups have reimplemented their AI agents using Mellea and “are having super positive experiences getting really significant performance gains on the benchmarks that they care about,” Fulton said.

The changes are often minimal. “Just taking their existing giant prompts and saying, ‘This prompt has six steps. So what if we do each of those six steps individually? This prompt has 15 requirements listed at the end of it. What if we rip out each of those and check them individually?'”

## The Principal Engineer Problem

The strongest reaction to Mellea has not come from researchers or junior developers. It has come from principal engineers — the folks with visibility across multiple projects in their organizations, Fulton noted.

“The strongest reaction we get is from that late senior to principal engineer person who’s just like, ‘Yeah, we clearly need to move away from the agent framework thing,'” he said.

These engineers have watched their teams try LangChain, DSPy and other frameworks. They’ve seen them fail. They’ve seen teams fall back to writing custom code for each project.

And they’ve noticed something: Every project rewrites the same patterns. Rejection sampling. Requirement checking. Validation loops. Teams “just rewrite that code for every single project, because there isn’t a shared library” for these standard patterns, Fulton said.

That’s where Mellea fits. Not as a revolutionary new approach, but as the shared infrastructure layer that should have existed all along.

## Engineering, Not Science

Fulton is disarmingly honest about what Mellea is and isn’t.

When he presents to the research community, they ask: “What’s new here?”

“It’s a fair reaction,” he says. “There are new things we’re doing. But the core things that I just described to you, they’re not deep research problems.”

He pauses. “For the most part, we’re solving engineering and coordination problems — social and engineering problems, not core science problems.”

It’s not the kind of work that gets published at NeurIPS. But it might be more important than much of what does, Fulton said.

After three months of design work — prototyping high-level abstractions and low-level implementations with a colleague from February through May — Fulton’s team built the actual library in about six weeks. They open sourced it in July 2024.

## Generative Computing

Mellea is part of IBM Research’s broader vision they call “generative computing,” which treats language models not as magical black boxes but as computational elements that need proper software infrastructure.

The project is led by David Cox, vice president of AI models at IBM Research, who argues that computing has moved through distinct phases: imperative computing (explicit instructions), inductive computing (learning from examples) and now generative computing.

“We believe that generative computing demands new programming models for using LLMs, new fundamental low-level operations performed by LLMs, and new ways of building LLMs themselves,” Cox wrote in a blog post.

The philosophy is straightforward: “The full potential of generative AI [GenAI] will be realized by weaving AI together with traditional software in a seamless way.”

For open source AI to compete with platforms like ChatGPT and Claude, it needs more than powerful models. It needs the surrounding infrastructure — the runtime abstractions, the design patterns, the developer tools — that make those models reliable and predictable in production.

## The Real Competition

Mellea might not solve AI’s biggest problems. It won’t make models smarter or more capable. It won’t prevent hallucinations or bias.

But it might solve a problem that’s been overlooked, and that is the infrastructure gap that makes open source models seem worse than they are.

If Fulton is right, companies are not choosing ChatGPT because OpenAI’s models are dramatically better. They’re choosing it because it comes with a complete software stack that makes it easy to use reliably.

Open source AI has been bringing a knife to a gunfight — or more accurately, bringing just an engine to a car race.

Mellea is IBM’s attempt to build the rest of the car. Whether it succeeds may determine whether open source AI remains a hobbyist curiosity or becomes a genuine alternative for the enterprise.

The early signs are promising. Inside IBM, at least, the principal engineers are paying attention.

## Anaconda Study

A recent study by Anaconda, which provides an open source data science and AI distribution platform for the Python and R programming languages, showed that 92% of respondents use open source AI tools and models, with 52% strongly preferring or generally using open source.

Also, nearly 40% pair open source and commercial tools, the Anaconda report showed. More than in previous years, organizations are also following suit. About three out of every four respondents (76%) said there’s either slightly or significantly more priority on open source this year compared to the previous 12 months. Additionally, 78% report their organization strongly supports open source or encourages it when the business case supports its use, the report said.

“Although there is commercial use, we see a lot of hybrid use cases, about 40% are pairing open source with some commercial offerings,” Steve Croce, field CTO at Anaconda, told The New Stack. “Still, securing and being able to use open source in AI is going to be a huge opportunity area for people to differentiate and do their own things.”

Moreover, “If you want to be successful, don’t focus on commercial offerings. Instead, focus on open source,” said Seth Clark, Anaconda’s VP of product, AI. “With nascent areas, open source moves faster because most commercial offerings lag. When innovation is a key part of your company’s strategy, open source is going to play a significant role.”
