## Is Champagne Over?

First of all, _why has everyone been panicking about AI lately?_

For many months, even years, one sole principle has ruled Big Tech's spending decisions and the research direction frontier AI labs took: **scaling laws**.

Heavily influenced by [Rich Sutton's](<http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html>) essay [_The Bitter Lesson_](<http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html>), most AI researchers and executives came to the conclusion that architecture breakthroughs did not matter and that the only things worth investing in were those that unlocked more compute.

In other words, as long as your architecture was easily scalable regarding raw data and compute, something we undeniably achieved with [Transformers](<https://thewhitebox.ai/transformers-the-great-paradigm-shift/>) (the underlying architecture behind models from ChatGPT, to Claude or Sora), you needed no more.

In other words, as long as your models got bigger, they would continue to show dramatic improvements relative to size.

> The bigger, the better.

However, some sources now report this was, surprise surprise, not true.

### Signs of Stagnation

[An article by _The Information_](<https://www.theinformation.com/articles/openai-shifts-strategy-as-rate-of-gpt-ai-improvements-slows?rc=gu2cf8>) now claims to have spoken to researchers at OpenAI, **who stated that the new model's results are underwhelming** , sparking significant discussion in the AI industry.

> This ‘illness’ is also reportedly being suffered by the upcoming [generation of Gemini](<https://the-decoder.com/googles-rumored-gemini-2-0-launch-in-december-could-support-llm-stagnation-thesis/>), meaning that it is common across frontier labs.

According to these sources, following their training, the latest generation of frontier AI models (think of Gemini 2, GPT-5, Claude 4 type of models) has delivered less improvement than anticipated over the current leading models like GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet.

In a nutshell, **these frontier AI models may be reaching a plateau**. For instance, while they reportedly perform better in language tasks, **they are “not reliably better” in other critical areas, such as coding — again, allegedly**.

Other insiders are also openly expressing concerns that pretraining is indeed showing diminishing returns, [including billionaire venture capitalists from a16z](<https://www.reddit.com/r/singularity/comments/1gkkrel/marc_andreessen_and_ben_horowitz_say_that_ai/>), **who have invested in companies like OpenAI and Mistral** , [and even Ilya Sutskever](<https://www.reuters.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/openai-rivals-seek-new-path-smarter-ai-current-methods-hit-limitations-2024-11-11/>), co-founder of OpenAI and one of the leading research figures behind ChatGPT (although the latter's opinion might have been taken a little bit out of context).

_But is all hope lost?_ It seems not _._

### A New Paradigm

When OpenAI decided that their new models were no longer ‘GPT-x’ but ‘oX,’ like the new o1 models, they were sending a message:

> This is a new model and we are entering a new paradigm.

In fact, [these models have been defined as Large Reasoner Models (LRMs)](<https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13373>) instead of LLMs.

Without going into excessive detail [as I already did](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/pro-review-of-openais-new-o1-models-03fd56971b1d>), the main difference is that o1 models move the balance of compute **from pretraining** (the phase where models are trained, the one that is allegedly stagnating) **to inference** , the phase where the model runs.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:698/0*2jm1E57YCgj4KuNQ.png)

In other words, they are models that think the responses for longer, a shallow but nonetheless clear analogy to human System 2 thinking, when we consciously engage in solving a complex task.

> Architecture-wise, LRMs are still LLMs, but wrapped in an algorithm that allows them to think for arbitrarily longer on any given task by generating multiple samples, exploring different solution paths, and backtracking when necessary, just like a human would engage a complex math problem, for instance.
>
> Here, the LLM plays the role of ‘human intuition,’ providing possible resolution paths to explore.

The comparison is best seen above, where we can appreciate the compute balance favoring inference with o1 (previously known as ‘Strawberry’). Therefore, if pre-training is really showing diminishing returns, frontier AI labs have two options:

1. Achieve/improve new post-training breakthroughs (like o1’s think-for-longer algorithm).
2. Scrap the status quo altogether and embrace new research to discover better architectures.

To be clear, option number two would be devastating for the valuations of frontier AI labs, in some cases already above the [$150 billion mark like OpenAI](<https://www.reuters.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/openai-closes-66-billion-funding-haul-valuation-157-billion-with-investment-2024-10-02/>), but also for Big Tech companies, the organizations paying the bills for these labs which have enjoyed massive stockmarket success in this venture despite showing almost [zero revenues from these investments](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/ai-bubble>), and which have been allowed to spend billions every quarter despite the nonexistent returns.

Obviously, that means all cognitive effort goes into option number one. And now, thanks to MIT, labs may now have another weapon in their arsenal.

## Test-Time Training

One of the main battlehorses of frontier AI skeptics is that these models have clearly detached learning from inference. In other words, they have a constrained, limited-in-time learning phase, and from then on,**that model remains unchanged and incapable of learning as consumers are using it**.

This is obviously sub-optimal as humans continuously make inferences with our brains and learn from those inferences, a term known as active learning or active inference (although the latter is usually reserved for a [Fristonian view of AI](<https://www.su.org/resources/how-free-energy-shapes-the-future-of-ai>) that has nothing to do with Deep Learning).

_But what if there was a way to do that with AI?_

### Test-time Training models

> The idea is pretty simple: **engage the model in lifelong learning**. Instead of having a constrained learning window, the model continuously updates its parameters to adapt to new tasks or data. Forever.

However, despite the hype around MIT’s research based on the amazing results we will comment on, adapting the model during inference isn’t new.

Back in August, [I wrote a Medium article on Test-Time Training Layers](</towards-artificial-intelligence/an-ai-has-come-back-from-the-dead-ec90592022d8>), which adapted the model's weights for every new inference if they found that the input was new (and, thus, worth learning).

However, MIT’s research takes a different approach.

While the research in the previous link required models to be Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to make the model update fast and cheap, **the MIT team maintains the use of Transformer models** , an essential requirement for adoption from AI labs these days.

Instead, they keep the essence of current models untouched but add [QLoRA adapters](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14314>) that train a small subset of the network for every new task while keeping the original model untouched.

I know this is a tough pill to swallow, so let’s simplify things.

When we refer to ‘model learning,’ we mean updating the model’s parameters (variables) based on the signal provided by the data. However, we are talking about updating the values of billions or even trillions of these variables, which takes time and isn’t cheap.

For that reason, under the banner of ‘parameter efficient fine-tuning’ (PEFT), several methods aim to update only a subset of parameters in any given data.

> This works because most **LLM/LRM tasks are intrinsically low-rank** , meaning that, to make a particular prediction, only a very small subset of the model’s parameters ‘matter.’ For that reason, when retraining a model to a new task, PEFT methods leverage this to make fine-tuning (retraining) fast and cheap.

One of these methods stands out: [LoRA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09685>). This method allows the training of ‘adapters,’ small packs of external weights that are ‘strapped on’ to the model and dynamically alter its behavior (as long as the adapters are connected to the model).

![Adapters depicted in orange. Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:356/1*5ZV09waSAIZ7oeDtI8RmCA.png)

These offer three great advantages:

1. Fine-tuning is extremely**fast** and **effective**.
2. **The base model remains frozen during training** , not only cheapening costs but also preventing the model from being negatively affected by very task-specific data, preventing the loss of generalization capabilities.
3. **They are dynamically added based on the task**. In other words, you can train one adapter per task and dynamically load the adapter into the model whenever we face that task. Interestingly, this is the precise methodology Apple has used with Apple Intelligence ([which I covered in detail here](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/how-does-apple-intelligence-really-work-5f79b368c86d>)) to have a small model fit in the iPhone, alongside dozens of adapters for each particular task. It’s like having 20 models for the size of one.

So,_how are MIT researchers using this to save frontier AI models?_

### Inference-time training with QLoRA

Now, getting into the weeds of the research, they have solely focused on improving AI’s performance on ARC.

> ARC is an IQ-test-type benchmark that aims to prove whether AI models can acquire new skills on the fly. This stems from the idea that most AI models today work only if they have previous experience solving a task.

![ARC examples. Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Sc9hP4eudG0qp98zKonvgQ.png)

As the main feature that makes it different to other benchmarks, ARC tasks are memorization-resistant and novel, testing whether AI can handle tasks it has never seen before.

Also, these tasks are designed so humans can perform reasonably well on them, further proving the dismaying gap between human and AI intelligence. Unsurprisingly, **AIs have been historically terrible at this benchmark**.

However, MIT has developed this new method that uses a Llama 3.1 8B model (a fairly small model by today’s standards), to reach 61.9%, **matching the average human score** , a completely unprecedented result.

Interestingly, it does not leverage symbolic engines (human-written code) to tackle the most complex reasoning parts, as previous examples like [Greenblatt et al](<https://redwoodresearch.substack.com/p/getting-50-sota-on-arc-agi-with-gpt>). or [Google’s AlphaGeometry models](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/google-microsoft-disappoints>) did to match human performance.

In this case, **the entire process is fully neural** , meaning the reasoning—or whatever you want to call it—occurs inside the model.

Although we are covering each step, the overarching idea is that, for every new task, the model generates a set of alternative examples in real time via different geometric transformations, creating a fine-tuning dataset on the fly, trains an adapter on these, performs the prediction, and offloads the adapter leaving the original model unchanged.

Let’s cover each one:

* **Dataset creation**

Probably the trickiest part of the process is generating a set of examples and a test attempt so that the model has data to learn about the task before making the prediction.

Looking at the image below, using the actual test task example pairs (left), we create the new test-time training data (right) by making permutations of the example pairs and applying geometric transformations that preserve the pattern to be uncovered while allowing the model to gain a new perspective of the same task, a common technique known as data augmentation.

> I recommend taking a few seconds on the picture to see the different transformations (horizontal/vertical flips, identity, etc.) to see how they create a new training sample.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*8UmCvrWl27P2FTT_Jcxn7Q.png)

* **Fine-tuning**

Using the previous method, we build a test-time dataset using different transformations. We then use a QLoRA adapter (the same as a standard LoRA adapter, but quantizing—reducing the precision of—the model weights so that the adapter is more memory efficient) and fine-tuning it on the data.

This way, the model is actively trained upon seeing a new task with different variations of the task, allowing it to learn how to solve it on the fly.

* **Prediction**

With the adapter fully trained and loaded into the LLM, we sample a prediction from the model, which is then evaluated to see whether the model has learned the new pattern.

* **Adapter offloading**

Once the prediction is done, the adapter is offloaded. Adapters are trained on a task-per-task basis, as researchers tried one single adapter for the entire ARC benchmark only to discover that it underperformed task-level adapters.

This means the model faces the upcoming new task in its original state, preventing it from losing accuracy due to excessive per-task fine-tuning.

> However, it must be said that this ‘original state’ is not the original Llama 3.1 8B model but a fine-tuned version for the ARC challenge; the adapter phase we are describing here today is just another per-task fine-tuning on top on the first one.

Overall, the results are very impressive, especially when compared to models considered state-of-the-art:

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*If0aDTuM3mGbESO6GXUD_w.png)

> [BARC](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.02272>) is a very recent program-synthesis fine-tuning method that creates a new dataset comprising of examples of Python code that define the functions that solve a particular ARC problem. To be clear, BARC isn’t a test-time fine-tuning method, but one that is done as post-training over the model. Instead, TTT, the method we are covering today,**refers to training that occurs during the model’s predictions**.
>
> As shown in the table, fine-tuning the original Llama 3.1 8B model with BARC and then using the TTT method explained in this article yields the best outcome.

At the model level, TTT increases performance considerably, not only for Llama 3.1 8B but also for Llama 3.2 1B and 3B, which were also evaluated:

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:654/1*L70zMutefK0XTY5CQWIggA.png)

And if you think frontier AI labs aren’t paying attention, think again, as Noam Brown, one of the brains behind OpenAI’s o1 models, showed excitement about this research.

> On a last note, [researchers also tried different inference-time strategies worth reading](<https://ekinakyurek.github.io/papers/ttt.pdf>). Instead of keeping the first response the model gave, they sampled the model several times for each task to see whether that improved the overall result (which it did).

## And Now… What?

I always found the idea that ‘scaling is all your need’ disingenuous… almost arrogant… as if humans think we know best and that AI is solved.

> Of course, as the late Charlie Munger once said, “Show me the incentives, and I’ll show you the outcome,” meaning that the reason this view was so self-serving was that it was a lazy yet great way of continuing to raise enormous amounts of capital from investors with too much money and that know no best.

Nonetheless, for the longest time, public and private markets appeared utterly unfazed by the obvious limitations of the current frontier. And even when they were aware, **they assumed that an imperfect model would become perfect simply by expanding it**.

Sounds crazy, but this was literally the sentiment not so long ago ([and still is](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ugvHCXCOmm4>), although the claims are less categorical these days).

However, **while the AI hype is clearly begging for a healthy correction** , we must also celebrate how some researchers are challenging the idea that no breakthroughs are required and proposing novel ways to fight stagnant progress.

Don’t get me wrong; I really hope TTT is the breakthrough that continues the AI hype train. Active learning is unequivocally something anyone, even the greatest of haters of LLMs, will agree is a step in the right direction.

> But this time, let’s stop pretending the current AIs are more intelligent than they really are. Please.
