## The Story of Evolution of Ranking

Understanding the history of ranking can be incredibly valuable. By looking at how these systems have developed over time, we can better appreciate the solutions we have today and how they’ve been designed to tackle real-world problems. Of course, if you’re eager to dive straight into the latest reranking methods, especially in the age of Transformers (after BERT), feel free to skip ahead. But if you’re curious about the journey that got us here, stick around as we’re about to explore how ranking systems have evolved over the years.

## Early Days of Ranking:

In the early days of search engines, things were pretty straightforward but limited. The technology back then, like BM25, was heavily focused on **exact term matching**. This meant that if the exact words in your search query didn’t appear in a document, that document wouldn’t be considered relevant, even if it was exactly what you were looking for.

At the heart of these early systems was a formula called **Term Frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF)** , which determined how relevant a document was based on three key factors:

1. **Term frequency** — How often does the search term appear in the document?
2. **Document frequency** — How many other documents contain the same term?
3. **Document length** — How long is the document?

Architecture of Lexical Search- Image by Author

To normalize document length and compare the relevance of documents to your search, a technique called **cosine similarity** was used within a **vector space model**. While this approach helped lay the groundwork for early search engines, it had a big drawback, it couldn’t understand the meaning behind words or detect synonyms. Everything had to match exactly.

For instance, if you searched for a “tragic love story,” a document with the phrase “star-crossed lovers” (like in _Romeo and Juliet_) might not show up, even though it’s exactly what you’re looking for. This issue, known as the **vocabulary mismatch problem** , was one of the biggest challenges for these early search systems. As language and terminology evolve, different terms might refer to the same idea, but exact match systems couldn’t bridge that gap.

To overcome this **vocabulary mismatch problem** , a few clever techniques emerged:

1. **Query Expansion** : This method broadens your search by adding related terms, either through user feedback or by assuming the top-ranked documents are relevant. It also uses tools like WordNet to find synonyms.
2. **Document Enrichment** : This improves how documents are represented, especially helpful when dealing with noisy data like speech transcriptions or short text snippets.
3. **Beyond Exact Matching** : Early semantic approaches like **latent semantic analysis (LSA)** and **statistical translation** tried to capture meaning beyond exact word matches. However, the real breakthrough came with **neural networks** , which revolutionized search by enabling **semantic matching,** the ability to understand the meaning behind words, even if they aren’t an exact match.

This shift from **exact term matching** to **semantic matching** marked a turning point in search technology. Today’s search engines combine both approaches to give us smarter, more accurate results, delivering relevant content even when the wording doesn’t match perfectly.

## The Rise of Learning to Rank:

Before the age of neural networks, search engines like BM25 and similar methods used unsupervised approaches to rank documents based on factors like term frequency. These systems could be fine-tuned with data, but they lacked a more refined, learning-based approach. This new method, known as **Learning to Rank (LTR)** , depended on features engineered from the text itself, such as term frequencies, document lengths, and BM25 scores. For instance, ranking systems could now consider how often specific terms co-occurred within a certain distance in a document, or how often they appeared in key areas like titles or anchor text. These insights allowed for more accurate, relevant search results.

LTR models are typically categorized based on how they handle ranking tasks:

* **Pointwise** : This approach looks at individual documents, treating ranking as either a classification or regression task. It’s like asking, “Is this document relevant or not?”
* **Pairwise** : This method focuses on comparing pairs of documents, aiming to optimize for preferences .For example, ensuring that Document A ranks higher than Document B when both are relevant.
* **Listwise** : Instead of focusing on individual documents or pairs, this approach evaluates entire lists of search results and directly optimizes ranking metrics, like **normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG)** , which measures the usefulness of the ranked results to the user.

By the early 2010s, **Learning to Rank** was at its peak. Tree-based models, particularly **gradient-boosted decision trees** , became the go-to solution for optimizing search rankings. In fact, these models were crucial in winning competitions like the Netflix Prize, which challenged teams to improve recommendations for movies.

LTR techniques paved the way for today’s advanced search systems. However, the arrival of deep learning has taken things to the next level, offering even more sophisticated ways to rank search results with greater accuracy and a deeper understanding of user intent. The evolution of search is far from over, but LTR represents a significant milestone in the journey.

## The Advent of Deep Learning: A New Era in Search Ranking

With the rise of deep learning, search ranking took a major leap forward. Two key breakthroughs defined this shift. First, **continuous vector representations** allowed models to go beyond simply matching exact terms, and understanding the deeper relationships between words. Second, **deep learning** reduced the need for manually crafted features, which had been a significant challenge in earlier ranking systems like Learning to Rank (LTR).

When discussing deep learning in text ranking, it’s helpful to think of two distinct phases: **pre-BERT models** and **BERT-based models** (or Transformer-based models). The introduction of BERT in 2019 was a game-changer, as BERT-based models delivered far superior performance compared to earlier models. This distinction essentially marks two eras in deep learning for search ranking.

## Pre-BERT Neural Ranking Models

Before BERT, there were two primary types of models used for neural ranking: **representation-based** and **interaction-based** models.

1. **Representation-based models** :

These models learned dense vector representations of queries and documents separately and compared them using metrics like cosine similarity. One early example is the **Deep Structured Semantic Model (DSSM)[5]** , which uses character n-grams to create vector representations. Later, variations like **CNN-based DSSM[6]** and **Dual Embedding Space Model (DESM)[7]** improved upon this by adding context and pre-trained word embeddings.

**2\. Interaction-based models** :

These models focused on capturing relationships between specific terms in a query and a document by using **similarity matrices**. The matrix entries reflected how similar the embeddings of a query term were to those in the document. This method helped address the **vocabulary mismatch problem** by using continuous vector representations rather than relying on exact word matches.

These models typically follow two steps:

* **Feature extraction** : Here, the model analyzes the similarity matrix and aggregates term similarities using different techniques. For example, models like **DRMM** and **KNRM** created histograms of these similarities, while more advanced models like **MatchPyramid** , **PACRR** , and **ConvKNRM** included positional information to capture patterns between sequences of words.
* **Relevance scoring** : After extracting features, the model combines them to produce a **relevance score** for the query-document pair using techniques like pooling and feedforward neural networks.

## Reranking Methods in the Age of Transformers (Post-BERT Era)

With the rise of Transformer models like BERT, ranking in search systems has become more sophisticated, giving us more accurate and relevant results. But let’s try to understand how exactly are these ranking methods incorporated into search and information retrieval (IR) systems.

### How Are Ranking Solutions Integrated into IR Systems?

Modern information retrieval systems often follow a two-stage process to balance speed and accuracy. Here’s how it works:

1. **Initial Retrieval** : First, a lightweight, efficient method, such as BM25 or Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), quickly retrieves a small set of candidate documents. These methods don’t require intensive computations and can process large amounts of data quickly. However, their accuracy is limited, as they don’t have a deep understanding of both the document and the query.
2. **Reranking** : Once the initial documents are selected, a more powerful neural network-based model is used to **rerank** these candidates. These reranking models, often based on Transformers like BERT, are much more accurate because they analyze the relationship between the document and the query in greater depth. However, because they’re computationally expensive, using them on large datasets from the start would take too long. So, they’re applied only to the smaller set of documents retrieved in the first stage.

## **1\. Relevance Classification based ranking**

## 1.1 Simple Relevance Classification with monoBERT (Cross encoder)

One of the simplest ways to approach text ranking is to frame it as a **text classification problem**. The idea is to classify each text as either “relevant” or “not relevant” to a query, then sort the results based on how likely each text belongs to the “relevant” category. Essentially, you’re training a model to estimate the probability that a given text is relevant to a user’s query and then ranking the texts by these probabilities.

For a given query **q** and a candidate text **d** , the model computes a score **s_i** representing the relevance, denoted as:

But where do these candidate texts come from? Given the size of typical text corpora, applying this process to every document in the database would be computationally impossible. Imagine running neural network inference on millions of documents for each user query, it would be way too slow and expensive.

Most search systems, including those using BERT, follow a **retrieve-and-rerank approach**. This means that instead of running BERT on all the documents in the corpus, the system first retrieves a smaller set of candidate texts using faster, more traditional methods like **BM25** (which relies on keyword matching). This initial stage is called **candidate generation** or **first-stage retrieval**.

Once the candidates are retrieved, **BERT** steps in to rerank them by calculating a relevance score for each one. This reranking step adds deeper context and understanding to the search, improving the quality of the results compared to the initial keyword-based scoring.

### **How monoBERT Works?**

The **monoBERT** model is designed to perform relevance classification by using BERT to analyze both the query and each candidate text in a structured way.

For each query **q** and a candidate document **d** , the input sequence is structured as follows:

Press enter or click to view image in full size

* **CLS** : A special token used by BERT to represent the entire input.
* **q** : The user’s query, tokenized.
* **SEP** : A special token used to separate segments.
* **d_i** : Tokens from the candidate text.

This structured input is called the **input template** and is a crucial part of how BERT processes the text. BERT then generates **contextual vector representations** for each token in this sequence.

monoBERT focuses on the **CLS token** ’s representation, which captures the overall relationship between the query and the candidate text. This representation is passed through a single-layer, fully-connected neural network to generate a relevance score **s_i** for the candidate document.

Interestingly, monoBERT only uses the representation of the **CLS token** to compute the relevance score, ignoring the other tokens’ representations. This keeps the model both simple and effective.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The monoBERT ranking model adapts BERT for relevance classification by taking as input the query and a candidate text to be scored (surrounded by appropriate special tokens).[[source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.06467>)]

MonoBERT takes a sequence of text as input. This sequence includes special markers and the content that needs to be compared. Specifically, it looks like this:

``` [CLS], query, [SEP], document, [SEP] ```

**_CLS_** and **_SEP_** are special markers used by BERT to identify where different parts of the input begin and end. The _query_ is the text the user enters, while the _document_ is the candidate text being evaluated for relevance.

For example, if a user searches for “ _best hiking trails_ ” the query would be the search text, and the document would be one of the results (like a description of a trail).

The query tokens are taken exactly as the user entered them. This detail becomes important when we explore different ways to express the search, like whether a search is a simple title or a longer description. The query gets labeled as _Segment A_ , and the document as _Segment B_ , helping the model understand the roles of each.

Once this input sequence is ready, it’s passed to BERT, which processes the entire sequence and generates a “contextual representation” for each token, or word, in the sequence. BERT’s job is to capture the meaning of the words based on the context they appear in.

In monoBERT, [CLS] acts as the model’s summary of the entire sequence. The final step is feeding this [CLS] token into a simple neural network to predict how relevant the document is to the query. The output is a score that tells us the likelihood of that document being a good match.

This process, where both the query and document are passed into BERT for comparison, is known as a **_cross-encoder_** approach. While monoBERT uses the [CLS] token for relevance scoring, it ignores the other token representations.

One key limitation of monoBERT is that it struggles with **longer input texts**. BERT, and by extension monoBERT, has a fixed input length, meaning it’s best suited for shorter passages. So while monoBERT can efficiently rank paragraph-length texts, it struggles with longer documents like full news articles. It can handle only sequences of up to **512 tokens**. Since it relies on position embeddings to understand the order of tokens, any input longer than 512 tokens loses this structure and is treated as a random collection of words, causing the model to lose its sense of how the text flows.

**Why length limitation is a problem with monoBERT?**

The **length limitation** of BERT presents two big challenges when it comes to ranking longer texts:

**1\. Training:** The big question while training is, what part of the document should we feed into the model? Imagine a document with a few relevant sentences scattered throughout or one where the whole document feels relevant as a complete package. How do we train monoBERT to handle these different cases? If we want to model different levels of relevance (like “relevant” vs. “highly relevant”), it gets even more tricky.

Then comes the problem of BERT’s length limit: since queries are usually short and documents are long, we have to **truncate the document** to fit the model. But what if we cut off the most important part? While we could use tricks like feeding BERT only the sections of the document that contain query terms, this still leaves us guessing whether the training data we’re feeding the model is actually the most relevant part of the document.

**2\. Inference:** During **inference** , when we’re ranking documents based on a search query, we face the same length limit. If a document is too long, we can’t simply feed it all into BERT. We have to **split the document into chunks** and decide how to process them. For instance, should we break it into **fixed-sized segments** or use **natural divisions** like sentences? How much should these chunks overlap?

Once we have chunks, we still need a way to **combine the relevance scores** from different parts of the document. There are two common methods:

* **Score aggregation** : We get a relevance score for each chunk and combine them (for example, by taking the highest score).
* **Representation aggregation** : We combine the representations of each passage first, and then predict a single relevance score.

## 1.2 From Passage to Document Ranking

### BERT–MaxP: A Solution for Passage Score Aggregation

To deal with BERT’s length limits, a solution was proposed by _Dai and Callan [2019][2]_ which is a clever workaround. Here’s the idea in a nutshell:

* **Training** : Don’t stress too much! Split documents into **overlapping passages** and treat every passage from a relevant document as relevant, and every passage from a non-relevant document as not relevant.
* **Inference** : Use the same approach as above, split the document into chunks, evaluate the relevance of each, and then combine the scores.

In more detail, documents are divided into **150-word segments** with a **75-word overlap** between each segment. For each segment (or passage), the model processes it just like monoBERT: the query and passage are combined and fed into BERT, with the final [CLS] token being used to score the passage. After scoring each passage, we can combine the scores in three ways:

1. **BERT–MaxP** : Take the highest score from any passage.
2. **BERT–FirstP** : Use the score from the first passage.
3. **BERT–SumP** : Sum the scores from all the passages.

By splitting documents into manageable chunks and then aggregating the results, we can rank entire documents while staying within BERT’s input length limits.

## 1.3 Leveraging Contextual Embeddings: CEDR

In earlier models like monoBERT, Birch, and BERT-MaxP, only the **[CLS]** **token** is used as a special marker used by BERT to summarize the input. It is used to compute how well a document matches a query. However, BERT generates **_contextual embeddings_** for all the words in the query and document. Ignoring these embeddings seems like a missed opportunity, right? That’s exactly what _MacAvaney et al. [2019][3_] thought when they developed CEDR (Contextualized Embeddings for Document Ranking).

CEDR builds on earlier work but takes it a step further by retaining and using the contextual embeddings BERT generates, rather than discarding them. The idea is that these embeddings can provide more nuanced signals about the relevance of different parts of the document to the query.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Architecture of CEDR, which comprises two main sources of relevance signals: the [CLS] representation and the similarity matrix computed from the contextual embeddings of the query and the candidate text

### **Here’s how CEDR works:**

To handle documents that are longer than BERT’s 512-token limit, CEDR splits the document into smaller, manageable chunks. These chunks, which include the query and special delimiter tokens, are processed one by one by BERT. After processing all the chunks, CEDR takes the **[CLS] representations** from each chunk and averages them to create a document-level [CLS] representation (a technique called **_average pooling_**).

But CEDR doesn’t stop there. Instead of just using the [CLS] token, CEDR also keeps the **_contextual embeddings_** from each chunk. These are then concatenated to form a complete sequence of contextual embeddings for the entire document.

Once the document-level embeddings are created, CEDR constructs **_similarity matrices_** by comparing the embeddings of each document term with the embeddings of each query term. These matrices capture how well different parts of the document match different parts of the query, providing a much deeper understanding of relevance than just using the [CLS] token alone.

By incorporating BERT’s powerful embeddings into existing neural ranking models, CEDR can process longer documents chunk by chunk, while still keeping the model end-to-end trainable. This not only solves BERT’s length limitation but also allows CEDR to fully utilize the rich contextual information BERT provides, giving it an edge in document ranking tasks.

## **2\. Refining Query and Document Representations**

One of the biggest challenges in information retrieval is the __**_vocabulary mismatch problem_** , where searchers and the authors of documents use different words to describe the same concept. For example, someone might search for “ _car sales_ ” while the document talks about “ _automobile sales_.” This mismatch in terminology has been a core problem in search technologies for decades.

Traditional ranking models that depend on exact matches such as BM25 struggle with this issue. If a relevant document doesn’t contain the exact words from the query, it won’t be retrieved, no matter how relevant it might be. Neural ranking models, especially those using continuous vector representations, offer a potential solution by learning “soft” matches. Instead of depending on exact word matches, they can recognize _semantic relationships_ between words like knowing that “ _car_ ” and “ _automobile_ ” are related.

However, there’s still a major bottleneck in these models: the _initial candidate generation_ stage. Most ranking systems use a multi-stage process, where the first stage retrieves candidate documents (usually with an exact match system like BM25), and the second stage reranks them with a more powerful model, like BERT. But if the first stage fails to retrieve a relevant document due to a lack of matching terms, no matter how good the re-ranker is, it can’t retrieve something that didn’t surface in the first place.

While it’s uncommon for a relevant document to have no overlap with the query, it’s common for it to miss a key term. A brute-force solution to this problem is to simply increase the number of candidate documents retrieved in the first stage, hoping that relevant documents will eventually show up in the deeper results. But this solution has its own drawback, increased latency. Reranking becomes slower as more candidates need to be processed.

The real solution is _to refine or augment_ the way queries and documents are represented, so they’re more closely aligned with the user’s search intent. This can be achieved through techniques like _query expansion_ and _document expansion_.

\- **_Document expansion_** works by adding extra terms to documents that represent their content more fully or relate them to potential queries. For example, a document about “automobile sales” might be expanded with the term “car” to better match queries like “car sales per year.”

\- **_Query expansion_** does the reverse. It adds synonyms or related terms to the query itself. For instance, expanding the query “car sales per year in the US” with the term “automobile” increases the chances of finding relevant documents that might use different words.

Both methods help address the vocabulary mismatch problem by increasing the likelihood of a match between the query and relevant documents, even if they don’t share the same exact wording.

### 2.1 Document Expansion with Query Prediction: doc2query

Document Expansion with Query Prediction offers a different approach compared to query expansion. Document expansion is not new, it’s been around for decades, but a modern twist on this idea, using neural networks, came about with the introduction of **_doc2query_** by _Nogueira et al. in 2019[4]_.

**How doc2query works?**
Using a sequence-to-sequence model (a type of neural network designed for tasks like language translation), doc2query takes a piece of text and generates queries for which the document could be relevant. These queries aren’t just wild guesses, they’re based on real-world data, where pairs of queries and relevant documents (also called relevance judgments) are used to train the model.

Once the model is trained, it can predict these queries for every document in the corpus. In the next step, these predicted queries are added to the original document text. These “expanded documents” now contain not only their original content but also potential search queries, which boosts their chances of being retrieved in the initial search stage.

When these expanded documents are indexed and searched, the system becomes much better at retrieving relevant information. This technique can be seamlessly integrated into the first-stage retrieval of any multi-stage ranking pipeline and works perfectly with the reranking models we’ve discussed earlier.

Then, indexing is as easy as instantiating the Doc2Query object and a PyTerrier indexer:

## **3\. Learned Dense Representations for Ranking**

One of the biggest breakthroughs brought by modern deep learning to text ranking is the shift from relying on **sparse signals,** which mostly depend on exact keyword matches, to **continuous dense representations** that can capture the **meaning** behind the words. This leap allows us to model relevance more naturally and flexibly, addressing challenges like the vocabulary mismatch problem.

In **dense retrieval techniques** ranking is performed directly on vector representations (usually generated by transformers). Instead of only matching words exactly as they appear in both the query and the text, dense retrieval works by comparing the **semantic content,** essentially, the “meaning” embedded in those vectors. This represents a major shift from keyword-based retrieval, which still leans on the old method of exact matches, even with improvements like document and query expansion.

Dense representations have shown their potential since the early days of word embeddings, which sparked what we now call the **“neural revolution”** in natural language processing (NLP). Word embeddings demonstrated that machines could understand relationships between words (like how “king” relates to “queen”), proving that language could be represented in a way that captures meaning, not just surface-level matches.

However, things get more complex when we extend this idea from single words to larger chunks of text like phrases, sentences, or entire documents. The same challenges that arise in **text ranking** come into play.

Dense retrieval encompasses various techniques and approaches. To keep this blog concise, I’ll conclude here, but I plan to write a separate post dedicated to dense representations for ranking.
