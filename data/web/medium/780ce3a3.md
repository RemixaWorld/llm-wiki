> Searching the scientific literature is an act of empowerment.

Today, scientific research has never been more accessible. Whether you’re a researcher chasing answers for your work or a curious reader looking to understand a health condition, test a new habit, or verify the latest claims about a supplement or diet, the power to find credible, science-backed information is at your fingertips. The only question is: _will you use it?_

But here’s the challenge: _there’s simply too much information._

We’re drowning in data. The sheer volume of studies, articles, and sources can be overwhelming. It’s no longer just about finding information — it’s about filtering, organizing, and making sense of it.

Classical research methods just don’t cut it anymore. That’s where digital tools come in.

We need to use everything at our disposal. So let me show you what a modern, fully digital research workflow looks like — and how it can help you streamline your process of doing research.

### Finding the knowledge

Once you have defined your research question, you will want to find the relevant literature to answer it. While traditional tools like Google Scholar and PubMed still remain relevant for precise searches, _AI-enhanced search engines_ can now speed up your process by summarizing findings and citing sources in real time.

[**Perplexity**](<https://www.perplexity.ai/>), for example, has become my go-to tool for exploring new research topics. Ask Perplexity a question, and it will scan the internet for sources (which you can filter to focus only on academic papers). It will then proceed to summarize all of the information and provide not only a thorough answer, but one that is properly backed up by citations.

![The speed of Perplexity searches makes it seamless to perform quick literature reviews on a specific topic. You can think of Perplexity as your know-it-all buddy, except this one actually gives you citations for everything. The only downside? You can’t grab a beer with it.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*dibhbXUIf8s1TW6s-xEwtg.gif)

Perplexity can help you:

* Get quick summaries of complex topics.
* Identify key literature with the citations it provides.
* Find specific answers to very niche questions.
* Retrieve useful code snippets.
* Ask mundane, quick questions (_does coffee break a fast?_ ; _how to properly pet a cat?_)
* Use it as a clutter-free alternative to “googling” stuff.

But what if you have a specific yes-or-no question? Is there a literature consensus on the matter? [**Consensus**](<https://consensus.app/>) will aim to provide you just that. Consensus queries indexed papers to show you their stance on your particular question, which is summarized via the “Consensus Meter”. The search can be restricted to articles published in Q1 journals, or having a minimum of citations. Also, like Perplexity, it generates a properly cited summary.

![The “Consensus Meter” is Consensus’ main feature. You can grasp, at a glance, what is the position of the most relevant papers on a certain topic. Should you start meditating? Your call.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*hPAXDdFjukzyERw0YSEzfg.png)

There are more AI-powered search engines, like [Google Gemini](<https://gemini.google.com/app>) or [Microsoft Copilot](<https://copilot.microsoft.com>). And you can also use [ChatGPT](<https://chatgpt.com/>) with the internet functionality. But I’ve found these two (again, Perplexity and Consensus) provide the perfect balance between ease of use, quickness of the response and quality of the output in our particular use case.

None of these tools, however, are substitutes of human-curated reviews, and you should _always_ check the sources they are using to back up their statements. But there is no doubt they make your life much easier when it comes to _opening new research topics_ and _finding foundational papers_. As any tool, use them mindfully.

### Storing (and organizing) the knowledge

You have identified your research papers of interest, but printing them and taking physical notes is just inconvenient, especially when you are working with lots of them. _Where did I put X paper? I can’t recall Y note I took…_

Digital library organizers like [**Zotero**](<https://www.zotero.org/>) (or [Mendeley](<https://www.mendeley.com/>)) help a lot to efficiently manage large amounts of papers.

1. First, they allow you to save papers directly from PubMed or a journal site to your library by just using a browser extension (the [**Zotero Connector**](<https://www.zotero.org/download/>) in the case of, well, Zotero).
2. Then, you can organize your papers inside your library thematically, by leveraging the use of collections and tags.
3. Finally, you can use the in-app viewer to do the actual reading and take notes. The notes will be synchronized across all your devices and you can search for them by keywords.

![Overview of the Zotero workflow. Save papers to your library with the Zotero Connector. Organize them in collections. Read them and take notes.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1000/1*0KRgzsl-QVvImXGBc6Imig.png)

Zotero is my particular recommendation. It is open source and benefits from community-developed extensions, such as [**Zotero Reading List**](<https://github.com/Dominic-DallOsto/zotero-reading-list>), which allows tagging your papers as “To Read”, “Reading” or “Read” to help you manage your reading progress.

![How a collection can look like. You can also use colored tags to further categorize papers (e.g., I use green tags for reviews). Do not pay attention to the large number of unread papers.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1000/1*jcGxHYkPrg9_jwyIdmSDgw.png)

### Synthesizing the knowledge

Summarizing the insights from dozens of research papers can quickly become overwhelming. That’s where [**NotebookLM**](<https://notebooklm.google.com/>) steps in. Unlike standard tools that summarize or let you “ _chat with_ ” a single document, NotebookLM lets you upload multiple sources into a single, unified knowledge base. You can even drag and drop your scientific papers directly from Zotero. _Don’t say I’m not making things convenient._

Ask complex questions and you’ll receive detailed answers — all backed by citations from the documents you have uploaded. Notebooks can be organized thematically, drawing on particular sets of papers you’ve uploaded for your specific research topics.

![Asking questions is not the only thing you can do with NotebookLM. Generate a study guide, a summary document, frequently asked questions that can drive your learning, or…](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1000/1*MAEM9Sq3Lt0t_oNVxwH-wQ.png)

Here’s where things get truly magical: NotebookLM can generate podcast-style audio overviews of your documents. You read that right. And these aren’t just robotic voiceovers — they’re surprisingly natural, dialogue-style conversations between two AI hosts casually discussing the content of your sources. Don’t believe me? Try it yourself: [go to the site](<https://notebooklm.google.com/>) (you just need a Google account) and upload any document you have at hand. It doesn’t even need to be a scientific paper.

AI-generated podcasts are a brilliant way of absorbing dense material while commuting, walking, or just resting your eyes. That’s one thing you should remember to do if you are staying fully digital.

### Reflecting on the knowledge

Letting AI summarize papers for you is undeniably practical, but it’s not how you truly learn or retain information. To build real understanding, you need to engage actively with what you read — and that means taking your own notes.

If you want to keep things digital, _good news_ : there’s no shortage of note-taking apps to choose from. In fact, it doubles as _bad news_ : there are too many of them.

Some options are more traditional, like [Evernote](<https://evernote.com>), [OneNote](<https://www.onenote.com>), or Google Keep. They are great for quick captures or general productivity. But if you’re looking for deeper organization, personal knowledge management, or networked thinking, consider tools like [Notion](<https://www.notion.com>), [Logseq](<https://logseq.com/>), or — my personal favorite — [**Obsidian**](<https://obsidian.md/>).

At its core, Obsidian is a Markdown editor. That means every note you create is a simple, lightweight text file using [Markdown](<https://www.markdownguide.org/>), a formatting language that lets you write with structure — like headings, bullet points, and links — without using clunky toolbars or proprietary formats. In practice, this means notes are easy to create and are always accessible, even without the app. If Obsidian is gone, your notes are not.

One of Obsidian’s biggest strengths is its ecosystem of community-developed plugins, which allow to extend the app’s features. This makes Obsidian pair beautifully with Zotero thanks to plugins like Zotero Integration or Citations, which let you:

* Pull in references directly from your Zotero library.
* Automatically generate properly formatted citations.
* Insert bibliographies.
* Link each citation to its corresponding paper or note.

![Your very own literature review may look like this. Replace my jargon with your jargon. Replace my folder structure with your folder structure. Choose your own colors. Choose your own plugins. But don’t spend too much time with that. Science is what truly matters.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*rwe14x-QTb3b05Lumo5s0A.png)

It’s a great way to build your own _personal literature review system_ — one that grows and evolves with your interests. Make it as simple or complicated as you need.

### Keeping up to the latest research

This might be the _most important step._ Science is always evolving. The goal isn’t just to know: _it’s to keep knowing_.

You might be interested in something as broad as a certain journal — say, _The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition_ (nutrition), _The Lancet Oncology_(cancer research), or the _Journal of Environmental Psychology_ (behavioral science). That’s easy enough: bookmark the online site, check it regularly, or sign up for email alerts. But if you’re interested in a _topic_ rather than a publication, you’ll want a more dynamic approach.

You will be looking into crafting a custom search in a database like [**PubMed**](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/>), a comprehensive database of biomedical literature. You can perform a classical search by keywords or craft a more advanced search, for which PubMed has its own query language. For instance, it uses a controlled vocabulary called MeSH, which groups related concepts and synonyms under a unified tag — so even if different papers use different wording, they’ll still show up in your search.

![What do current studies say about caffeine’s effect on sleep? You may already know the answer, but try crafting a search like this to see if research surprises you. Notice how you can create alerts/RSS, below the search bar.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1000/1*TIwxLVxkPWzMOiV7s-sZIA.png)

Not sure how to craft a good search? Don’t worry, it’s the 21st century! Ask a tool like ChatGPT to help you generate one. Once you’ve created a solid search, how you stay updated is up to you. You can:

* Set up an email alert in PubMed, and get notified when new papers match your query.
* Or, go for a more flexible and distraction-free method: RSS feeds.

_RSS feeds_ let you subscribe to updates from websites — like journals or PubMed search results — and receive new content automatically in one place. Instead of checking each site manually, an _RSS reader app_ (like [Feedly](<https://feedly.com/>) or [**Feeder**](<https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.nononsenseapps.feeder.play&hl=es>)) pulls the latest articles into a single clean feed. Like building your own, custom research newspaper.

![Example of what an RSS feed looks like (in the Feeder app, Android). This feed comes from a PubMed search on a specific term (“ctDNA”, again, my jargon) across several renowned journals. Now and then, I check it over coffee. Way better than doomscrolling, right?](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*ZBt0IY_97qYSKwDf5tICSw.png)

### Conclusion

Research in the digital age is a different game. With the right tools, you can access, organize, and synthesize information more efficiently than ever before.

But no matter how advanced the technology, one thing must not change: _you need to always think critically_. Whether the information comes directly from a peer-reviewed journal or summarized via an AI model, never take it at face value. Question it. Cross-check it. Keep digging.

> Above all, **stay curious**. That’s the real driver of good research — and the best tool you’ll ever have.
