## The Token Problem

With most frontier AI models today, including LLMs, you need to understand that it’s all about tokens.

_But what is a token?_

### Semantic Units

In short terms, in the case of LLMs it’s a word or subword, a way to break a sequence of text into smaller parts. Then, the LLM will predict the next token in that sequence.

That is the vanilla answer you will see that never really answers the question. In reality, a token is much more: **it’s the smallest unit of information with an implicit internal meaning**.

In layman’s terms, it is a way to break up inputs into semantically meaningful parts:

* A text into words or subwords
* An image into pixels or patches of pixels
* A video first into frames and then into patches of pixels but also incorporating temporal information across frames

However, you will agree that breaking inputs into tokens might force the model to lose the overall composition of the input. These tokens don’t just carry meaning by themselves; their overall meaning is also a function of their surrounding context.

Simply put, just like the word ‘bank’ will have a different meaning (river bank or financial institution) depending on the context, this also applies to images too (a patch of pixels depicting an animal ear could depict a wolf or a husky depending on the pixel resolution and the rest of the image).

So, _how do we make tokens share information?_

### Mixing Tokens and Channels

Here’s where the attention mechanism you’ve probably heard talk about often comes in.

In simple terms, the attention mechanism is a token mixer, a neural circuit that allows tokens in a sequence (words in text or pixels in images) to share information and, importantly, update their meanings with regard to each other.

For the sake of length, I won’t go into detail, but the image below [and the following link](<https://thewhitebox.ai/the-attention-mechanism/>) will explain everything you need to know about this mechanism.

![The Attention mechanism. Generated by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*pz-7ocLPJkU4uuguqUHfPw.png)

But you may realize that the input sequences don’t have all the information. For instance, in the sequence _“Michael Jordan played the game of…”,_ nothing in that input sequence indicates that Michael was a basketball player.

Therefore, we need to find a way to add that exogenous information to the mix, which is, of course, inside the model.

For that reason, **we also need a channel mixer** , in the case of LLMs in the form of MLPs, neural circuits that embed core knowledge learned by the model into the tokens in the sequence.

This gives you the overall structure of LLMs, known as a Transformer block, depicted below:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:518/1*yyjeWuNsQrpqN1-F7Qtv-A.png)

Consequently, all LLMs do is take an input sequence, use attention to make tokens in the sequence talk to each other and use MLPs to embed core model knowledge into the mix so that the model gains a clear intuition about what the next word (token) is.

> RMSNorm is a normalization layer that ensures the intermediate calculations remain stable during training and inference.

_And why am I telling you all this?_ Simple, because these two mixers have a hard truth: **the unit economics are painful.**

### A Huge Complexity

Both mixers have problems of their own.

In the case of attention, the cost complexity is quadratic to the input sequence length for memory and compute, meaning:

* doubling the input sequence length quadruples compute and memory requirements
* tripling the input sequence length increases both ninefold
* Adding insult to injury, in inference, for long sequences [the attention cache](<https://thewhitebox.ai/kv-cache-chatgpts-memory/>) is the biggest limiting memory factor.

And as if that weren’t enough, MLPs increase both compute and memory just linearly but account for**two-thirds of the total number of parameters** and, at scale, **almost all the compute.**

> [According to Meta](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.07185>), MLPs can account for 98% of total compute at scale.

Hence, we have to heavily limit the number of tokens we allow to send to a model, a term we know as the context window, **with most models still having a very modest size.**

> As most LLMs are Transformers, sequence length is also limited due to the extrapolation problem, an issue where the model decreases its performance heavily when run on sequences much longer to those used to train it.
>
> This problem is thought to be solved by combining Transformers with Mamba layers, [as Samba](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/samba-an-ai-miracle-3d9a7e83a8e4>), but that goes well beyond the extent of this article.

Long story short, the shorter the sequence, the better. But this, in most modalities, is a huge problem, especially when working with spreadsheets.

## A Great Compression Step

When wanting to use LLMs with spreadsheets, something deeply desirable if we ever want to use Excel or Google Sheets in a declarative form (asking what you want instead of doing), we need our AIs to understand the spreadsheet.

But here’s the thing: spreadsheets carry an insane amount of information beyond the actual text: structure, cell addresses, distribution, etc.

Thus, if we look at our previous segment on tokens, the amount required to capture the meaning of a spreadsheet can be huge. For reference, [a spreadsheet with 576 rows and 23 columns yields 61k tokens](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.09025v1>), **almost half of GPT-4o’s entire context window**.

This leaves little room for the user's query and any additional context the model might require, such as information from other sources. Naturally, for bigger spreadsheets, the problem is simply untractable.

Therefore, [Microsoft proposes a new encoding method that maximizes compression](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.09025v1>). _But what does that mean?_

### A Lossy Extraction

Luckily, in most spreadsheets, there’s abundant unnecessary information, especially when you want to catch the gestalt of the spreadsheet.

Therefore, the amount of actually useful information we have to send to the LLM is much, much smaller.

And this is what Microsoft proposes with SheetCompressor, a three-step process to encode a spreadsheet with minimal essential information loss by drastically reducing the number of tokens actually fed into the model.

* **Structural-Anchor-Based Compression**

In this phase, we address the issue of large spreadsheets containing numerous homogeneous rows or columns that minimally contribute to understanding the spreadsheet’s layout and structure.

![Source: Microsoft](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*00zyBPqpAgEY7V6Wu976JQ.png)

To do so, it identifies structural anchors, which are heterogeneous rows and columns at possible table boundaries (highlighted in yellow above). Next, homogeneous rows and columns that are far from these anchors are removed, resulting in a condensed “skeleton” version of the spreadsheet.

> A more detailed description of the heuristics (methods) used to identify the boundaries can be found in [Appendix C in the pape](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.09025v1>)r.

The extracted rows and columns are then re-mapped to ensure continuity in cell coordinates, preserving data relationships within the compressed spreadsheet.

* **Inverted-Index Translation** :

In this phase, we aim to improve token efficiency by handling numerous empty cells and repetitive values. Instead of traditional row-by-row and column-by-column serialization, this method uses a lossless inverted-index translation in JSON format.

> Lossless means that no information is lost in this step.

In other words, it creates a dictionary (bottom part of the image below) indexing non-empty cell texts to their pertinent addresses and, whenever two cells have identical values and are close together, they are merged to compress the information even more (if cells B2 to B4 show the same value, we aggregate it as B2:B4) all the while guaranteeing data integrity.

![Source: Microsoft](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:396/1*KUBhxTZc9YwUvegSFoskAQ.png)

* **Data-Format-Aware Aggregation**

Finally, the third phase aims to reduce the number of tokens by aggregating adjacent numerical cells that share similar numbers or formats.

For that, in this step, the encoder isn’t looking to store precise values but to capture the data type and how these data types spread across the sheet.

> Although this step is lossy, too, it’s entirely deterministic (the output is always the same for the same input), as proven by the algorithm described in [Appendix M](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.09025v1>).

This is key and one of the main misconceptions regarding this compression method: **the goal is to streamline spreadsheet understanding without excessive token expenditure**.

![Source: Microsoft](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:410/1*G0jSXEgLYu0uoHQtvpHa1g.png)

Naturally, this part results in a loss of information, but provides sufficient information for the LLM to understand ‘what the spreadsheet is about’.

If you’ve reached this point, you may be tempted to believe that this process will fail due to information loss. But here’s the thing:

You are ‘sort’ of right (or wrong, depending on how you view it).

## Chain-of-Spreadsheet

When Ethan Mollick, a Wharton professor, praised this method, many jumped to conclusions too quickly, arguing that it wouldn’t work.

### The stochastic problem

Many people reacted to this piece by claiming that most Excel activities should be entirely deterministic and, thus, avoid randomness. This is true and, importantly, goes against the nature of LLMs, signaling that each should not work.

_But why?_

LLMs are stochastic models. When making a prediction, they don’t output the next token but the distribution over the next token, **from which they arbitrarily sample one of the top most plausible tokens.**

> This is why hallucinations are a thing and why they aren’t a bug… but a feature of the model.

However, the actions one usually performs on spreadsheets, such as calculations, macros, etc., are all deterministic. In other words, if we execute a certain calculation or process, the outcome should always be the same and no information should be lost.

As you may imagine, the previously described sampling method LLMs follow goes against this.

Then, _why am I saying these people are wrong?_

### Spreadsheets aren’t meant to lose information

Whenever we think about compression in AI terms, remember to add the term ‘lossy’ before. In a nutshell, AI compression always means ‘loss of information.’

Needless to say, the better the compression, the more essential data is kept, and LLMs are prime examples of this, as they are nothing more but large-scale data compressors.

Nevertheless, SheetCompressor also compresses the spreadsheet into a dictionary lossily, as not only the boundaries in step one can be wrong, but in step three, **we willfully discard numerical data to keep only the data type.**

Acknowledging this, haters quickly ‘kill’ this method by arguing that ‘lossy compressions don’t work with Excel.’

But as I said earlier, they are jumping to conclusions too quickly, simply because people on the Internet love to correct others. _But what do I mean by that?_

Here’s the thing:

> Sheetcompressor is not part of the analytical process; we only use it to help the model capture the overall meaning of the spreadsheet.

Therefore, once the LLM understands the spreadsheet, it identifies the part related to the user’s query and performs vanilla extraction, **aka extracting all data from that part of the process, a process called Chain-of-Spreadsheet.**

In other words, SheetCompressor is just a great way to encode the overall meaning of the Excel/Google Sheet, allowing the LLM to then extract the key parts of the data fully (with no information loss) and use those as context to answer the prompt.

Fears neglected._And the results?_ Quite extraordinary.

### A New SOTA

For starters, in terms of compression, **_SheetCompressor_** achieves a 25x increase over the vanilla method (full extraction).

Ironically, despite discarding more information than a full extraction encoding, a GPT-4 model fine-tuned with this method achieved a 27% improvement over full extraction, meaning that not only is the process more efficient, **but the signal-to-noise ratio upgrade also yields a more performant model.**

![ICL means In-context learning models (without fine-tuning). Source: Microsoft](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:582/1*BBEoPWaz0xsP_T1pPPNS_w.png)

## The Future of Excel?

For a while, I’ve argued that software's future as a whole is declarative, where users ‘declare’ what they want instead of actively ‘doing it.’

In that future, LLMs enhanced with tools (agents) will be a key component, and methods like SpreadsheetLLM will be of dire need, considering the obvious issues with the current generation of agents (think Copilot365), which are slow, buggy, and error-prone.

While the method still has limitations, like not being capable of encoding cell color, it’s a very promising path toward fully-fledged agentic software, the key unlocker of productivity that Generative AI has been heralded as being.
