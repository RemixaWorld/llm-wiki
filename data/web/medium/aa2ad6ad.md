## It Makes… Total Sense??

As you can guess by the name, the core idea that has some making [such bold statements](<https://x.com/untitled01ipynb/status/1843170432087626227>) about Entropix [is entropy](<https://github.com/xjdr-alt/entropix/tree/main>).

In information theory of communication systems, like chatbots, one of the key areas of mathematics directly applying to Generative AI, we dictate that**the higher the entropy, the higher the uncertainty.**

Simply put, **the higher the ‘entropy,’ a measure of ‘disorder,’ the more ‘unsure’ the model is over the next prediction**. Therefore, if we minimize entropy, models should perform better predictions and, this way, reduce hallucinations, which usually emerge when the model is very uncertain of its own predictions.

_But what does all this gibberish mean?_ It sounds very complicated, but it’s not.

### Understanding the basics of LLM decoding

As you probably know, when predicting the next word, LLMs model their entire vocabulary of known tokens (words or subwords), ranking them according to their appropriateness as the next word by assigning them probabilities (how likely word _‘x’_ is).

Consequently, we can measure how ‘uncertain’ a model is by how this distribution looks. If the model assigns 99% probability to a word and 1% to the thousands of other words in its vocabulary combined, we can confidently say that the model is pretty sure about what the next word should be.

On the other hand, if the most likely token has a probability of 30%, we can argue that the model has no clue. In the former case, we say the model’s prediction has very low entropy. For the latter, it’s the opposite.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*jmfHAbVplqyO_FnJ)

If we look at the image above, one could make a solid case that the entropy of the next prediction is high; the model isn’t really convinced that any of the possible words are unequivocally more suitable than the rest.

So, _what does Entropix propose?_ In simple terms, when fighting hallucinations, **we have been doing it wrong all along.**

Very wrong.

### Move Away from Greedy Decoding!

Currently, whenever we want to minimize model hallucinations, we normally recommend setting the temperature hyperparameter of the model to 0.

_But what is this ‘temperature’?_ Before we describe this crucial parameter, we must clarify what’s going on just before the model outputs a new word.

The last layer of an LLM (without considering the unembedding look-up table, which takes the chosen vector embedding (a vector representing a word) and turns it into the actual word the user sees) is the softmax layer of the last Transformer block.

This layer takes the logits, the score of each token in the vocabulary that is a candidate to become the next word in the sequence, and transforms them into values between 0 and 1, ensuring that they sum to 1.

This, in essence, is equivalent to categorizing words relative to each other as more or less likely next candidate to the sequence, which in turn tells us what words the model prefers over others.

> For the sake of length, I will not entertain the discussion whether these are actual probabilities (they are not, because the softmax layer rarely (if ever) outputs a calibrated probability distribution). What this means is that a word having an 80% probability of being the next word doesn’t mean that the word would be chosen 80% of the time in this identical setting.
>
> Our current approach is very convenient (we don’t need calibrated probabilities) because it distributes the LLM’s entire vocabulary (all the possible words it knows) according to their suitability as next word to this particular sequence, which works just fine most of the time.
>
> Thus, while statisticians would be right to call me out for saying the output is a probability distribution, it certainly helps us see what the model thinks is the most likely word, the second most likely, and so on, **which is why everyone calls it a ‘probability distribution’**.
>
> To support the statistician’s view is that models tend to be overconfident, assigning higher probabilities than the real ones to each word.

But I digress.

> Long story short, for every single prediction, the model goes way beyond a simple next-word prediction; **it’s actually modeling uncertainty** , making an effort to consider not only the best likely outcome but also the chances the most likely prediction turns out to be wrong.

However, when it comes to decoding (sampling new words from a model given a sequence), we as an industry have largely ignored this unique capability of models to acknowledge their limitations for every single prediction, which is the missing gap that Entropix precisely taps on (as we’ll see later).

Simply put, we can measure uncertainty for every prediction, but we largely decided not to. Instead, **we took the much more convenient and simple-to-implement path of greedy decoding**.

_But what is that?_

Looking at the image below, this is the equation we use in the softmax layer to transform a list of real numbers into a distribution of numbers between 0 and 1 that sum up to 1 (thereby becoming a list of probabilities).

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:370/0*iy9EwCcCmETb17VD.jpg)

_Notice the ‘T’ variable?_

That’s the temperature hyperparameter we mentioned earlier. By adapting that parameter, we effectively model these probabilities' distribution, as shown below.

For a very small ‘T’ value (left), the distribution becomes much more skewed toward a tiny subset of values, effectively reducing uncertainty. On the other hand, for large values (right), the probability distribution becomes much ‘flatter,’ which can be interpreted as the model being very uncertain of which word should come next.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*fkiwslTbSK6_yqTb.png)

As you may have realized by now, we aim for very small temperatures in situations where we want the model to be as accurate and predictable as possible.

In particular, most model APIs support ‘T=0,’ which the industry calls ‘greedy decoding.’

> Mathematically speaking, this wouldn’t be possible because it would lead to an infinite value in both exponents in the above equation, but what these labs are doing behind doors when you specify the temperature to be zero is that they define a very small value of ‘T’ (not zero, maybe 0.2 as the image above on the left) and simply instruct the model to choose the most likely next word every single time no matter what.

But here’s the thing: although we may not realize it at first, **doing this doesn’t necessarily improve the chances of the model not outputting a hallucination;** we are simply forcing it to assign a much higher probability to one word relative to the rest.

In reality, by making the model choose a word no matter what, this is akin to a torturer hitting a man until he confesses a crime. We might have led to a confession, but that does not mean it’s real.

Indirectly, this gives us peace of mind that we are doing our best to reduce hallucinations, **but that doesn’t mean that the real uncertainty, whether the model truly knows what the next word should be, is reduced at all.**

> In fact, as proven by Entropix and [Google’s research](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10200>), the opposite is true; with _greedy decoding_ , the quintessential method to reduce model hallucinations, **we are actually making things harder for the model**.

Long story short, we were achieving the exact opposite of our intended goal, and adding a simple heuristic could change the hallucination problem forever.

_But how?_

## A New Decoding Era?

As a summary of the previous segment, in our standard ‘hallucination-proof’ decoding method, greedy decoding, even if the model was uncertain, we nevertheless forced it to settle for an answer immediately, which, as we have seen, actually leads to the model hallucinating even more.

_But how is that possible? Can we prove it?_ Luckily, that’s what Google did.

### Let Models Hallucinate!

[Google Deepmind proved a few months ago](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10200>) that chains of thought are intrinsically present in a model’s next-token prediction task.

_But what do they mean by that?_

Chain of thought occurs when an LLM, instead of simply going straight for an answer, takes a multi-step approach to solving tasks, taking its time and generally leading to better outcomes, just like humans tackle complex probelms.

For instance, OpenAI’s o1 models, the new state-of-the-art in AI reasoning, do just this; they are given ‘time to think’ and generate several reasoning chains (ways to solve a problem) until they converge on what the model considers to be the best approach (thought process) and answer.

Due to traditional LLM’s difficulty eliciting these reasoning chains, we thought these models were simply ‘incapable of intrinsic reasoning,’ which led to prompt engineering techniques like asking the model to go ‘step-by-step’ (chain-of-thought prompting) or simply providing examples (few-shot prompting) to solve a problem in the prompt to induce this behavior.

But, again, that’s because we were terribly wrong.

With low-temperature values, while incentivizing the model to collapse its probability distribution to make it easier to sample a token with ‘higher certainty’ (again, we know that the real certainty isn’t affected by temperature) this actually led to worse reasoning outcomes, as we can see in the image below:

![Source: Google](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*nH_L0jR0ont9uwEB)

As we observe, when we choose low temperatures (they use ‘k’ instead of ‘T’ here, but it’s the exact same hyperparameter) we force the model into ‘acting very certain’ leading to short and confident answers that, in reality, are terribly wrong.

For instance, if we measure the model’s true uncertainty for the prediction in greedy decoding (k=0), the model is just 2.9% certain. Yet, the model gives us a single-token response, which is akin to a human being so — falsely — convinced about its thoughts that it doesn’t need to think for long and gives a straight answer.

> But this is an illusion of confidence, and the model has actually no idea if its answer is remotely accurate!

However, if we assign a higher temperature, leading to what at first may seem much more uncertain predictions, and we couple this decoding process with an uncertainty threshold (aka, do not stop thinking until you get to a certainty above x%), the model starts ‘thinking’ and, magically, eventually arrives at a prediction that, if measured, **_is actually much more certain!_**

This leads us to Entropix, the decoding method that has broken the Internet by implementing just this.

### The Entropix Approach

To implement ‘entropy-measuring LLMs,’ Entropix generates ‘pause’ tokens when uncertain and uses this _‘extra breathing time’_ to think until a certain uncertainty threshold is met.

As mentioned, the logic behind this decision is that we want the model to choose the token it is most certain about. However, we just needed to take a different approach.

> In fact, I am myself ‘guilty’ of recommending ‘greedy decoding’ with no second thoughts when wanting to decrease hallucinations as part of my [prompting guides](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/hacks-make-genai-pro-today>) (my guides are still accurate for everything else).

_But how do we measure uncertainty?_

There are different methods, but the Google paper proposes using the ‘probability gap’ between the first and second most likely tokens averaged across all predicted tokens in the sequence.

In other words, without intervening the model with low temperatures that induce the model to falsely create the certainty gap, we let the model think until it reaches a token prediction where the difference between the probability of the first token and the second token is naturally large, meaning the model is intrinsically certain (not externally influenced by prompting or temperature to be certain) about which word should come next.

> Specifically, Google added the average over all prediction uncertainties, but that simply complicates the explanation too much and the intuition is exactly the same.

> This is the kind of research where you look back and say, ‘I mean, it was kinda’ obvious.’ And when research feels that way, the likelihood of it being a big deal is huge, explaining why it has drawn the interest of so many relevant researchers.
