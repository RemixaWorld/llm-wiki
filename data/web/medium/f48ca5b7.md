## **Introduction:**

Our journey through advanced modern LLMs has reached its final segment. While I’d love to cover all aspects of modern LLM research, there are simply too many papers to explore each area in depth. Therefore, I’ve focused on research that has significantly shaped the field of LLMs. In this article, I’ll introduce you to two highly influential studies on the “mixture of experts” (MoE) approach.

As we saw in the article on Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), fine-tuning tends to emphasize parts of the matrices that were already learned during general training. But what if we could emphasize them in a slightly different way? More specifically, what if we took the output from the self-attention layer and fed it through different feedforward modules within the transformer? Each of these modules, known as “experts,” would be able to emphasize the outputs in unique ways.

Although the original MoE research paper was published in 2017, before the findings of LoRA, it’s fascinating to see how later research connects back to these earlier insights.

## **Background:**

While I won’t delve deeply into the original MoE paper, I highly encourage readers to explore “Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer” by N. Shazeer et al., 2017. I’m not covering this paper in detail because it introduces the MoE concept in the context of LSTM — a model now considered outdated for LLMs. Additionally, the MoE concept from this original paper has been only slightly adapted to work with transformers in the 2022 paper, “Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity” by W. Fedus, B. Zoph, and N. Shazeer. So, explaining the latter paper will naturally cover the essentials of the original work.

The idea behind Switch Transformers is straightforward: rather than creating a model with more parameters that fully activate all neurons, this approach activates only a small number of neurons across the entire transformer model. This sparsity reduces computation during inference. Furthermore, by having multiple small layers in parallel and activating just one layer, the model can be fine-tuned with minimal additional computational demands during inference.

### **Gating Router Calculation for the Original MoE:**

As readers examine the architecture, the gate router is likely the most intriguing aspect. Let’s dive into the mathematics and mechanics behind it.

To start, I’ll introduce the gating router as described in the original paper:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Rofi6ZN_FoWfR95WqFOqaw.png)

It’s important to note that the original paper developed this mechanism for an LSTM (long-short term memory) neural network. Therefore, the input xxx represents a single token. Below is the architecture illustrating how this gating router functions within an LSTM model:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*5o-ywqjR7LD4Kd2uLeWnvw.png)

As shown, the output G(x) must have dimensions of [1 x n], where n is the number of feedforward (expert) modules in the MoE layer. We then apply a softmax function on G(x) to determine which expert feedforward module to use based on probability.

If x is used directly as a single token, then Wg needs to be sized [1 x n] so that G(x) will be the correct size of [1 x n]. If a token, x, is modified to represent a specific vector space, [1 x d], then Wg would need to be sized [d x n] so that again G(x) will have the dimension of [1 xn]. However, the original LSTM MoE introduces an additional twist to the gating router with top-k selection and Gaussian noise. The top-k function on softmax allows selection of the top-k expert feedforward modules rather than just the top one. The authors believe that using multiple experts in aggregate can lead to better outcomes, as it resembles the concept of a random forest model.

The mathematics of top-k and adding the Gaussian noise take the following form:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Rla7hNCzgR1tSmzsHzmXMQ.png)

With top-k, you are not creating a [k x n] matrix as G(x) for each token. Instead, you are still generating a [1 x n] matrix as the output from x multiplied by Wg. However, you set the values below the top-k positions to negative infinity, so their probability of being chosen becomes zero after applying the softmax function.

The authors also added Gaussian noise for an important reason: they observed that a few “strong” experts were consistently being chosen, which led to bias and poor training outcomes. This happened because the same experts were repeatedly activated, which limited exposure of all parameters to the training samples. Adding Gaussian noise encourages the model to explore a broader set of experts, promoting diversity and more balanced training.

**Gating Router Calculation for Switch Transformer:**

In the Switch Transformer, the top-k approach is simplified to select only the single top choice. The authors do not explain why this change was made, but they show that it maintains model quality and performance. Here’s some additional context:

With the LSTM model, there’s only one opportunity to select the correct expert because there is only one MoE layer. However, in the transformer architecture, each layer can choose its own expert. This means a single token has multiple chances to pass through different experts before reaching the final output, which is somewhat equivalent to selecting multiple experts in aggregate.

Additionally, the authors found that Gaussian noise introduced instability. As discussed in my article on LLM training, transformers require a gradual warm-up phase to train effectively. Transformers are highly sensitive to the degree of random noise, so the authors removed the Gaussian noise addition.

One important note: since transformers process an entire sequence, not one token at a time as in an LSTM model, there needs to be a slight adjustment to the dimensions in the gating router calculations.

Take a look at the image below that briefly explains the dimensions of variables in the MoE layer of the transformer.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*PCc02rJGCM2uaxjKV0yAEA.png)

As expected, the input to the MoE layer has the dimension [embedding x sequence], just like in a standard transformer. Don’t be confused by the two inputs shown for the MoE layer. The authors just want to highlight that each token of the sequence is passed through different experts. However, similar to a normal transformer, the original input remains the entire sequence.

In contrast to the regular transformer, where the entire [embedding x sequence] passes through the feedforward layer, only one token is selected from the input. As shown in step 2, one token embedding is extracted from the input, which has the dimension [embedding]. This token then goes through the gating router process, as illustrated in the equation below:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*QMnqN_qHKdjUcz_Y-_gSvA.png)

Just like in the original paper, a [1 x n] vector is created from the gating router at the end. How is this possible? Note that h(x) = Wr * x. Since x has the dimension of [embedding], Wr must have the dimension [n x embedding]. This makes both h(x) and p(x) have the dimension [1 x n]. This modification of the W matrix is what distinguishes the Switch Transformer from the original MoE model.

In step 3, the input goes through the feedforward layer, changing the dimension from [embedding] to [4 x embedding], and then back to [embedding].

Finally, in step 4, we multiply the output from the feedforward layer (which has the dimension [embedding]) by the softmax output probability from the gating router corresponding to that token. This results in an output with the dimension [embedding], effectively scaling the output by the token’s probability calculated by the gating router. This step ensures that, even after the expert is chosen, the importance of each expert is weighted by the softmax probability output.

Since steps 2 to 4 are done for each token (or for each embedding slice in the [embedding x sequence] input), the process is repeated for the entire sequence. The results are then concatenated, resulting in an output of dimension [embedding x sequence].

**Distributed Switch Implementation:**

When designing an architecture, a data scientist must always consider both memory and computation. If many tokens are directed towards a single expert, and that expert is within one computational machine, the tokens must be processed sequentially, which can lead to long inference times. To address this issue, the authors introduce a capacity factor:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*RLUdsmtQi8ZpeesJ9_YlVQ.png)

Ideally, all experts would receive the same number of tokens per batch to optimize inference time. However, this could sacrifice accuracy, as some tokens might require processing by the same expert. To manage this, the capacity factor can be adjusted during training to set a maximum number of tokens that each expert can handle during inference or training. If any tokens “arrive” at an expert that has reached its capacity, those tokens will not be processed and will instead be added to the residual.

**Auxiliary Loss Calculations:**

While distributed switching can reduce the amount of sequential computation through capacity control, it cannot guarantee or encourage an even distribution of tokens across the experts. To address this, the original MoE paper introduces an importance loss:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*e0BXq346RT7zSfRcjqudmg.png)

In this case, CV refers to the coefficient of variation, and w_importance is manually tuned. I won’t go into much detail here, as this auxiliary loss function is slightly more computationally expensive due to the CV computation, which introduces an extra step that scales with the number of experts.

Instead, the Switch Transformer introduces a different loss function that doesn’t scale with the number of experts. Let’s take a look at the loss function that the authors of Switch Transformer developed:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*MumJsWYo1TnZ28zh1ud4_A.png)

The title of the loss function is important, especially for those new to deep learning or even seasoned data scientists, as it’s easy to forget that any loss function must be differentiable. Therefore, making the loss function differentiable is significant.

Now, let’s look at how the loss function is calculated. The inputs to this loss function are p(x), T (the number of tokens in the batch), and N (the number of experts in the MoE layer). These parameters are typically predetermined before training or calculated along the way in the MoE layer, eliminating the need for recalculating certain values (such as CV in the original paper).

Besides reducing computation, this loss function is automatically minimized when the probabilities and tokens are evenly distributed. I could prove this with calculus (which would be the most direct approach), but I’ll offer a more general explanation. This is similar to maximizing the area of a rectangle with a fixed perimeter. If you calculate the derivative, you can see that when the perimeter is distributed evenly, you get the maximum area (which forms a square). In this case, the “area of the rectangle” is the denominator of the loss function, so the loss function is minimized when the probabilities and tokens are evenly distributed.

This auxiliary loss function will be applied to every layer of the MoE in the transformer and then added to the final accuracy loss after being scaled by alpha constant.

## **Results:**

Before we dive into the results section, it’s important to set some expectations for how the Switch Transformer would perform compared to the MoE transformer (which uses top-k and the CV loss function). We can expect no significant improvement in accuracy. After all, the goal of this research is to reduce computation time while maintaining similar accuracy, since the research replaces top-k with a single value and swaps the auxiliary loss function for a more computationally efficient one.

Below is the result:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*ShBKuEu6o3MF5p0CrnT3iw.png)

As you can see, the “Quality after 100k steps” column does not show a significant accuracy improvement for Switch-Base compared to MoE-Base. However, we should focus on the “Time to Quality Threshold” column. This column shows that, compared to MoE-Base, Switch-Base achieves nearly a 20% reduction in computation time.

## **Conclusion:**

The Switch Transformer and Mixture of Experts (MoE) models represent significant advancements in scaling neural networks efficiently. Both leverage a sparse activation approach, meaning only a subset of the model’s parameters are active during each forward pass. This reduces computational costs while maintaining high performance, making these models attractive for large-scale tasks. The Switch Transformer, a specific type of MoE, routes each input token to a single “expert” model, rather than multiple experts, to simplify training and reduce resource consumption. This design enables scaling to billions of parameters without requiring linearly increasing compute resources, effectively balancing model size with inference efficiency.

The key advantage of both Switch Transformer and general MoE architectures lies in their ability to maintain or even improve accuracy on complex tasks, like language processing, without proportionally increasing computational load. This efficiency can be highly beneficial for real-time applications, such as translation and recommendation systems, where fast response times are critical. However, challenges remain, including the need for complex routing algorithms, potential issues with expert imbalance, and difficulties in deploying these models on hardware with limited parallelism. Overall, Switch Transformers and MoE models demonstrate a promising path forward for large-scale neural networks, balancing scalability with efficiency in transformative ways for AI applications.
