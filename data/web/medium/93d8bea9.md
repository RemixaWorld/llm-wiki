Coming fresh out of South Korea, a team of researchers has presented a new training method for Large Language Models, named **Odds Ratio Preference Optimization (ORPO)** , that offers increased efficiency in terms of computation and, importantly, **seems to create better-performant models.**

Fascinatingly, it ‘kills’ one of the most troublesome and expensive stages in the process, which could mean that fine-tuning models will become affordable for consumers and enterprises, causing a massive explosion of new models with improved performance.

Indeed, ORPO positions itself as a huge democratizing force over LLM training, one that gives even more power to the open-source and enterprise communities to create better models and release themselves from the persistent claws of the immensely rich incumbents.

_But how?_

## From Two to Three, and Back to Two

For starters, it’s important to note that LLMs are neural networks (NNs), so they have a very specific training method: **glorified trial and error**.

### How NNs Learn

To train a neural network, we compare its responses to the ground truth (the correct answer). This gives us a ‘signal’ on how well the NN is performing so that we can adapt its parameters to minimize that loss.

In the case of LLMs, contrary to popular belief, the output isn’t a single word,**but a list of them.**

As we want them to predict the next word in a sequence, the model gives its opinion on how adequate each word in its vocabulary is as a continuation to the input sequence.

Therefore, the model’s output is a probability distribution over the next word, the full list of its vocabulary (the words that the LLM knows and can generate) distributed according to their likelihood as a reasonable ‘next word’, as we can see in the example below:

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:647/0*zJfVf0alKkzJ1dmw)

> **But why do we do this?**
>
> LLMs are models that model (generate) language. Unlike other types of predictions, predicting the next word is highly non-deterministic, as there are multiple ways to continue a sequence while saying the same thing. In other words, for a model to appropriately model language in a creative and varied way, it needs to account for, or handle, **uncertainty**.
>
> Looking at the image above, depending on the context, any option could be valid. Thus, broadly speaking, **the model not only has to be capable of guessing what word looks more certain, but also account for the chances that other words might be correct too**. For this reason, probability distributions are widely used in Machine Learning, not only in LLMs.

But if the model outputs a list of possible words, _how do we measure its performance?_

To measure the loss, we use an **objective function**. In the case of LLMs, we use the **cross-entropy function** below, where **‘ _yi_ ’** is a boolean with a 1 over the ground-truth word and a 0 on the rest, and ‘** _yhat_** ’ is the probability the model assigned to the correct word.

Simply put, the **loss** of the model (how bad its prediction has been) is measured by looking at the probability it gave to the correct word. In other words, the higher the probability the model assigns to the word that it should, the better your model is.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*m1-S5_Q-Ajadhgx4)

> **Why the negative sign?**
>
> As we are calculating a loss, we want it to decrease over time, not grow. Thus, if we maximize the probability of the correct word, we need to add a negative sign so that it works as a loss. Otherwise, we would be maximizing the loss (making the model worse).

This single equation above has dominated the entire industry for years now, **as all LLMs, and I mean all, are trained by optimizing the model against this function.**

But then,_why do we bother measuring the probabilities assigned to the rest if we simply ignore them?_ Bear with me, **as this is one of the critical points underpinning ORPO’s breakthrough.**

With that said, although the process revolves around this equation, the path to building a ‘ChatGPT’ model is more complicated.

### The LLM Training Pipeline

Training an LLM is a two-step process that’s actually a three-step process yet sometimes four-step process that ORPO aims to perform in two steps.

Sounds like a tongue-twister but it’s not. These stages are standardized across the industry, and are as follows:

1. **Pre-training stage:** The model is fed with all the data that is humanly possible to ingest. Here the model learns to predict the next word in a sequence accurately, **but it’s incapable of following instructions.**
2. **Fine-tuning stage — Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT phase):** Here, the model is trained using a [supervised training method](<https://thewhitebox.ai/simple-introduction-to-ai/>) to follow instructions, hence why you will often see models at this stage with names such as “**Instruct-GPT** ” (OpenAI) or “**Llama 3 8B-Instruct** ” (Meta). Still, these models are often uncut for production-grade environments due to, ironically, their unwavering desire to help the user.
3. **Fine-tuning stage — Alignment:** Here the model undergoes **safety training**. While remaining helpful (step 2), **it learns to not respond to dangerous requirements.** Here, models are trained using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), DPO, or both, as [Llama 3 researchers did](<https://ai.meta.com/blog/meta-llama-3/>).

> It must be noted that in the third stage the objective function we saw earlier changes. As mentioned, in steps 1 and 2 we want the model to learn to predict the next word. **But in step 3, we want the model to improve decision-making, which might sound the same but it’s not**.
>
> For that, the model is given two accurate responses to a prompt, with one being ‘better’ than the other one (this is called a preference dataset) and the model has to learn to choose the correct one. The model obtains a reward whenever it chooses the ‘better’ response and a penalization otherwise. In other words, while steps 1 and 2 revolve around maximizing ground-truth token probability, **step 3 is a reward maximization process.**
>
> [**For an in-depth review of the full training pipeline, read here.**](<https://thewhitebox.ai/llms-the-backbones-of-frontier-ai/>)

Overall, for big models, this process takes months and millions of US dollars, sometimes in the 9-figure range. For example, it is rumored that Meta has spent upwards of $100 million training Llama 3.

But despite its costs,**the aforementioned training pipeline has remained untouched for years,** making both pre-training and fine-tuning something only a few — the usual suspects — can actually afford.

However, finally, ORPO might have solved this.

## ORPO, Throwing Steps Out of the Window

From above, it becomes really hard to understand why we divide fine-tuning into steps 2 and 3. _Aren’t both modeling the model’s behavior?_

### Strangely Resilient

In the LLM training pipeline, we have two clear stages, **pre-training and fine-tuning**. But, crucially, **all real knowledge the model learns during the whole training is achieved during pre-training.**

Therefore, **the fine-tuning stages (steps 2 and 3) are simply behavior adaptation.** Instead of teaching the model new stuff, **we are modeling the way it will behave.**

In particular:

* In step 2, the supervised fine-tuning or SFT phase, **we turn it into an extremely helpful conversational assistant.**
* In step 3, the alignment phase, **we set guardrails to prevent the model from eliciting discriminatory behavior**(like racism or homophobia) while also preventing it from aiding in exercises of dubious ethical considerations, if you know what I mean.

> By now you will have realized that steps 2 and 3 are contrarian; it’s a trade-off. You sacrifice a certain degree of utility (the whole point of the model) in lieu of safety considerations.

Yet, one can’t stop wondering why aren’t they done together. In fact, many teams have tried to discard the former altogether, **but the results obtained from keeping it always justify its inclusion despite how expensive it is.**

Now, [ORPO](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07691.pdf>) researchers seem to have figured out a solution to doing them together, faster, and with better results.

_But how?_

### Be Careful with What you Implicitly Assume

If we trace back to the objective function, while this function indeed maximizes the likelihood of assigning the highest possible probability to the correct word, **it completely forgets about the rejected responses, as the ‘ _yi’_ boolean term marks a zero for every other response.**

> This is pretty ironic, because we are forcing the model to assign probabilities to unchosen words too, all to not pay attention to their tendencies over time.
>
> In fact, while the earlier equation indeed induced the model to maximize the probability assigned to the correct word, **we mistakenly assumed that it would also implicitly learn to assign very low probabilities to the rest.**
>
> And well, as it turns out, **it does not.**

To prove it, they performed a test. They fine-tuned a model using SFT, but instead of just monitoring the probabilities assigned to the correct words, they also monitored the probabilities assigned to the wrong words (words that are not equal to the ground-truth label).

Fascinatingly,**they** **witnessed how both increased over time.**

> In fact, in some cases the probabilities the model assigned to the wrong words are **higher** than those assigned to the correct word. But as we never monitor rejections, researchers don’t even realize this is happening!

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:596/0*m5F6UazjoQ5kF_4_)

And this is the key issue all along. The equation we saw earlier, while improving the model’s accuracy tendency to choose the correct word, **implicitly increases the likelihood of undesired responses too** , making the need for an extra stage, alignment, completely inevitable.

> The key intuition is that if both options are ‘valid’ to the model, it’s really hard for it to choose one. Thus, we need it to not only assign a higher probability to the correct word, but it has to be much, much higher to decrease the model’s uncertainty in the decision.

_So what did they do?_ Well, they introduced a penalty term.

### One penalty a day keeps the mistakes away

Unlike the previous cross-entropy equation, ORPO proposes a new objective function, **where _LSFT_ is the original cross-entropy we saw earlier (maximizing the correct word’s probability)**, and **_LOR_ is the odds ratio between the chosen and rejected responses.**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:458/1*yefyx45ouWLh6pSNKTpzZA.png)

> **But how does the odds ratio work as a penalty term?**
>
> The Odds function (represented above two times) indicates how likely an event is to happen in the presence of another event. In layman’s terms, given **‘x’**(the input sequence to the LLM) how likely is the model to choose**‘yw’**(the winning response) or **‘yl’** (the response that should be rejected).
>
> Consequently, the higher the odds of the former over the latter, aka the higher the term on the top over the one below, **the higher the value of the odds ratio (the division between both) will be.**
>
> This way, LOR works as a penalty term, as we are implicitly telling the model: **“As the metric to evaluate your accuracy also includes this new term it’s not enough that you assign a big probability to the correct response, but you also need to assign a very small probability to rejected responses, otherwise LOR will be large so your overall loss will also be large”.**
>
> **Clarification:** Again, we add a negative sign at the beginning as we are aiming to decrease the loss.

Consequently, the overall loss the model learns to minimize is as follows:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:504/0*ZAvnBsGOeTJCKys4)

Where.

* **_LSFT_** increases the likelihood of choosing appropriate responses
* ** _LOR_** decreases the likelihood of choosing bad responses

And with this, researchers conclude that **the SFT phase (Step 2) is no longer required** , allowing for a much improved computational efficiency and, interestingly, better overall results, **as models trained with this method improve over models trained with traditional pipelines.**

_But why was it so important to get rid of the SFT phase?_

## Beautiful, and Extremely ‘Door-Opening’

After several years following the same training pipeline, ORPO seems to hit the right notes and presents itself as the **first** method to eliminate the SFT phase for good.

> ORPO training is already available in [HuggingFace’s TRL library](<https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/orpo_trainer>).

### A Huge Efficiency Gain

In particular, **the computational efficiency gains are massive** , as step 2 required an additional reference model for step 3.

In layman’s terms, step 3, the alignment phase, required having two ‘copies’ of the model in step 2; one for training, and the other one as a reference from which to compare. Of course, this meant that for every training step, both models had to be prompted, which essentially doubled the costs.

> **Technical explanation:** RLHF and DPO techniques used in step 3 require a reference model to avoid excessive drift. The reason for this is that, if we recall, **step 3 is not word probability maximization, but reward maximization.**
>
> This means that simply optimizing against the ‘alignment objective’ (the reward) would induce the model to learn to ‘hack’ it, even generating ‘gibberish’ text sequences that makes no sense but still generate a high reward for whatever reason.
>
> Consequently, we add a term that measures how different the model in step 3 is from step 2, as a penalty for this drift. Simply put, we wanted to align the model while retaining its qualities from step 2.

But without the SFT phase,**you avoid having to instantiate an additional model to use as a baseline, which saves huge amounts of money and time.**

### A Force of Disruption

As we are seeing time and time again, fine-tuning is a must when we really want to optimize foundation models for particular tasks. Hence, in my view, **ORPO opens many doors to both open-source and enterprise communities by making fine-tuning much more affordable.**

Until now, fine-tuning has been a prohibitively expensive task, and people around the industry have adopted the mantra that ‘prompt engineering’, or modifying the prompts given to the model to ground it to the particular task, ‘was enough’, **which is of course completely false.**

With ORPO, I expect companies, researchers, and even data scientists around the globe to take matters into their own hands to train highly-performant models.

Was that the case, it may be an unstoppable deterrent that prevents the incumbents from becoming even more powerful and influential, preventing the most powerful technology in the last 30 years from becoming a _de facto_ outsourced technology from the whole world to three or four players.
