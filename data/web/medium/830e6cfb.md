From roughly 2018–2023, theAI model recipe was simple and it worked embarrassingly well. Make the model bigger. Feed it more text. Use more computers. The famous “scaling laws” paper from OpenAI showed that capability improved predictably and reliably as you turned up any of those three dials. GPT-2 → GPT-3 → GPT-4 was essentially that recipe applied at increasing scale.

But 2024 was the year in which improvements in model performance were primarily driven by post-training and scaling test-time compute. In terms of pretraining there hasn’t been as much news. The publicly accessible internet, the raw material for pretraining, is largely exhausted as a source of new signal.

**So, what replaced it?**

Let’s look at each of these innovations in more detail.

As of 2026, the industry has hit a “Data Wall” where high-quality human text is essentially exhausted. This has forced a pivot from **Quantity** to **Verifiable Quality**.

The frontier has moved past “SFT → RLHF.” Here is what is actually happening in the labs right now.

## **1\. The Death of the “Cold Start”: RL-Zero**

For the first decade of deep learning, the training pipeline followed a clear sequence: **first you teach the model what good answers look like (supervised fine-tuning), then you reward it for producing better ones (RL).** The assumption was that you needed the supervised step to give the model a foundation, a sense of format, style, and reasoning structure, before RL could usefully improve it. Without that foundation, the RL would fail.

**DeepSeek-R1-Zero demolished this assumption**. The design choice to bypass the supervised fine-tuning phase entirely before RL training stemmed from a hypothesis that human-defined reasoning patterns may actually _limit_ model exploration; whereas unrestricted RL training can better incentivize the emergence of novel reasoning capabilities.

The mechanism they used is called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. Where the dominant RL algorithm (PPO) requires training a separate “critic” network to estimate baseline rewards, adding significant memory and compute overhead, GRPO eliminates the need for a separate critic network entirely. Instead of training a value function, it estimates the baseline from a group of responses.

For each prompt, it samples a group of outputs and computes the advantage of each response by normalizing its reward relative to the group’s mean and standard deviation. The model doesn’t compare itself to an external standard; it compares each of its answers to the other answers it just generated for the same question.

The reward signal is almost aggressively simple: it is solely based on the correctness of final predictions against ground-truth answers, without imposing any constraints on the reasoning process itself. No one told the model _how_ to think. They only told it whether its answers were right or wrong.

What happened next is genuinely surprising. The **model develops an ability to reflect upon its own solutions by revisiting and evaluating prior components of its reasoning process**. Similarly, the model begins to explicitly test out and explore alternative solutions or approaches while trying to solve a problem. Notably, this behavior is not explicitly programmed into the model — RL allows the model to discover different strategies for arriving at a correct solution.

Researchers at DeepSeek called one specific emergent behavior the “Aha Moment”: **a point during training when the model learns to pause mid-reasoning, recognize that its current approach isn’t working, and backtrack to try something different** — a behavior recognizable to anyone who has ever worked through a hard problem on paper. It wasn’t trained to do this. It discovered it was useful.

The self-evolution of DeepSeek-R1-Zero underscores the power of RL: rather than explicitly teaching the model how to solve a problem, simply providing the right incentives allows it to autonomously develop advanced problem-solving strategies.

The practical results were staggering. On AIME 2024 (a competition mathematics benchmark), the **pass@1 score increased from 15.6% to 71.0%** , with output token length per problem also increasing — indicating the model naturally learns to solve tasks with more thinking time and token generation.

One important caveat emerged from follow-up research: the base models used for R1-Zero training may already exhibit self-reflection keywords and behaviors before RL training begins, suggesting that at least some of what appears to be emergent behavior may have roots in pretraining rather than being purely a product of RL. The honest read is that RL seems to _amplify and sharpen_ latent capabilities rather than create them from nothing. That’s still remarkable. But it’s a more nuanced claim than “reasoning emerged from pure reward signals alone.”

What changed is the _cost structure_ of the discovery. You no longer need expensive human-annotated chains of reasoning to bootstrap the process. You need a verifiable reward signal and enough compute. That’s a much lower barrier.

## **2\. PRMs: The “Step-by-Step” Reward**

The most common failure mode in early RL training for reasoning tasks was something researchers called “**getting the right answer for the wrong reasons.** ” A model would produce an answer that happened to be correct, but via a chain of reasoning containing logical errors. The outcome reward signal which only cared whether the final answer was right, would reinforce the entire reasoning chain, including the flawed steps. Over time, the model learned to produce confident-sounding but structurally broken reasoning.

The technical term is “**sparse credit assignment.”** If the only feedback signal comes at the end of a long reasoning chain, there’s no principled way to identify _which_ steps contributed to the success or failure. Outcome Reward Models provide a reward signal only for the final output, typically mapping an entire output sequence to a scalar reward. The step-level nature of **Process Reward Models** allows the discovery and correction of localized errors, enabling improved explainability and targeted feedback during generation or evaluation.

The practical difference is significant. With an ORM,**a model that produces five correct steps followed by one catastrophic error and somehow arrives at a lucky correct answer gets fully rewarded**. **With a PRM, that sixth step gets a negative signal** regardless of the final answer. The model learns that the _path_ to a solution matters, not just the destination.

OpenAI’s foundational paper on PRMs (“Let’s Verify Step by Step”) established the empirical case. Process supervision significantly outperforms both forms of outcome supervision. The PRM regularly surfaces correct solutions to MATH problems that have a low single-digit percentage solve-rate under the generator. These are problems the base model could theoretically solve but almost never does in a single attempt, the PRM effectively guides the search toward the rare correct paths.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*5i4MAtWyJNhbbGM7jVrTIg.png)

PRMs also unlock a powerful capability at inference time. In “test-time scaling,” PRMs function as verifiers or critics, scoring each candidate in a set of independently generated solutions. Output selection is then based on maximizing the aggregate step- or token-level reward, outperforming outcome-only ranking or self-consistency selection. You generate 16 candidate reasoning chains, score each one step-by-step with the PRM, and pick the one that scores highest. The PRM effectively turns brute-force sampling into guided search.

The active research front is making PRM annotation scalable. Human annotators rating individual reasoning steps is extraordinarily expensive, OpenAI’s PRM800K dataset required trained annotators to label hundreds of thousands of steps. Commonly used Monte Carlo estimation-based data synthesis for PRMs typically yields inferior performance and generalization compared to LLM-as-a-judge and human annotation methods. Monte Carlo estimation relies on completion models to evaluate current-step correctness, leading to inaccurate step verification.

The frontier solution is a hybrid: use Monte Carlo sampling to generate candidate step labels cheaply, then apply a strong judge model to filter and validate them. This combination: cheaper than pure human annotation, more accurate than pure Monte Carlo — is where the field has largely landed. The deeper ambition, still unsolved, is a PRM general enough to verify reasoning steps in domains where you can’t check the answer mathematically — law, strategy, policy analysis — where “correct” is inherently contested.

## **3\. Test-Time Scaling Laws (The o1/o3 Paradigm)**

The discovery that launched a thousand research papers is deceptively simple: you can trade inference compute for reasoning quality. Give a model more time to think, and it thinks better. This isn’t obvious. A model with fixed weights shouldn’t get smarter just because you let it run longer. But it does — and understanding why requires understanding what “thinking” actually means in this context.

The standard LLM pipeline is shallow: a prompt goes in, tokens come out in a single forward pass, one after another. There is no explicit deliberation, no backtracking, no checking of prior steps. In contrast, a reasoning model like o1 or o3 is trained to generate a long internal “thinking” stream — a chain of tokens where it explores approaches, verifies sub-steps, notices errors, and revises — before committing to a final answer. Crucially, it is trained to _optimize this search process itself_ , learning when to push forward and when to reconsider.

**o3’s core mechanism appears to be natural language program search and execution within token space at test time** , the model searches over the space of possible Chains of Thought describing the steps required to solve the task, in a fashion perhaps not too dissimilar to AlphaZero-style Monte Carlo Tree Search, guided by some kind of evaluator model.

The AlphaGo analogy is worth dwelling on. AlphaGo with MCTS achieved an Elo rating of approximately 5,200; without MCTS it dropped to approximately 3,000 — well below top human players. This example underscores the importance of inference-time compute in achieving superhuman performance. The game-playing insight has been ported into language: intelligence isn’t purely a function of the model’s weights; it’s also a function of how much deliberate search happens at the moment of answering.

The search strategies being explored vary in how they balance breadth versus depth. Simple “best-of-N” sampling generates N independent answers and picks the highest-scoring one, wide and shallow. Sequential refinement iterates on a single answer, deep and narrow. Monte Carlo Tree Search navigates a tree of partial solutions, expanding promising branches and pruning dead ends. Methods based on standard MCTS use a fixed branching factor as a hyperparameter.

As demonstrated by the success of repeated sampling, effective inference-time scaling requires leveraging a diverse and vast output space, so a fixed width hinders scaling. The most recent work like Sakana AI’s AB-MCTS — allows the branching factor itself to be adaptive, dynamically deciding whether to generate new candidate responses or refine existing ones based on feedback signals.

The cost curve is steep but collapsing. Early o3 at high compute cost tens of dollars per query. A year later, o4-mini matched o3’s performance at roughly 10x lower cost. The economics of inference-time compute are following a similar trajectory to the economics of pretraining compute expensive early, rapidly commoditized as the industry scales production.

There are only two techniques that scale indefinitely with compute: learning and search. The o1 paradigm marks a shift in focus to the latter. [X](<https://x.com/DrJimFan/status/1834279865933332752?lang=en>) What’s emerging is a two-tier model: cheaper, faster models for routine tasks that don’t require deep deliberation, and more expensive reasoning models that are deployed specifically when the answer matters enough to justify the compute. The question of _when_ to use deep reasoning and how to automatically calibrate reasoning depth to problem difficulty is one of the open engineering challenges defining the current moment.

## **4\. Quiet-STaR: Token-Level Latent Reasoning**

Every reasoning system described so far operates at the level of visible, discrete steps — either a chain-of-thought you can read, or a search tree you can inspect. Quiet-STaR operates at a more granular and stranger level: every single token the model generates is preceded by an invisible internal monologue.

Quiet-STaR is a generalization of the Self-Taught Reasoner in which language models learn to generate rationales at each token to explain future text, improving their predictions. Generated rationales disproportionately help model difficult-to-predict tokens after continued pretraining on a corpus of internet text with Quiet-STaR, zero-shot improvements on GSM8K went from 5.9% to 10.9% and on CommonsenseQA from 36.3% to 47.2%.

The mechanism is architecturally novel.**Quiet-STaR utilizes custom learnable meta-tokens to explicitly mark the start and end of generated thoughts. A mixing head, a shallow MLP, is employed to dynamically combine predictions with and without rationales. The mixing weight for two logits is learned, determining how much the hidden thought should influence the final token prediction. The model generates a thought, the thought influences its next word prediction, and if the prediction becomes more accurate because of the thought, REINFORCE reinforces the thought generation. The thoughts that help get strengthened; the thoughts that don’t get pruned out.**

The practical consequence: the model learns which tokens are genuinely hard, which ones require deliberation, and learns to think harder before producing them. Inspection suggests it disproportionately helps predict tokens where recalling relevant information is useful, such as the name of an applicable theorem or the start of the next step in a proof. For a model writing a mathematical proof, it doesn’t need to think hard about filler words. It does need to think hard before committing to the name of the theorem it’s about to invoke. Quiet-STaR learns to allocate cognitive effort token-by-token.

This is a fundamentally different relationship between computation and output than anything in the standard pipeline. The model isn’t thinking once before answering. It’s thinking _continuously_ , at varying depth, proportional to the moment-to-moment difficulty of what it’s about to say.

The engineering challenge is significant. Generating rationales for every single token multiplies the compute cost dramatically. Due to high computational cost demanded by token-level reasoning, the process is designed to generate multiple rationales in parallel using a special attention map that enables all thought tokens to only pay attention to themselves, all preceding thought tokens within the same thought, and the preceding text.

Follow-up work (Fast Quiet-STaR) explored reducing the number of explicit thought tokens by training the model to _internalize_ the reasoning — progressively replacing explicit token-level rationales with implicit compressed representations. The endpoint of that trajectory is a model where thinking is invisible not because it doesn’t happen, but because it’s been compressed into the weights themselves.

Whether this approach will scale to match the benchmark performance of explicit reasoning models remains an open question. The numbers so far are promising rather than decisive. But the underlying idea — that intelligence should be _continuously_ allocated rather than reserved for explicit reasoning blocks — is philosophically distinct from everything else being built, and worth watching.

## **5\. The Synthetic “Immunology” (Solving Model Collapse)**

The adversarial filtering pipeline described in the original section is real and widely deployed. But it sits within a larger and more alarming context that deserves its own treatment: the entire AI industry is now wrestling with whether training on AI-generated data will eventually poison the systems it’s trying to improve.

The problem was formally named and studied in a 2024 Nature paper. Model collapse refers to a degenerative learning process in which models start forgetting improbable events over time, as the model becomes poisoned with its own projection of reality. As model-generated data becomes pervasive on the internet, models trained on web-scraped data increasingly train on the outputs of their predecessors.

The mechanism is statistical, not malicious. Generative models don’t reproduce the full distribution of human text — they approximate it. Rare, unusual, or outlier content is underrepresented in the model’s outputs relative to its frequency in genuine human writing. When you train the next model on outputs from the current one, those rare cases are underrepresented again. Over successive generations, models trained on AI-generated data initially lose information from the tails — the extremes — of the true distribution of data (early model collapse). In later model iterations, the data distribution converges so much that it looks nearly nothing like the original data (late model collapse).

The analogy to genetic inbreeding is apt: a population that only breeds within itself loses genetic diversity and eventually produces offspring with narrow, fragile traits. The AI equivalent is a model that confidently produces plausible, grammatical, average-sounding text — and has lost the capacity for the genuinely unusual, highly specific, or structurally complex outputs that made the original training data valuable.

The critical finding is that collapse only becomes catastrophic under a specific condition. Model collapse is inevitable in a “replace” scenario — where each model trains _only_ on synthetic data from its preceding model. In the “accumulate” scenario, where each training uses all real and synthetic data generated so far — collapse is avoidable. The problem is that the accumulate scenario requires continuous access to clean, human-generated data. And the internet — the primary source of that data — is filling up with AI-generated content at a rate that makes provenance tracking increasingly difficult.

This is the quiet long-term crisis behind the short-term training pipeline innovations. Every lab knows it’s happening. Few have disclosed exactly how they’re addressing it.

The multi-model adversarial filtering pipeline described earlier is one response. Rather than using raw synthetic data, practitioners apply filtering steps to remove low-quality synthetic samples before retraining. Synthetic sentences are often screened using grammar checkers or LLM-as-a-judge pipelines. Training on filtered synthetic data not only avoids model degradation but can even enhance performance. The key is the filter: a separate, harder-to-fool model whose job is to identify and reject the average, the repetitive, and the structurally shallow outputs before they enter training.

The economics of this approach scale in a counterintuitive direction: as models get better, the filter must also get better to remain discriminating. This creates a permanent arms race between generation and verification inside the training pipeline — an adversarial game that the field is now playing indefinitely, with no clear terminus. The long-term answer may be provenance watermarking — baking source identifiers into AI-generated content so future scrapers can reliably filter it. But no such standard exists yet at scale, and the incentives to implement one are deeply misaligned: every lab would prefer that its competitors’ data be watermarked, not its own.

## Closing Thoughts

A lot of benchmarks have been beaten or broken completely in the last few months. The moment we make a benchmark, we can also identify how to teach that to model as well. But the fundamental question remains, how long till we have systems where we can mathematically constraint and control systems. To me, current system still feels a bit hacky, an advance form of memorization.

Be it RLHF or test time scaling, none of them really understand what is happening, they perform well because of simple statsitcal reality. And who knows even humans might be doing that, but I doubt that.

Think about it for a moment, none of these figure out ways to improve or learn anything, all these are finely tuned by humans, and at best what we build is generate n-options and choose one with some stastical metric assigned to it.

And if you want to see proof of that, these models are still terrible where there are no verifiable proofs or methods.
