## A very naïve explanation of LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) like Gemini or GPT-4 are incredibly versatile and work wonders for a variety of tasks simply by being asked. They can do your homework and get you some A’s, write polite emails so you don’t have to tell Sandra from Accounting to f*ck off, come up with a diet plan to lose some fat or gain muscle, or write some Lana Del Rey-ish lyrics. All you have to do is ask, and you can ask almost anything.

But in fact, what we are asking is more something like “Here is some text. How this text might go on?” or maybe “What words are the most likely to come next?”. LLMs get to answer such questions based on the distribution of tokens (words, parts of words, punctuation, etc.) available in the [public corpus](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_text_corpora>). This is why conversational agents based on LLMs, like ChatGPT, as impressive or scary they are, may still get confused or misunderstood at times, and produce nonsense, fabricate information, or outright lie.

An interesting example to further explain the importance of the train data of LLMs is that allegedly ChatGPT, [would answer ‘42’ most of the times, when asked to produce a random number. ](<https://www.reddit.com/r/ChatGPT/comments/1796evg/so_apparently_asking_chatgpt_to_give_you_a_random/?rdt=32973>)A prevalent explanation of why this happened is related to the Douglas Adams’s book ‘The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy’. In ‘The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy’, number 42 is supposed to be the ‘Answer to the Ultimate Question of Life, the Universe, and Everything’ — a concept that apparently takes such a large portion of the public corpus data that GPT-3.5 was trained on, that it leads it to be greatly biased on the number 42. Most probably, not a great random number generator… Similarly to life imitating art, any AI application more or less reproduces human expression, knowledge and bias of the data it was trained on, operating in a GIGO principle.

Daniel Kahneman in his book ‘Thinking fast and slow’, explains that a human brain works in a similar ‘statistical’ way at times. When we are required to answer ‘What is 5 x 6?’, we don’t actually do the multiplication on our brains from scratch, but rather recall that ’5 x 6 = 30’, in an automatic, ‘what is most likely to follow?’ way. This works because we have heard and seen it thousands of thousands of times in the past, in other words we have ‘trained’ our brain and now we can retrieve the correct answer with a 100% accuracy. Thus, we don’t really ‘think’ about it. This automatic response is somewhat similar to how LLMs predict text.

On the contrary, in order to calculate a more complex, or not so usual product like 12 x 52, we would have to consciously work through the problem, put in some effort, maybe feel a little uncomfortable, and perform the multiplication algorithm. This would be a completely different mental process than the previous, automatic multiplication we performed. This category of deliberate thinking is essentially the kind of thinking that AI is not yet capable to perform — carrying out a wide variety of tasks that requires knowledge or reasoning beyond their training data.

## Everything can be human

The question of whether AI will ever be equivalent to human consciousness involves not just the technical capabilities of AI but also our perceptions of what it means to be human and conscious. We can easily attribute human features to almost anything, without it necessarily resembling a human, even a pencil.

> … I can pick up this pencil, tell you its name is Steve and go like this… and part of you dies just a little bit on the inside. ~Jeff Winger, Community S01E01

We can more formally refer to this phenomenon as [anthropomorphism](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphism>), if we are being fancy. That is, attaching human-like attributes, features, behaviors, characteristics, and ultimately consciousness -a soul-, to objects or animals. A similar concept is [personification](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification>), that is representing abstract concepts with the form of a human. This includes many different things like natural phenomena (the wind, sky, or sea), good or bad traits (beauty, freedom, envy, or greed), places (cities, continents, and countries), lots of godly and religious figures, as well as, animals that go to work, do laundry and drink coffee. Sometimes we may go even further and give a name to our car, or some part of our body. This tendency is deeply rooted in human psychology, allowing us to form emotional connections with objects and concepts that do not have consciousness.

Would we consider any of these _actually_ conscious? Eh, most probably not. Nevertheless, on an emotional level, we can connect with all these things, even if they literally never said a word to us or even existed, even if our brains do understand that they are soulless. It only takes us to imagine ‘what would my car say, if it had a voice’, and then we maybe name our car Bob. Ultimately, humanity is fairly inclined to accepting something has consciousness rather than rejecting it.

Imagine how confused our prehistoric brains may be when they get to interact with ChatGPT or Bard, spontaneously generating bunches of words that **_do sound very much human actually_**. It is very easy to get ahead of ourselves and forget all that statistical distribution mathematical nonsense. Our capacity for anthropomorphism makes it rather easy to forget that such models operate purely based on statistical patterns rather than true understanding or consciousness. That’s why it is so important to get ourselves educated as AI consumers. Anyone using a LLM should have at least a superficial understanding of how LLM’s function, how their responses are formulated and what data they were trained with. They do not “understand” or “think” (for now at least) in the way humans do. Instead, they produce responses by determining the most statistically likely continuation of the given input.

## What does being conscious even mean?

Even if AI was conscious, how would we ever even know? There is no definition of human consciousness; we still don’t know what makes us _us_. Regardless of thousands of years of research, analysis and discussions, we still have no idea how consciousness works, and the concept of consciousness remains as one of the greatest mysteries of science and philosophy.

For what it’s worth, we can loosely understand consciousness as:

* Being able to think about one’s own existence as an entity separate from the environment and other entities
* Being able to experience subjective experiences (that is, only _you_ experience being _you_ here and now, and there is no way to repeat it or replicate it for someone else)
* Being able to experience sensation and feelings
* Being able to form intentions, wants and desires

Given that consciousness is inherently subjective, it is rather difficult to form a standard test, measure, or procedure, in order to define what is conscious and what is not. After all, AI maybe came a bit early actually. Advanced language models like GPT-4 or conversational agents like ChatGPT, can simulate human-like behavior and dialogue to an impressive extent; they may seem kind, or entertaining, even tell you they feel pain, sadness or joy, be angry or disappointed — actually produce any emotion or feeling that exists in the public corpus they were trained on. But we would have no way to check if an AI has actual subjective experiences or it is pretending to; if it is clever or if it imitates what a clever person would say.

In the[ ‘The Infinite Conversation](<https://www.infiniteconversation.com/>)’, German filmmaker Werner Herzog and Slovenian philosopher Slavoj Žižek have never-ending, AI-generated, discussion about anything and everything. The voice, tone, accent, vocabulary, and actual arguments presented are fairly convincing — actually, so convincing that the site creators deemed appropriate to remind us that ‘The opinions and beliefs expressed do not represent anyone. They are the hallucinations of a slab of silicon.’. The concept of a philosophical zombie (p-zombie) is relevant here. A p-zombie is a hypothetical being that behaves exactly like a human but lacks conscious experience. It eventually raises the question: if an AI can perfectly mimic human behavior and dialogue, can we distinguish it from a truly conscious being?

## On my mind

In the end, the problem is, that even before all those AI stuff, we already struggled to provide a good definition of consciousness. We have no idea how all of these things work, and what it is at the end of the day that makes us _us_. Thus, with no concrete definitions for these elements, it is quite confusing when AGI is achieved and is able to perfectly imitate the human behavior, how would we be able to ultimately evaluate it and decide if it is awake or not.
