In GenAI applications, especially retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, we tend to rely heavily on vector embeddings to make sense of unstructured data. Embeddings encapsulate the semantic meanings of text documents in a machine-readable way, in the form of high-dimensional vectors. But there’s a common misconception out there: that the only thing you **_can_** do with “unstructured” text is embed it into vectors. Vectors are the language of AI systems, right?

In a way, it sometimes feels a bit like we’ve collectively forgotten that there were ways to analyze and transform text for machine learning models without vector embeddings, and before the rise of GenAI. **Embeddings are powerful and solve a lot of problems, but they weren’t meant to do everything.**

In this article, we’ll explore why relying solely on embeddings is often insufficient and why we should consider other complementary approaches to processing text for getting maximal information and knowledge from our documents. Unstructured data is, by definition, not neatly organized into tables or formats that classical algorithms can use. However, that doesn’t mean we should strip away the structure that inherently exists — headings, hyperlinks, section relationships, metadata — and simply embed the data into vectors. In fact, when we ignore this structure, we may inadvertently make our systems weaker, less efficient, and more prone to missing crucial pieces of information. Preserving and leveraging existing structure can lead to more effective and powerful retrieval systems.

## The problem with relying solely on embeddings

Semantic search alone is often not enough to fully address the needs of complex information retrieval. By adding structured approaches — like preserving document relationships, metadata, and thematic context — we can make retrieval systems much more effective. Embeddings are powerful, but when combined with structured data, they become part of a smarter, more capable hybrid approach.

### Vector embeddings are not perfect

Vector embeddings are powerful, yes. They enable AI systems to represent documents in a high-dimensional space where the relationships between pieces of text can be evaluated through straight-forward vector operations like cosine similarity. This helps in finding documents that are relevant in a semantic sense — meaning that even if exact wording is different, the model can still identify related ideas. However, there’s a fundamental issue with trusting everything to vectors: embeddings are lossy.

In the process of embedding, we transform rich, detailed documents into a fixed-length vector, and in doing so, some nuances are lost. As discussed in my recent article, [Vector Embeddings Are Lossy](<https://bit.ly/4ffhrqi>), embeddings may fail to capture minor but important details, specific phrasing, or relationships between different parts of the document. The resulting vectors are good at summarizing the main content, but they’re not perfect at preserving everything that might be valuable for retrieving all relevant documents, particularly when queries are nuanced or complex.

Imagine asking an AI system a question about a specific aspect of a historical event. If the detail you’re interested in was embedded into a vector that didn’t emphasize that aspect enough, the retrieval might miss it entirely. The problem isn’t just the model’s understanding of the question but also how the data itself was represented. Embeddings are an abstraction, and like any abstraction, they come with trade-offs.

### The challenge of non-deterministic retrieval

Another problem with relying solely on embeddings is the non-deterministic nature of large language models (LLMs). Once a document is retrieved, the LLM’s response can vary each time, even if the same vector is used as input. This variability, while helpful for generating natural language that feels human, can lead to inconsistency in the details provided. The randomness means that even if the relevant document is retrieved, the specific information needed might not always make it through to the response.

Moreover, embeddings capture semantic similarity, but that doesn’t always equate to the exact match or the specific information needed. Sometimes, what we need isn’t the conceptually closest document but the one that contains a precise fact or detail. Embeddings, by nature, do not prioritize exact matches — they prioritize conceptual similarity. This is a fundamental limitation when trying to retrieve specific, well-defined information.

## “Unstructured” data isn’t entirely unstructured

We often call it “unstructured” data, but the truth is that most document datasets contain a significant amount of implicit structure. Documents aren’t just a soup of words — they have headings, sections, tables of contents, references, and hyperlinks. This structure helps readers understand how information flows and which pieces are related to each other, but it also serves as valuable metadata for retrieval purposes. Ignoring this structure during the embedding process strips away a lot of useful context.

In another recent article, [Your Documents Are Trying to Tell You What’s Relevant](<https://bit.ly/3BKZAJv>), I highlight how documents naturally point to other pieces of knowledge through hyperlinks, footnotes, and citations. These references are an existing form of structure that can be preserved and used for retrieval. When we embed text into vectors, we typically lose this layer of interconnectedness, making the retrieval process weaker than it could be. If we instead choose to preserve these references and use them during retrieval, we can improve the quality and relevance of the retrieved documents.

Looking at the example from the article, of a document that contains a hyperlink to another document. This hyperlink is a deliberate indication that the linked document contains related or supplementary information. If we simply embed both documents as independent vectors without considering this connection, we lose the ability to understand that relationship during retrieval. Instead, we should consider preserving these semi-structured layers to explicitly maintain these connections, thereby providing a richer context for retrieval systems.

As shown in the article, one straight-forward way of improving retrieval using document structure is using a graph RAG approach — in which we build a knowledge graph with connections between documents and concepts, and traverse the knowledge graph during the retrieval process to potentially find documents that may not have been found with semantic vector search alone.

## Other strategies for managing unstructured data

In addition to vector embeddings, there are various methods to enrich the retrieval process by leveraging the inherent structure within documents. Structured approaches, such as thematic chunking, metadata preservation, and keyword-based grouping, can significantly enhance the effectiveness of RAG systems. By integrating these strategies, we can address the shortcomings of embeddings alone and improve the overall quality of retrieval.

### Thematic chunking for enhanced retrieval

To improve retrieval from unstructured data, we should often consider how we chunk and embed documents. Often, documents are divided into chunks based on size limits, with little regard for thematic coherence. Optimizing chunking strategies — dividing documents by themes or logical sections — can significantly help preserve the meaning and structure of the content.

Thematic chunking creates self-contained, coherent text segments, improving embedding quality. Rather than arbitrarily splitting sections that may lose context, thematic chunks maintain a logical flow of ideas, making them more effective for embedding and retrieval. For example, splitting a product manual into sections like “setup instructions,” “troubleshooting,” and “maintenance” results in more meaningful embeddings compared to arbitrary, size-based chunks.

Thematically coherent embeddings lead to more accurate retrieval because each chunk represents a distinct topic, rather than a mix of unrelated content. This approach can greatly enhance retrieval quality, especially in RAG systems where context continuity is crucial for generating well-informed responses.

In [Your Documents Are Trying to Tell You What’s Relevant](<https://bit.ly/3BKZAJv>), I built an example of how a RAG system might struggle to retrieve all relevant documents when answering a query about the Space Needle in Seattle. For example, a query like “What is close to the Space Needle?” might miss contextual documents that do not explicitly mention the Space Needle but contain related information about the neighborhood.

In this scenario, thematic chunking would ensure that documents about Seattle landmarks are treated as coherent units, even if they don’t mention the Space Needle directly. Grouping documents by thematic content (e.g., all landmarks in the Lower Queen Anne neighborhood) would increase the likelihood of preserving relevant context during retrieval.

### Preserving document metadata

Leveraging document metadata is another effective strategy for enriching retrieval. Metadata such as author names, publication dates, section titles, and referenced topics can be invaluable for improving retrieval accuracy. Instead of embedding a document solely based on its text, metadata provides additional cues for relevance.

For instance, if an AI system is asked about recent developments in a field, metadata like publication date becomes crucial for identifying the most relevant documents. Preserving and using metadata during retrieval allows the system to prioritize newer documents, delivering more accurate, up-to-date information. Similarly, section titles and headings help maintain the internal structure of a document, guiding the system to the sections most relevant to a query. Treating metadata as an integral part of the retrieval process helps capture context and specificity that embeddings alone often miss.

In the Space Needle example, metadata could indicate relationships to other prominent landmarks or neighborhoods. If a document about the Space Needle includes metadata linking it to Lower Queen Anne, a retrieval system can gather related documents more effectively, even if they weren’t flagged as semantically similar during vector search.

For time-specific queries, such as “recent renovations of the Space Needle,” publication dates in the metadata allow the system to filter and prioritize documents. While vector embeddings capture general content, metadata ensures relevance by adding specific, structured cues.

### Keyword linking and search for targeted retrieval

Keyword linking and search provide an alternative to pure vector embedding. Unlike embeddings, which represent documents in high-dimensional space, keyword linking and search prioritize specific terms or phrases during retrieval, ensuring precision. This is particularly effective for queries requiring exact matches rather than just semantic similarity. Common approaches for keyword linking and/or search include: building knowledge graphs including keywords and concepts for graph RAG, integrating full-text search with vector search, and inverted indexes on text documents.

For example, if a dataset contains documents about different machine learning models, and a user asks for details specifically about “random forests”, keyword linking ensures that all documents containing the exact term “random forests” are prioritized. In contrast, a vector search might retrieve documents about ensemble methods in general, which may not directly address the query.

While keyword linking lacks the flexibility of vector embeddings in understanding broader concepts, it excels in precision. By incorporating both embeddings and keyword linking, we can balance semantic understanding with targeted retrieval — providing the best of both worlds.

Consider a scenario involving technical documents on machine learning. If a user asks, “What are the drawbacks of using random forests?” keyword linking and search ensure that documents explicitly mentioning “random forests” are prioritized over those discussing ensemble learning in general.

An embedding-based approach might retrieve documents related to ensemble learning, but this may not be enough for a user seeking specific details about random forests. Keyword linking and search ensure that the system provides targeted, precise information directly addressing the user’s needs.

## The hybrid approach: structured retrieval as a complement to embeddings

The key takeaway here isn’t that vector embeddings are bad — they are an incredibly powerful tool for representing and retrieving unstructured data. However, embeddings are not the only option, and they have notable limitations that can be mitigated through structured approaches. A hybrid retrieval system, which incorporates structured methods like thematic chunking, metadata preservation, and keyword-based grouping, can significantly improve the quality and relevance of retrieved documents. By balancing the strengths of embeddings (semantic similarity) with the precision of structured retrieval, we can create systems that are both comprehensive and precise — capable of handling nuanced queries while maintaining consistency and reliability.

This hybrid approach can be particularly effective in RAG systems, where the goal is to provide a well-informed response based on a variety of retrieved documents. By incorporating structure into the retrieval pipeline, we can ensure that our AI systems are equipped to understand not just the meaning of individual documents but also the relationships and context that tie those documents together.

## A future with embeddings, but not ONLY embeddings

Vector embeddings are wonderfully useful, but they are not the only strategy for working with unstructured data. Unstructured data isn’t entirely unstructured — documents contain inherent structure in the form of headings, links, references, and metadata, all of which can be leveraged to improve retrieval.

As discussed in my articles mentioned above, **vector embeddings often lose important details and structure** , which can limit retrieval quality. And, **preserving document relationships and references can enhance retrieval performance** by maintaining important context. By optimizing how we chunk text, preserving metadata, and incorporating keyword-based grouping, we can improve the accuracy and reliability of AI systems, especially in retrieval-augmented generation applications.

Ultimately, a balanced approach that combines the strengths of semantic search through vector embeddings with the precision and context provided by structured methods will yield smarter, more capable AI systems. Let’s start treating unstructured data as containing hidden structure waiting to be leveraged — not something to be stripped away in the embedding process.
