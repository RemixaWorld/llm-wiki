Recently, several AI labs have converged into the same idea: using synthetic, reasoning-enhanced data to improve our current models' palpably modest reasoning capabilities.

While blending active search with LLMs might be where most enthusiasts point, it might not be possible due to capital and energy constraints, as those models could be orders of magnitude more expensive.

However, a new method that automates high-quality reasoning data has emerged and has been hinted at by some (from OpenAI to Cohere or Scale.ai) **as crucial to building the next generation of frontier AI.**

Moreover, it might be the only way to prevent big tech from creating an economic moat around reasoning, the next intelligence leap.

## The Harsh Reality

While incumbents claim that AI models [are already as smart as high schoolers](<https://www.ccn.com/news/technology/openais-gpt-5-phd-level-intelligence-2026-cto-mira-murati/>) or even [as smart as undergraduates](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xm6jNMSFT7g>), these claims are totally unsubstantiated because our current methods of intelligence evaluation are, mildly put, deceiving.

### Confusing memorization with intelligence

Seeing how some LLMs have passed [the US lawyer’s BAR exam](<https://www.abajournal.com/web/article/latest-version-of-chatgpt-aces-the-bar-exam-with-score-in-90th-percentile>) or [the SAT](<https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/jul/31/chatgpt-better-than-undergraduates-at-solving-sat-problems-study-suggests>), one could be easily convinced that these models are, in fact, as smart as some humans when, in reality, **they are much closer to databases.**

_But what do I mean by that?_

Most of the current LLMs’ prowess can be reduced to memorization. In other words, if LLMs are acing those exams, it is simply because much of **the effort required to solve them can be traced to some sort of known fact or reasoning chain memorization.**

![MMLU dataset examples](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Zbqa5flHjRxciHp6QC2MgQ.png)

Simply put, most, if not all, of an LLM’s responses in these evaluation benchmarks aren’t ‘novel’ answers but simple regurgitation of facts or reasoning chains they saw during training.

> What I mean by reasoning chain is a way of solving a problem that can be memorized, like solving a quadratic equation step by step. Knowing how to solve those problems by pure memorization does not mean you are performing reasoning but simply solving it by pure instinct or memorization.

> This is akin to giving a 12-year-old a college-level test and a book that contains detailed instructions for solving every problem, and when the kid aces the test, claiming he/she is a savant.

That said, we can’t claim these models are strict databases either, as they perform extensive compression during training, meaning that they are, in a way, **extracting key features from data that they then use to solve problems.**

> **But what do we mean by compression?**
>
> Meta’s LLaMa 3–8B model is an extreme example. While only weighing 16 GB, the model was trained on 15 trillion tokens (or 12 trillion words more or less).
>
> At two bytes per token, that is 30 TB of data that the model has “learned” to reproduce pretty accurately. As the model is 1,875 smaller than the dataset, it can’t memorize the dataset fully.
>
> Instead, it stores only the crucial data (like grammar, syntax, key facts, etc.) and generalizes into the rest.

However, don’t be deceived, as in the absence of retrievable facts or reasoning processes, aka when the model can’t use its past experiences, **failure is almost guaranteed.**

And we have proof of this.

### The Easy Way to Beat Benchmarks

If we look at all the benchmarks usually pointed at when presenting new models, most of them can be aced by a human with a book on the side.

> In other words, if you have enough core knowledge on the inquired matters, novel reasoning isn’t really that much required to ace these benchmarks.

Therefore, whenever we test these models’ intelligence using these benchmarks, we must ask ourselves: _are we evaluating smartness or memorization/compression capabilities?_

Sadly, it’s really hard to discern whether the model is simply fetching everything from having seen it several times or is really thinking its way through the problems.

> **The added problem of contamination.**
>
> In some cases, the results are even less trustworthy as some of these tests are actually part of the training dataset for many models. Crucially, [in very recent research](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bDVCaYCOUfQ>), a Together.ai team proved that we can identify when a model has memorized the dataset.
>
> But how can we prove this if we don’t have access to the training dataset?
>
> **If the models do in fact understand the problems they solve in those benchmarks,** **their score should be permutation invariant**. In layman’s terms, if we switch the order of the questions, the results should be the same. Well, that’s not the case for some models, meaning they have potentially memorized the benchmark they are then tested on, a term known as ‘contamination’.

_But_ _is there a way to validate whether LLMs perform non-memorization-dependent reasoning?_ Luckily, the answer is yes.

### The ARC-AGI Benchmark

A few weeks ago, a group of LLM skeptics led by François Chollet, famous Google AI researcher and the creator of the** _Keras_** Deep Learning library, released the ARC-AGI benchmark, the Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for AGI.

This is a set of complex pattern-matching exercises where you get a few sets of examples and have to extract the pattern and use it to complete the last one.

![An ARC-AGI example. Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*BdnPzTnnf5yF1wpzyReI_A.png)

While solving this benchmark would not mean we’ve reached AGI, the team behind it considers it a prerequisite for a model to be considered AGI and has even offered a million-dollar prize for whoever achieves at least an 85% success rate.

> Models with Internet access (basically all frontier AI LLMs) cannot claim the price.

_But why the need for this benchmark?_

The key here is that these exercises are memorization-resistant, created specifically to prevent LLMs or other powerful models from seeing the tests beforehand.

At the same time, however, humans who haven’t seen those problems earlier can anyway extract the pattern with just a few examples, **meaning that an average human will get a very high score.**

But that’s not the case for vanilla LLMs, with GPT-4o having the best score at a very modest 9%.

Therefore, based on these results, in the first-ever benchmark where novelty is required, **LLMs fail miserably** , opening the question of whether every single trace of intelligence they have elicited can be cynically reduced to just memorization.

Now, independently whether that’s true or not, the question is: _what can we do about it?_

## Data Augmentation to Save the Day

Whether you fully agree with François Chollet or not, the truth is that our best models perform very basic reasoning at best.

Everyone acknowledges this. Thus, _how can we overcome this problem?_

### Enhancing Data for Reasoning

One aspect people claim causes LLMs to be poor reasoners is data. Simply put, these people argue that most data used to train these models, mainly public data, is not prepared to enhance reasoning capabilities.

Nonetheless, **it must be acknowledged that most data on the open Internet is average at best** , and most of the great content you will find often showcases the final answer, not the writer's reasoning process.

Knowing this, it’s no surprise that a model trained on that data will not develop powerful reasoning capabilities.

Hence, to solve that, synthetic data specifically designed to teach the model to think more advanced thoughts is being proposed.

> This process is being heavily championed by many, including [Alex Wang](<https://youtu.be/jNbEr9F0wiE?si=iDi8pzBD2POLgbF2>), co-founder and CEO at Scale AI, or [Aidan Gomez](<https://youtu.be/B45s_qWYUt8?si=PVxU5uNsFEJbhp0K>), co-founder and CEO of Cohere.

One example of a company that has openly researched this is OpenAI, through their famous [Let’s Verify Step-by-Step paper](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050>).

### Rewarding Thoughts, not Answers

In succinct terms, OpenAI proposes training the LLM to approach problems in a step-by-step approach by design.

Although this sounds very similar to [Chain-of-Thought](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903>), the technique in which we ask models to take a step-by-step approach to solving a problem, the idea behind this process is that **the model will work that way by design.**

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*6fHtjbdayRAIz3F3QA_69w.png)

By doing so, just like humans improve their problem-solving performance when they break a problem into parts, AI naturally becomes much more proficient, too.

_But what does OpenAI propose?_

During the alignment phase, we tune a model’s behavior by rewarding it when it answers correctly and punishing it when it doesn’t, improving the model's response pattern.

However, instead of simply rewarding the outcome, **we reward—or punish—the thought process, too**. Therefore, while the answer could still be correct, if the thought process is wrong, the model is punished (and vice versa).

![Source: OpenAI](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Vx9Yz0T8aMmA4nMO5-8Zeg.png)

Obviously, to do this, you need a much more thorough dataset, and human experts in various sciences like math, chemistry, or physics, which are extremely expensive assets to hire, to create very high-quality reasoning chains on which the model can be trained.

_But what if there’s a better way to obtain this data?_

### Chain-of-Preference

Currently, when you ask ChatGPT a question, it responds immediately without hesitation. _But what if we allowed it to think?_

In this new scenario, the model would generate various potential solutions to a given problem, using special heuristics (such as entropy, which we won’t delve into today) or verifiers—additional models that validate each generated thought.

The model continues this process until it converges into the best solution.

The seminal [Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) paper](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.10601>) is a prime example of this search process, shown below compared to the standard Chain-of-Thought approach discussed beforehand.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:648/0*nN7Ta7wPzTMqTAF_)

Long story short, ToT thoroughly explores the solution space before arriving at the best answer.

However, as you may imagine, ToT is an extremely expensive process that requires extensive text generation — the model has to generate every single thought in every solution path — but you might also need exogenous models, called verifiers, to check and validate every thought.

_But why don’t we use this method to augment the data on which to train the model and run it in full CoT style on inference?_

That’s precisely what [Chain-of-Preference Optimization](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09136>) is, a method by which we use methods like ToT to augment the data, create new synthetic reasoning data, and train the model on it.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*MiN-abcNLhbfGTmY)

In other words, instead of the model actively searching the solution space in inference, which is extremely expensive, we use this method to generate the dataset (similar to what OpenAI might have done with Let’s Verify step-by-step) and train the model to learn these reasoning chains to the point it instinctively behaves that way.

_And the results?_

Although they must be taken with a pinch of salt, **CoP seems truly powerful** , achieving higher scores than any other method, including active inference search (Tree-of-Thought) across several models and tasks, signaling that improving the model’s reasoning baseline may be an even better heuristic than performing active search.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*D1htLzCEji7gmq-U1Vm6qg.png)

## Was Data the Issue All Along?

While purists like François Chollet will argue that models that actively search for the solution in inference (and, ultimately, learn from it in real-time) are still the best path toward true AI intelligence and that CoP simply trains the model on better reasoning data,**the truth is that this might be our only hope at the moment.**

With our current, much less compute-intensive models already posing a serious threat to our power grids and [building huge data centers being a priority for all incumbents](<https://www.ans.org/news/article-5842/amazon-buys-nuclearpowered-data-center-from-talen/>), **proposing models that increase compute requirements by, potentially, an order of magnitude at the very least could not be feasible.**

Additionally, automating the curation of reasoning datasets through methods like CoP could prevent private, heavily capitalized AI labs like OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google Deepmind from building an economic moat around reasoning datasets, **as the use of human expert annotators required for that endeavor is simply not within reach mortals.**

_But is this all we need to build true intelligence?_

Probably not, considering that, as the ARC-AGI benchmark proves, **our models are still terrible few-shot learners**. Simply put, if your kid can learn to play tic-tac-toe by seeing the rules one time, our models should not need hundreds of examples to do the same.

Thus, training models to generate millions of possible responses to a given problem or teaching them to memorize reasoning chains might not be enough, and we might need additional breakthroughs to overcome our current limitations.

_The problem?_

All money has flown into one single bet, LLMs. So we better be right.
