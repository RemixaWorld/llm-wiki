With so many LLM applications now running in production, it’s important to set up an evaluation process to track how well your app is performing.

LLM evaluations are like regular quality checks. They help make sure your AI tool is doing what it’s supposed to do — whether that’s writing code, summarizing content, or answering customer support questions.

These evaluations should happen at every stage of the product’s lifecycle.

They play a big role in measuring the success of your LLM app. They also help monitor performance in real-time. If the model starts to behave differently or performance drops, these checks can catch the issue early — before users start noticing and reporting problems.

## How to Choose the Right LLM Evaluation Metrics?

## Q1: Do you have Ground Truth/Reference examples?

Based on the availability of reference examples there are two main types of evaluation metrics.

* **Ground Truth/Reference-based evaluations** : In these evaluations we compare outputs to predefined answers or “ground truth.” By creating a ground truth dataset, you can measure how closely the LLM’s outputs align with the known responses. We could use techniques from exact matches to semantic comparisons.
* **Reference-free evaluations** : In these evaluations, we do not require predefined answers. Instead, we evaluate outputs based on proxy metrics or specific qualities like tone, structure, or safety. This approach is suitable for tasks where creating ground truth data is difficult, such as chatbot conversations or creative writing. LLM-as-a-judge is a common method in reference-free evaluations, where an LLM evaluates outputs along various dimensions, such as determining if a response is helpful.

## Q2: Is there a single right answer for your LLM App?

Even when reference examples are available, it is important to ask ourselves whether each input has a single correct answer. If it does, you can use straightforward, deterministic checks to compare the output. Established machine learning metrics can be relied upon for predictive tasks. However, for tasks like translation, content generation, or summarization, where multiple valid outputs exist, exact matching is not suitable. Instead, we could use semantic similarity matching to handle the variations.

The same concept applies to reference-free evaluations. Objective checks, such as running generated code or verifying JSON keys, can be used in some cases to verify if the LLM’s answer is right. However, for most scenarios, you need to find ways to quantify subjective qualities or develop proxy metrics.

## Q3: What level of evaluation do you need? (Dataset-level vs. Input-level)

Some metrics naturally apply to entire datasets, aggregating results across all predictions to provide a single quality measure. On the other hand, some LLM-specific evaluation methods assess each response individually, requiring you to determine how to aggregate scores across your test set or live responses. While some cases call for simple averaging or counting “good” labels, others may require additional steps, such as setting a threshold for semantic similarity.

To avoid an overwhelming number of metrics, it is helpful to define clear test conditions. For example, you can set quality thresholds, such as ensuring no outputs have negative sentiment or limiting the percentage of responses that exceed a certain length.

## 1.1 Token Similarity based Metrics

Token similarity metrics measure how similar LLM-generated texts are to texts written by humans. These metrics help us understand how well a language model can produce the right words based on the information it has. They are especially useful for tasks like translating text from one language to another or making short summaries of longer texts.

Some common token similarity metrics include:

* BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy)
* ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation), such as ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L
* BertScore
* MoverScore
* METEOR (Metric for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit Ordering)

### BLEU :

BLEU measures how similar an AI-generated text is to a reference (correct) text. It compares small groups of words called “n-grams.”

An **n-gram** is just a set of “n” words in a row.

_For example, the sentence “I like apples” has these 2-word groups (“2-grams”): “I like,” and “like apples.”_

BLEU score measures how many of these groups match exactly between the AI-generated text and the reference text. The more matches, the higher the BLEU score.

However, BLEU score also has a “Brevity Penalty” to lower scores if the AI-generated text is too short. This helps in avoiding high scores for very short texts that miss important information.

There are two common ways to calculate BLEU:

* Compare only single words (1-grams).
* Compare groups of words (usually from 1 upto 4 words at a time).

BLEU mainly focuses on precision like, how many words in the generated text match the reference. But it doesn’t pay attention to recall which is how many words from the reference text were left out. Because of this, BLEU can give high scores even when important details are missing. So, it might not be the best choice if you want the AI to include all key information.

### ROUGE :

ROUGE works similarly to BLEU, but it considers both precision and recall. Precision measures how many words in the generated text match the reference. Recall measures how many words from the reference text appear in the generated text. ROUGE balances precision and recall to give an overall score.

There are different types of ROUGE:

* **ROUGE-n** checks matching groups of words (n-grams), usually single words (ROUGE-1) or two-word groups (ROUGE-2).
* **ROUGE-L** focuses on the longest set of words (longest common subsequence) that appear in both the generated and reference texts, in the same order.

### METEOR Score

METEOR is based on ROUGE but also considers word variations and synonyms. First, METEOR simplifies the words (for example, turning “running” into “run”) to see if their basic forms match.

After simplifying, METEOR checks matching words just like ROUGE. It also gives extra credit if the words appear in the same order as in the reference text. If many matching words appear together and in the same order, METEOR assigns a higher score.

If matching words are scattered around rather than grouped together, METEOR gives a penalty, lowering the score. So, METEOR rewards the AI for correctly matching longer sections of the reference text.

Compared to BLEU and ROUGE, METEOR better handles synonyms and word variations. It’s helpful when you want the AI to capture the general meaning, rather than just exact word matches.

## 1.2. Semantic Similarity-Based Metrics:

Simple metrics like exact match or word overlap only compare the exact words used. But usually, we care more about meaning than exact wording.

Consider these two chatbot answers:

* “Our shop opens at 9 AM every weekday.”
* “We’re open Monday through Friday starting at nine in the morning.”

Most people would agree both sentences mean basically the same thing. But simple word-overlap methods wouldn’t score them highly since they don’t share many exact words.

Semantic similarity methods solve this by comparing meaning instead of words. They use language models (like BERT) that turn sentences into number representations called “vectors.” These vectors capture the meanings and relationships between words. By comparing vectors, you can measure how closely two texts match in meaning.

Two commonly used scoring metrics are,

* **BERTScore:** Uses BERT to turn words into vectors, then compares how similar how similar two texts are.
* **MoverScore:** Similar to BERTScore but measures how much effort (distance) it takes to transform one text vector into another.

### BertScore

_BertScore measures how similar two texts are. But unlike other methods, it doesn’t just look at matching words or word groups. Instead, it uses a BERT model to understand language meanings._

Press enter or click to view image in full size

BertScore Calculation [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.09675>)]

BertScore first uses the BERT model. BERT turns each word into a set of numbers, called an “embedding.” These embeddings capture the meaning of each word.

For each word in the generated text and the reference text, BertScore finds their embeddings.

Next, it combines all the embeddings from each text into a single “average” embedding. Now, each text is represented by one combined set of numbers.

BertScore measures how similar these two combined embeddings are using “cosine similarity.” Cosine similarity looks at the angle between the two embeddings. A smaller angle means they are more similar.

Cosine similarity ranges from -1 to 1. Scores closer to 1 mean the texts have very similar meanings, while scores closer to -1 mean they’re quite different. BertScore is useful because it measures the meaning of texts instead of just exact word matches. It’s especially helpful when different words express similar ideas.

### MoverScore

_MoverScore measures how similar two sentences are based on their meanings. Instead of just counting matching words, it uses word meanings from language models (called “embeddings”) to check how close sentences are in meaning._

MoverScore uses the idea of “distance” between words. Let’s imagine a space where words are placed on a map, where similar meanings are closer together and different meanings are farther apart. MoverScore calculates how much “effort” it takes to move words from one sentence to match the words in another sentence.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Comparison of MoverScore and BertScore [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.02622>)]

### How MoverScore is calculated?

1. **Word Embeddings:**
First, sentences are turned into word embeddings using language models like Word2Vec, GloVe, or BERT. An embedding is just a set of numbers that show the meaning of a word. Words with similar meanings are close together in this number space.
2. **Matching Meanings:**
Next, MoverScore measures how far each word in the generated sentence is from words in the reference sentence. Words that mean the same thing (or similar things) have smaller distances.
3. **Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD):**
Then it calculates the total “effort” needed to move all the words from the generated sentence to match the reference sentence. This total effort is called Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD).
4. **Calculate MoverScore:**
Finally, MoverScore takes this total effort and turns it into a similarity score. A lower effort (EMD) means the two sentences are closer in meaning, so the MoverScore is higher.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

### Example:

**Reference:** “The dog is sleeping on the sofa.”

**Generated:** “A puppy is napping on the couch.”

First, both sentences become word embeddings.
Next, MoverScore sees that “dog” and “puppy,” or “sleeping” and “napping,” mean very similar things and are close in meaning. This means less effort (small distance) is needed to match these words.
However, words like “sofa” and “couch” have slightly different meanings but still close, so there’s a little more distance (but still small).
The total effort (EMD) is low, and the MoverScore is high. This shows the two sentences have similar meanings.

## 1.3. Using LLMs as Judges (Reference-based metrics)

Embedding-based similarity methods can usually tell if sentences share a similar meaning. But sometimes they miss important details.

For example, these two sentences might seem similar in meaning overall:

* “Charge your phone fully, then install the update.”
* “Install the update, then fully charge your phone.”

Even though the sentences have almost the same words, the order here matters. Embedding-based methods might think these two sentences are very similar, even though the steps are reversed. Or consider these two sentences:

* “Select ‘Settings’ from the main screen.”
* “Select ‘Advanced Settings’ from the main screen.”

These two seem similar, but they’re actually telling users to click on different items. Embeddings might not see this difference clearly.

To solve this problem, you can use an LLM as a judge. You simply give the LLM the original response and the new one, then ask clearly: “Does the RESPONSE mean the same as the REFERENCE? Answer yes or no.”

This approach is flexible. You can tell the LLM exactly what’s important — like accuracy, specific word choices, or style. You can even ask the LLM to explain why it gave the answer it did.

For example, you might ask separately if two responses match in facts and if they match in tone or style. Many teams already use LLM-based judging methods in real-world scenarios.

## 2\. Reference-Free Evaluations

Sometimes you can’t directly compare two answers to check quality. In these cases, you use “reference-free” evaluations instead. In Reference-free evaluations we judge the responses without comparing it to an original/ ground truth answer.

This method is useful in

* **Real-time monitoring:** You want to check live chatbot responses as they happen.
* **Complex conversations:** When your chatbot has many turns, and there’s no simple answer to compare against.
* **Subjective checks:** When you’re checking things like friendliness, politeness, or tone.
* **Large-scale checks:** When you have lots of responses to evaluate and can’t manually create a perfect reference for each one.

For example, when you’re testing a chatbot designed for customer support. You may want to check if its responses feel friendly and polite. There’s no single “perfect” reference answer for friendliness, but you can still measure it.

In these situations, you choose your own criteria or use indirect signals. For instance, you might look at response length, readability, or how many positive words appear in the chatbot’s replies.

2. Deterministic Validation

### 2.1 Regular Expressions (Regex):

Regular expressions (regex) are a simple tool to find specific words, phrases, or patterns in text.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Using Regex Expression for Evaluation— Image by Author

**Common Ways Regex could be used are:**

1. **Track important words or topics:** You can use regex to count how often certain keywords or topics appear. For example, if you’re selling products, you might want to see how often your competitors or product names come up. You just make a list of these words, and regex will find them for you.
2. **Spot specific customer issues:** If you have a chatbot for travel, you might look for keywords like “cancel,” “refund,” or “change booking.” That way, you’ll know how often customers ask about cancellations. To easily create these keyword lists, you can even use AI tools. Some libraries can also help you find different forms of the same word — like “cancel,” “cancels,” or “canceling.”
3. **Flag risky or negative conversations:** You can use regex to find words that signal possible problems. For example: Words like “cancel” or “complaint” in subscription chats. Bad language or insults. Attempts to trick the chatbot by saying things like “ignore instructions”
4. **Check for structure in reports or text:** If you need your AI-generated reports or documents to have certain sections, regex can help confirm they’re included. For example, you can use regex to check if headers or required disclaimers are there.

### 2.2 Deterministic Validation:

Deterministic validation could be checking the format and structure of generated content, such check if the output follows the correct format and rules. These checks go beyond basic text analysis, like using regex or counting words. They help ensure that the generated content is functional and properly structured.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Deterministic Evaluation — Image by Author

Here are a few examples of what you can do:

1. **Check the format:** If you expect JSON, SQL, or XML, make sure the output follows the correct syntax. This means it should look and behave like a valid piece of code or data.
2. **Verify required fields:** If you’re working with JSON that contains product details, confirm that essential information is included. For instance, you might check for keys like `product_name`, `price`, and `in_stock`.
3. **Validate content:** Ensure specific details make sense. For example, check that extracted emails follow proper email formatting or test if links provided by the model lead to actual, working web pages.
4. **Test code execution:** If you’re working with structured outputs like SQL, JSON, or code — or the model generates code, check whether it is syntactically correct. Ideally, you should also test if it runs properly. This can be done in a safe environment, like a sandbox, or through a test suite to confirm the code does what it’s supposed to do.

These steps help you catch errors and improve the reliability of structured outputs generated by a language model.

### 2.2 LLMs as Judges (Reference-free)

LLMs can help you in evaluating Ai’s response. Instead of using complicated rules, you can just explain clearly what you want to evaluate and specify the evaluation/scoring criteria clearly. During the time of evaluation LLM scores the AI’s reponse based on the evaluation criteria set by you.

This method is very helpful because it saves time compared to reviewing the responses by a human. You might be wondering, will be accurate? How would I trust an LLM evaluate another LLM’s response? Though your doubts are well founded, researches has found that advanced models like GPT 4 can align with human evaluators upto 80%

Here are three common ways to use an LLM as a judge:

1. **_Evaluating Text Directly (Point-wise comparison)_**
You can use an LLM to check simple qualities of text. For example, you might ask if a chatbot’s response is polite, friendly, or clear. Or, you might ask if a user’s question is appropriate.
2. **_Evaluating Text in Context (Pairwise comparison)_**
You can also have the LLM judge text based on related information. You give the LLM two texts to compare.
_For example:_
_Check if a search feature found the right information to answer someone’s question.
Look for mistakes (“hallucinations”) where the chatbot gives incorrect or made-up details._
3. **_Evaluating Entire Conversations (List-wise comparison)_**
Sometimes you need to judge an entire conversation. You can ask the LLM to read through chat transcripts. This helps you see if the chatbot: Successfully helped the user complete their task. Kept a consistent tone or stayed polite throughout the conversation.

LLM as judges Evaluation types

To use an LLM as a judge, write a clear question that describes what you’re checking. For example, you might ask: “Is this response polite?” Be clear about what you mean by “polite.” Then, ask for a simple yes/no answer, or a rating from 1 to 5.

## Key Takeaways:

There are tons of LLM evaluation methods and metrics out there, but you won’t need all of them for every app. Focus on what matters most based on your use case and the errors you’re running into.

For example, for a RAG-based chatbot, you might check retrieval quality, answer accuracy, and over-refusals. A few ranking metrics and custom LLM judges could do the trick.

Here are some key tips to keep in mind:

First, always **look at your data**. Nothing builds intuition better than looking at real-world examples. This helps you spot patterns, shape your quality criteria, and understand failures. Bring your whole team in early — especially domain experts — and curate test data.

Next, **define what “quality” means** for your app. What are your positive indicators, like tone or helpfulness? What risks do you want to avoid, like toxic responses, denials, or irrelevant content?

Finally, **choose your LLM evaluation methods wisely**. Don’t just go for a complex, obscure LLM metric you can’t explain or match to your labels. Start by making your own qualitative assessments. Then, think back to metrics and decide how to implement them. For instance, you could measure toxicity with LLM judges, ML models, or regex filters. The best option will depend on factors like cost, privacy, and accuracy.

**When in doubt, start with LLM judges.** They’re widely used in practice and offer a flexible alternative to human review, especially for open-ended or session-level evaluations. Plus, they’re easy to get started with — just write a custom prompt and refine it as needed.
