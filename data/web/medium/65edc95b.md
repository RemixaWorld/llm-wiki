# Magicore: Multi Agent Iteration Framework for Coarse-to-fine Refinement for improved LLM solution quality

Large Language Models’ (LLM) reasoning can be improved using test-time aggregation strategies, i.e., generating multiple samples for each problem and aggregating over them to find a better answer, but these approaches do reach a saturation point. Refinement exists as another alternative by using model-generated feedback to sample solution and improve quality, however do suffer from issues of Excessive refinement causing over-correction,Inability to localize and address errors and Insufficient refinement.

To address those issues, authors of this paper[1] propose MAGICORE, a framework for Multi Agent Iteration for Coarse-to-fine Refinement. It aims avoid excessive refinement by categorizing problems as easy or hard, solving easy problems with coarse-grained aggregation, and solving hard ones with fine-grained and iterative multi-agent refinement.

Key contributions:

* propose MAGICORE, a multi-agent coarse-to-fine framework that adaptively uses targeted feedback to outperform refinement methods such as Self-Refine, as well as aggregation methods such as Best-of-k and Self-Consistency, at all sample budgets across five datasets and two LLMs.
* proposed difficulty-aware coarse-to-fine resource allocation results in sample-efficiency, even outperforming k-way SC while using 8× fewer samples
* analysis shows the necessity of incorporating RMs for refinement, showing that an LLM alone is less effective in determining when refinement is needed compared to trained off-the-shelf RMs
* validated that while refinement baselines used do not show improvement over iterations, MAGICORE consistently improves with additional iterations

## Issues with Refinement

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Sd-8ybfG7q7jodoPaGWoNg.png)

### i) Issue 1 : Excessive refinement:

* refinement risks over-correcting, turning correct solutions into incorrect ones.

### ii) Issue 2: Inability to localize and address errors

* LLMs have a limited ability to self-correct and struggle to identify and correct their own mistakes in a targeted way

### iii) Issue 3: Insufficient refinement

* an effective refinement should gradually make the output better, but stopping too soon could leave errors uncorrected

## Magicore : adaptive coarse-to-fine refinement framework

MAGICORE is an adaptive framework that aims to improve both the performance and efficiency of multi-step reasoning with LLMs by intelligently applying test-time aggregation and refinement.

### i) Magicore Framework

* incorporate three models:

(1) an LLM with three roles: the Solver, the Reviewer, and the Refiner for generating initial solutions, targeted feedback, and refinement,

(2) an Outcome Reward Model (ORM) for generating global, solution-level correctness score, and

(3) a Process Reward Model (PRM) for generating local step-by-step correctness scores

**a) Overview**

* high-level overview of MAGICORE is presented in figure below

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*0XVwvdomle9s-CAmh8IuGQ.png)
* process begins with the Solver generating k reasoning chains for each problem, followed by the ORM and PRM providing solution-level scores
* Next, the input problem’s difficulty is classified based on two criteria: (1) the quality of the majority answer and (2) the RMs’ answer confidence
* Refinement is initiated only when the problem is deemed difficult, which occurs when the majority answer receives a low average RM score and the answer distribution is flat – indicating no single answer is significantly better than the others (i.e., low confidence).
* For these hard problems, we employ a multi-agent setup with three agents: Solver, Reviewer and Refiner.
* The Solver generates k reasoning chains, passing them to the PRM for step-wise scoring to pinpoint errors
* The Reviewer turns scores into targeted feedback, and the Refiner improves the previously generated k solutions using the Reviewer’s feedback
* This review-refine communication cycle is repeated until either of the two conditions passes, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

### **ii) Classifying Problem Difficulty and Assigning to Coarse or Fine Methods**

**a) Condition 1: Is the Majority Answer of High Quality?**

* To assess problem difficulty, the Solver generates k solutions for the input question and group them by their final answers
* From the largest cluster of solutions, we calculate the average RM score and normalize it by the average score across all solutions, denoted as Savg.
* After normalization, if Savg is greater than 0, Condition 1 is true, meaning the majority answer is already high-quality, and hence no refinement is needed. If it is lower, Condition 1 is false, suggesting even the most frequent answer is poor, so the generated solutions may benefit from refinement.
* using both ORM and PRM separately with same procedure, if any of the RMs gives the majority answer a low score, Condition 1 will be false

**b) Condition 2: Is Reward Models’ Answer Confidence High?**

* check if the RMs are confident in any single answer. Confidence is determined by how concentrated the answer distribution is (i.e., if one cluster stands out)
* concentrated distribution indicates high confidence, while a spread-out distribution shows low confidence. The answer distribution is formed by (1) the frequency of each unique answer and (2) the total RM score of each answer cluster
* each answer cluster is weighed by its aggregated RM score, similar to the approach used in Weighted Self-Consistency
* calculate confidence (denoted as C) using the entropy of the answer clusters. If C ≥ 0.5, Condition 2 is true, meaning RMs are confident in one answer. If C < 0.5, Condition 2 is false, indicating uncertainty and the need for further refinement

**Coarse-to-Fine Decision**

* If either of the conditions is met (the quality of the answer is high or the RMs are confident on an answer), an instance is delegated to the easy — or coarse-grained — method.
* Conversely, if both conditions are not satisfied, the instance is delegated to the hard — or fine-grained–method.

## Fine-Grained Multi-Agent Iterative Refinement

* By categorizing problems as either easy or hard and applying refinement only to hard problem solutions, we mitigate the issue of excessive refinement, addressing “Issue 1”(Excessive refinement)
* we address “Issue 2” by employing a multi-agent setup with three agents: (1) the Solver, which generates the initial solution (2) the Reviewer, which takes PRM scores and a reasoning chain as input, and generates targeted feedback that pinpoints the errors within the chain, and (3) the Refiner, which takes the feedback generated by the Reviewer to refine the previous chain
* to tackle “Issue 3” (insufficient refinement), we iterate the refinement process until an adequate solution is found, for which here are the details of the fine-grained refinement process:

### i) Solver generates k solutions

* MAGICORE begins by generating k solutions to a given problem as done in the previous step
* given a problem that is classified as hard, we can directly re-use the k reasoning chains already generated by the Solver

### ii) Reviewer generates targeted feedback

* For localizing and addressing the errors,PRM is used to evaluate each generated solution and assign local step-wise scores
* these scores are appended to the end of each step and results is sent to the Reviewer.
* goal of the Reviewer is to incorporate step-wise correctness scores from the PRM to generate actionable feedback, as it takes a chain with the PRM scores as input, and is prompted to identify problematic steps that need refinement and possible ways to fix them

### iii) Refiner improves solutions based on targeted feedback

* Refiner then uses the targeted feedback provided by the Reviewer to enhance the reasoning chain initially generated by the Solver.
* By highlighting specific errors in the reasoning chain, the targeted feedback enables the Refiner to address mistakes more effectively, as it clearly identifies which steps are incorrect; this can help models correct errors

### iv) Iterating the refinement process for bidirectional communication

* For some hard instances, one round of refinement may be insufficient; thus, we iterate the refinement process across multiple rounds, creating a communication cycle between the Reviewer and the Refiner, with outputs from the Refiner going back to the Reviewer in the next round.

### v) Final answer selection

* refinement process described above operates on all k chains simultaneously, producing k refined chains in each iteration.
* At the end of each iteration, we use the ORM to assess whether the refined solution has improved based on its global correctness score., which means that by the end of each iteration, we have 2k reasoning chains — k initial and k refined — but retain only the top k based on their global ORM scores
* Here we choose to base the decision on the ORM score because the targeted feedback is generated with PRM’s step-wise scores, so selecting the solution via another scoring model avoids overfitting.
* Finally, the answer is selected using Weighted Self-Consistency over these retained top k chains, at the end of each iteration

* When compared to 120-way SC, our method shows an even greater average improvement of 3.3% on Llama-3–8B and 3.2% on GPT-3.5. Turning to refinement-based methods, we compare against Self-Refine without aggregation, where each iteration operates on a single instance
* On average, MAGICORE shows 17.1% and 13.5% improvements over SR for Llama-3–8B and GPT-3.5

### ii) Analysis

**a) Selective refinement avoids over-correcting and improves overall performance**

* Table below shows Comparison when uniformly adopting aggregation (i.e., Weighted SC) or refinement to all instances.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:416/1*Yz5vfs1B5KZ1JxCgRy-93A.png)
* observed that uniformly applying refinement actually degrades performance; comparing Weighted SC (the “Aggregation-Only” in row 1) to refinement-only (row 2), we see that refining all samples leads to 3.8% and 5.2% drops on MMLU and MATH, respectively, pointing to the over-correction issue
* Conversely, one iteration of our selective refinement (row 3) targets only the challenging instances where the weighted majority vote is unlikely to succeed, resulting in up to 2.6% improvement compared to uniformly applying aggregation (row 1).

**b) PRM-based targeted feedback enables better refinement**

* Table below shows Comparison of different refinement variants in MAGICORE.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:486/1*33z06x5mUU3sNLMympzPaA.png)
* using LLM’s self-refinement (row 1) results in an average drop of 1.5%, indicating that using the LLM for refinement is less effective than using a PRM.
* Overall, MAGICORE’s method of using the step-wise feedback from the PRM is most effective for refinement.

**c) MAGICORE’s two conditions for classifying problem difficulty is more effective than prompting LLMs or any condition alone**

**d) Separating Reviewer and Refiner roles outperforms combining these roles**

* findings show that maintaining separate roles (as in our multi-agent setup) leads to better performance, with the “Joint Roles” configuration resulting in a 0.6% drop in MMLU and a 1.2% decrease inMATH

## Conclusion

* introduce MAGICORE, a method that adaptively allocates more computational resources to more challenging problems and selectively applies refinement where appropriate, i.e., on harder problems.
* MAGICORE addresses three key issues in refinement: excessive refinement on easy examples, the inability of LLMs to detect and correct errors in their reasoning, and insufficient refinement on hard instances
* results across five math datasets and two models show that our coarse-to-fine method consistently outperforms both coarse-grained aggregation and fine-grained refinement alone at any given budget and even outperforms baselines using substantially more computation
