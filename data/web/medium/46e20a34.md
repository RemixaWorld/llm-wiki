I recently submitted a paper at _ACL 2025_ with Adobe Research. The idea was to tweak an LLM’s internal states to make it more faithful to the context. To be clear, that means no matter what the context said, the LLM needed to abide by it.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Even if the context says, “Einstein invented gravity” (Image by author)

> The context is the paragraph of text provided to the LLM along with the question.

Recently, I discovered a paper that analysed the LLM’s internal states in a much more meaningful way while helping them stick to the context, and in the process, discovered exactly how LLMs pull information from the context.

They copy-paste information from the context using what’s called “retrieval heads”. In this blog, we’ll go over the mechanism of how LLMs do that.

First, let’s recap the **attention mechanism**.

## Attention mechanism

I found Jay Allamar’s blog post “The Illustrated Transformer” incredibly useful in understanding the attention mechanism in LLMs. In any case, I’ll explain what you need to know here.

Let’s start with the basic concept of self-attention.

### Self Attention

Let’s start with a simple attention block. The goal of any block in the transformer neural net structure is to take some input and transform it into an output, before integrating it back into the residual stream. At a particular token and layer position:

Press enter or click to view image in full size

A particular token and layer position. The output is a function of the input, but also depends on the inputs at other token positions within that layer. (Image by author)

The tokens inputted into that layer are converted to _q_ , _k_ and _v_ vectors (query, key, and value respectively) using specific weight matrices.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Generating q, k and v vectors for the input vector. (Credits: [The Illustrated Transformer](<https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/>))

Once the _q_ , _k_ and _v_ vectors are generated, we use them to determine the final output vector at that layer. Essentially, the dot product of _q_ of one token _i_ and _k_ of another token _j_ is proportional to how much token _i_ attends to token _j_.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Here, we are computing the attentions at the “it” token to all other tokens at a particular layer (layer 5) to determine the output from layer 5 at that token position of the transformer. (Credit: [The Illustrated Transformer](<https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/>))

Then, to get the output at the _i_ th token position, we do a weighted average of the values (_v_) of all tokens with weights proportional to how much token _i_ attends to those tokens. Thus, the final output at that layer and token position depends not only on that token but all other tokens as well, helping the transformer capture long-range dependencies in language.

But what if, for a given layer, we want to allow the token to attend to different tokens at the same time? For example, a token might attend to the contextual tokens to copy-paste information, while they might attend to the subject of the sentence to capture a different relationship (the noun that the pronoun points to).

To do this, we introduce multi-head attention.

### Multi-head attention

The idea is to have multiple attention blocks in parallel, all capturing different relationships. The final outputs are summed up and added back into the residual stream.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

We have multiple attention blocks (“heads”) with their own weight matrices. Each of them performs the attention operation independently, attending to a different set of tokens. The outputs are summed up at the end. (Image by author)

We have multiple attention heads with their own weight matrices. Each attention head performs the attention operation independently, attending to a different set of tokens. The outputs are summed up at the end.

Every layer and token position has multiple attention heads, which is why an LLM is so complex and difficult to interpret.

But now we claim that there are specific heads relevant to retrieval.

Where are those heads, and how do we find them?

## Finding retrieval heads

For a given token position, we could draw a matrix representing the different attention heads across layers like the diagram below:

A matrix representing the different attention heads across layers. (Image by author)

Now we need to check the specific heads relevant to retrieval. These heads are called the “retrieval heads”.

> Retrieval heads are defined as those heads **that copy-pasted at least 10% of the context tokens**.

But how do we know when the LLM copy-pasted a token from the context?

First, let’s set up the problem.

### Needle in the haystack

Our goal is to set up a needle-in-the-haystack problem.

Suppose we have a context (_x_), a key (_k_), and a query (_q_). The key is a small sentence containing the answer to the query, and the context is irrelevant to the query. We place the ‘key’ sentence somewhere within the context. The LLM needs to find that sentence and answer the question accordingly. Since the sentence containing the answer is somewhere in the middle of the context, it’s analogous to a needle in a haystack.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The key (the sentence containing the answer) is placed within an irrelevant context, analogous to placing a needle in a haystack. (Image by author)

Here’s an example. Let’s say we provide a long paragraph (_x_) about Albert Einstein, and insert a short sentence (_k_) “California is a great travel destination because it’s sunny” into it. Then, we might ask the query (_q_), “Why is California a great travel destination?” and it’s expected to copy the phrase “because it’s sunny” from the context.

Since the query _q_ is related to _k_ , the LLM must copy information from the “needle” (_k_) within the “haystack” (_x_).

### How to tell if text is “copy-pasted”

For us to say that an attention head has copy-pasted a word _w_ from the key, two conditions need to hold:

1. **The output word is a substring of the key sentence.** For example, if the word _w_ is “travel”, and the key _k_ is “California is a great travel destination”, then this condition holds.
2. **The output word attends maximally to the corresponding token in the key.** For instance, the specific attention head in question at the “travel” output token attends to all other tokens with varying weights. If the token with the highest weight is the “travel” token within the key sentence _k_ , then “travel” was probably _copied_ from the key.

Mathematically, the conditions amount to:

The first condition states that _w_ belongs to _k._ The second states that if _j_ is the index of the token with the maximum attention weight, the _j_ th word is identical to the output word _w_.

### What is Retrieval Strength?

Here’s a nice visualisation of the copying process.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The shapes represent individual tokens which are copied from the key. (Image by author)

Let’s assume the key contains 10 tokens. As we see, 2 of the 10 tokens were copied into the answer by the LLM (the red triangle and the green circle). There seems to be one more token generated that’s not present in the key (the blue rectangle).

The Retrieval Strength is defined as the ratio between the number of tokens copied from the key by the total length of the key. In the above image, the Retrieval strength is 2 divided by 10, which is 0.2.

> Retrieval Strength = 2/10 = 0.2

If we take several prompts and compute the retrieval strength for each head averaged across these prompts, we get the mean retrieval strength of each head. By setting some threshold (e.g., 0.1), we can single out the **“retrieval heads”** as **those heads with a retrieval strength greater than the threshold**.

We can come back to our matrix and highlight the newly found retrieval heads.

## Why is this useful?

It turns out that across contexts, keys and queries, the retrieval heads remained more or less the same. This tells us that these heads are somehow involved in copy-pasting text from the context, regardless of the prompt.

If this hypothesis is actually true, and these specific heads are responsible for copy-pasting information from the context, then surely by eliminating these heads, the LLM should no longer be faithful to the context, right?

Well, some scientists tested that out by masking out the retrieval heads they found. And guess what happened?

Press enter or click to view image in full size

We mask out retrieval heads and see if the LLM still copies text from the context. (Image by author)

The LLM started outputting text that was unsupported by the context, and lost its ability to remain faithful to the context.

This shows that retrieval heads really do have something to do with helping LLMs stick to the context. And maybe this observation could help us develop LLMs that always copy text from the context — making them much more trustworthy and predictable.

## Concluding thoughts

We observed:

* Specific attention heads were responsible for copy-pasting text from the context into the answer.
* Masking out these retrieval heads leads to unfaithful responses.

All evidence points to this: _“retrieval heads have something to do with retrieval”_.

Another paper I read improved LLM faithfulness by using the concept of retrieval heads to generate realistic unfaithful responses, and used these in combination with finetuning and contrastive decoding to allow the LLM to generate more faithful responses.

I might go over that in more detail in another blog, but for now, it’s enough to say that the concept of retrieval heads could be applied in exciting ways to improve LLM outputs.

This is also a testament to the fact that the more we try to understand how LLMs work, the more we can do with them. That’s what makes interpretability such a promising field. By understanding more about how LLMs copy-paste text from the context, we not only make sense of their mechanisms of action, but we make them more powerful.
