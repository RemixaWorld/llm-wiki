Just when the world couldn’t get any more chaotic, it’s going to get even worse, as researchers from Anthropic and ETH Zurich have shown how **AI can be used to deanonymize people online pretty effectively**.

Surprisingly, this isn’t some Hollywood-style hackery; the “secret” is just having AI do what AI does best: **profile us, achieving very high precision above 90%.**

> This means the AI can correctly **deanonymize** 9 out of 10 accounts they try.

_And the “funniest” thing of them all?_ This is actually not hard to do at all; **you could do it yourself with off-the-shelf models like ChatGPT**.

## What AIs Are Best At

With all those discussions about AIs being super smart or super dumb, which are mostly irrelevant as **we can’t even agree on what intelligence is in the first place** , we ignore what AI’s real powers are.

In reality, **it shines with pattern matching** (a core component of human intelligence, nonetheless, but not all of it): the ability to look at seemingly unrelated, noisy data and pick up regularities.

For example, realizing that all objects fall to the floor if there’s no force acting upward is picking up a pattern called gravity.

And that in itself justifies AI’s existence as a valuable technology, even when used for dubious cases like the one we are covering today.

### Picking up the commonalities

Saying AIs can process patterns across large amounts of data is an understatement. Nevertheless, some AIs on this planet today have seen trillions upon trillions of words, billions upon billions of images, millions upon millions of videos…

> **They’ve basically “seen it all”.**

And having seen so much data, particularly text, has allowed them to become great at two things that are crucial for today’s topic:

1. **They are extremely proficient at named-entity recognition** , a technique that identifies and classifies entities in unstructured text into predefined categories such as person names, organizations, locations, dates, monetary values, and quantities.
2. **They are excruciatingly nitpicky** when it comes to capturing the subtleties in text, allowing them to “reason” about the differences between two pieces of text in terms of style, format, and semantics, picking up details that humans, be that for lack of attention or simply tiredness, may miss.

And this leads us to today’s research: finding the human behind the account.

### Sharing is showing

A popular way to deanonymize people is to analyze the text they write and identify facts they reveal about themselves that can then be “matched” to a known profile.

For example, if the user @thisisanexample99 is anonymous but says they are born in Des Moines, they are an IT specialist, has two dogs named Sally and Bobby, and went fishing two days ago, all things people who want to remain anonymous still share through their anonymous accounts, it’s highly probable you can find someone on LinkedIn (where profiles are public), **who happens to meet those three facts and just happened to upload a photo phishing two days ago.** The statistical likelihood that they are the same person is very high.

But to be clear, this technique is not something new at all. Just to name a few past examples:

* [Latanya Sweeney’s re-identification result](<https://dataprivacylab.org/projects/identifiability/paper1.pdf>) showed that a large fraction of people can be uniquely identified using a small set of quasi-identifiers (e.g., ZIP code, birth date, gender).
* [Narayanan and Shmatikov’s “Netflix Prize” deanonymization](<https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~shmat/shmat_oak08netflix.pdf>) showed that anonymous Netflix movie ratings could be linked to real people by matching a few known ratings from public IMDb profiles.
* [De Montjoye et al.’s mobility uniqueness](<https://www.nature.com/articles/srep01376?utm_source=chatgpt.com>) showed that just four approximate location–time data points from mobile phone records are enough to uniquely identify 95% of individuals in a large anonymized dataset.

> Hate to break it to you, but your trace is everywhere, and LLMs are just going to make it more apparent.

_But how?_

### ‘I can tell it’s you by the way you talk.’

The process outlined [in the research we are covering today](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.16800>) has four steps: **Extract** , **Search** , **Reason** , and **Calibration**.

As the name suggests, extract takes several snippets of text from an anonymized user and performs extractions, trying to identify facts that the user claims about them.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:642/1*IguHxsE6vJW-edw-2rTSBg.png)

There’s really nothing more to this part of the process beyond having LLMs use their named entity recognition capabilities to structure these texts into several attributes that define what the user is sharing and assemble a user profile.

Next, we have the search phase. This is a little bit more complicated because we use embeddings.

For the sake of brevity, I can’t fully explain what these are here, but the idea is to take all the facts from the anonymous user, build a text summary profile about them (the user profile), and ‘embed’ it, creating a numerical representation (a list of numbers).

_For what?_

**The idea is that this list of numbers represents what the text says, but in numerical form**. These numbers are like attributes that capture the semantic particularities of that text.

If that sounds weird, you’re not alone, but the most intuitive way to think about them is as ‘attributes’. Heavily simplified, say that a number in the list concerns ‘apples’. If the text profile mentions the user loving apple juice, that number will be marked as ‘1’. If not, it’s a 0.

If we could have embeddings that assign an attribute to every single concept in the world, you could have one-hot encodings, which are the simplest form of embedding where each concept is assigned a vector with a ‘1’ on the concept they describe, and ‘0’ for the rest:

![Source: Google](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*5NVfWH7ljEmRPTDYSSnQrg.png)

Instead, the embeddings used here are far more sophisticated, capturing several attributes of a text in one single vector, like representing a ‘king’ as a set of attributes that define it; high on ‘human’ and ‘royalty’, low on ‘feline’.

_But why do this?_ Simple, **to compare**. The more similar two embeddings are, the more semantically similar the underlying text is. ‘king’ and ‘queen’ will be closer to each other than ‘king’ and ‘diplodocus’.

> This exercise is called doing ‘semantic similarity’, a way to compare the semantic nature of concepts using maths, enabling us to do things like search for semantically-similar concepts to the one we’re looking for. Embedding search is used everywhere, from Google Search, Netflix’s recommendation system, and a long tail of other applications.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*Uv202OOFKH9u0XY2.jpg)

Another interesting and easy-to-understand use of embeddings is Google Lens; the fact that you can search images on the Internet is simply because they are converted into embeddings and the top ‘k’ most similar ones are retrieved for you:

![Source: Author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*w1byRN878X6C9XmbPh-ktA.png)

Relevant to our current discussion, **embeddings are not single-word-only and can be applied to entire sequences** ; for example, ‘I like cake’ and ‘I’m enamoured by the taste of this tart’ encode the same meaning (or similar) and will have similar embeddings.

At this point, you’re probably guessing what the point of this is in this discussion:

1. Using the text snippets from an anonymous online user,
2. We combine them into a profile text summary,
3. Encode it into an embedding,
4. And then compare it to a database of embeddings associated with known, publicly available human profiles.

In other words, using embeddings, we get a series of potential candidates for that particular anonymous entity: public profiles that share interests and facts with the anonymous profile, and, amongst them, **potentially the same skin**.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:638/1*pTTqEpjZXT-RqnYFoAq-jw.png)

_But why not simply use LLMs to compare each profile to every other profile?_

Well, **speed and cost mostly**. By defining a similarity threshold (e.g., vectors must be more than 90% similar) to automatically discard any embeddings (candidates) that do not meet it, **we effectively reduce the number of candidates to a smaller pool.**

Simply put, generating embeddings and comparing them using cosine similarity is much cheaper than running an LLM for every comparison.

Moving on to the next step, we have **reason**.

With a much smaller pool of candidates, at this stage, **we’ll literally have LLMs compare two profile summaries** to identify which, if any, candidate is likely to be the person posting as an anonymous user.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:632/1*kumEx41q-HFgKbKz3t_mOQ.png)

At this stage, we should know who the person is. But there’s one last step that's more focused on how we want the system to behave. This last step, **calibration** , which I won’t go into in depth, **essentially controls how confident the system must be before declaring a match**.

In simple terms, you can either:

* Build a system that outputs matches only when the evidence is extremely strong, achieving very high accuracy but missing many real matches.
* Or build a less strict system that finds more real matches but also risks more mistakes.

In statistics, this is known as balancing **precision and recall**(i.e., balancing the system's confidence). _And does this system work?_

Oh boy, it does.

> **LLM-based methods substantially outperform classical baselines** , achieving up to 68% recall at 90% precision compared to near 0% for the best non-LLM method.

In layman’s terms, 90% of the candidates the system proposed were indeed the real users, **and the system correctly deanonymized 68% of the total profiles**.

For a system that works out of the box (researchers did zero training), and with models at the disposal of anyone who can pay for them and is willing to do sketchy things, **it’s scary that the numbers are already so incredibly high**. This, my dear reader, is only going to get worse.

So, _what now?_ Let’s end this by talking about the new normal.

## With AI, New Threats Emerge

It’s simple: **times are changing**.

I’m a techno-optimist by nature, but that doesn’t mean I can’t foresee AI being used for dubious finalities.

> Unrelated to our topic of today, I’ve forced my parents to generate a private password they and only they know in case one of them gets scammed with the voice of the other using an AI. Because if you believe you can tell whether an AI is your mom or not simply because of the voice, **you are wrong**.

In fact, just three days later, a friend of mine was scammed out of $4,000 using the same technique. Those risks are already here.

> I can’t encourage you enough to do this with your close family and friends. Don’t wait until tomorrow; do it today. Protect your loved ones.

Knowing all this, it’s not surprising to see tools like [Deveillance’s Spectre-1](<https://www.deveillance.com/>) emerging that promise to make your private conversations inaudible to AIs.

The product is outrageously expensive in my opinion, so clearly not worth it, **but I predict we will all have one of these anti-AI relatively soon** , once competition forces these first-comers to drop prices.

> **But today’s “deanonymator” hits harder to me because of how simple it is**.

I kid you not, **the technology-savvy barrier to doing this is strikingly low** ; I could build this system in hours if I wanted to, simply because the real complexity is “absorbed” by AIs that are already in the hands of all of us.

_Is this an advertisement against open-source, then?_

Hardly, because one way to look at it is: yes, now millions of people can do this. The other is curtailing this technology under the control of a handful of uber-powerful entities **that will definitely do this** , and with you having no option to prevent it.

AIs can be used to fight the bad AIs, but if nobody but a few have all the powerful AIs, then the rest of us are doomed.

Look, AI progress is coming whether you like it or not. And starting to think about how you can protect yourself from it is something I hope it’s much top-of-mind for you by now.
