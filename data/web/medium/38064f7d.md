## How Models Do What They Do

In short, [DeepSeek has introduced a new model architecture](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.07372>), Engram, that leverages a fast-retrieval method to prevent models from _“simulating retrieval through computation.”_

If that made zero sense, don’t worry, it’s expected. To fully understand what DeepSeek has done here, we first need to take a mechanistic approach, **visualizing the essence by which a Transformer** (i.e., all modern models) **makes predictions** , which is surprisingly beautiful.

### Embeddings, how models “see” the world

An embedding is just a representation of a real-world concept. Interestingly,**these concepts are not expressed in the model in text but in numbers**(AI models run on classical computers at the end of the day, and thus require everything to be numbers).

> A component known as the tokenizer will take your text, chunk it into words and syllables, and translate each chunk into a set of numbers for the model. These chunks are called “tokens”.

**Each embedding** , which is literally a list of numbers, **can be thought of as a compressed list of attributes of the underlying concept**. Embeddings are all around you, not only in AI, but in things like recommendation systems or search systems.

The point of these embeddings is that they situate each concept in an ‘embedding space’ ruled by “the principle of similarity”, where similar real-world concepts will have similar vector embeddings. Bear with me.

In this space, each number represents a dimension, or feature. For instance, if you only have one dimension, you could classify food based on, say, ‘sandwichness.’

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*7bW5xph4sQIHNKt8Xjbtkw.png)

The more dimensions you have, the better you can classify concepts. With three dimensions, you can already clearly separate ‘apple strudel’ and ‘borscht’ from the rest by adding the dimensions of “dessertness” and “liquidness”:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*vHgsw7zWFg1Dmvqb_mKfkQ.png)

The point is that, in the eyes of an AI, a system that lacks “tacit knowledge” because it can’t experience the world, ‘pizza’ and ‘hot dog’ are more similar to each other than they are to ‘borscht’, and also that they aren’t a dessert.

> This similarity principle is how AIs differentiate stuff; it’s all based on relativity. It’s not that ‘pizza’ is ‘x’, but ‘pizza’ is similar to this stuff and dissimilar to that other stuff.

Therefore, when the user asks for a suggestion on a dessert, ‘apple strudel’ is much more likely to emerge as the prediction because it has the ‘dessertness’ attribute much stronger than the rest of the options.

This is very important to understand because it subtly explains how predictions are actually made (this will matter later).

### The knowledge gatherer

Now that we know the basic semantic unit of AI models, the embedding, we can understand what models like ChatGPT do with these embeddings.

And the answer is vector transformations.

If we look back at the previous image, ‘hot dog’ and ‘shawarma’ have a zero on ‘liquidness’ and ‘dessertness’, and different values of ‘sandwichness’, potentially being represented in the model’s embedding space as [0,3,0] and [0,4,0].

So if the user asks the model, _“Give me a food that is similar to a hot dog”,_ considering that the model in this toy example doesn’t know about things like hamburgers and can just “play” with the items in the image above, it should predict “shawarma”.

The key element here is that, as both items are expressed as vectors, the model can mathematically ‘transition’ from one to the other.

In other words, if the model needs to retrieve the closest item to the one the user has asked for (‘hot dog’), it can easily identify ‘shawarma’ as the closest, as ‘apple strudel’ and ‘borscht’ are basically orthogonal. Moreover, ‘pizza’ and ‘salad’ in this particular three-dimensional space would be both very close to [0,0,0] as they don’t meet any of the three attributes.

_And what does all this mean?_ **This vector compositionality is literally what allows the model to make predictions, as it lets it create new concepts by combining others.**

For example, adding the vector embedding for ‘red’ to ‘pirate’ makes it a ‘red pirate’. Or if you have ‘king’ and you subtract ‘man’ and add ‘woman’, you get ‘queen’.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:600/1*BSN-n2g3m3lqSWDlkI3zhA@2x.jpeg)

Which is to say, the moment each concept is represented as a list of attributes, models can modify the attributes of the object with these vector embeddings to create new versions of that concept or create entirely new ones that the model knows are semantically similar because they have similar vectors.

![Source: Author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*UMemlKrW-wtsYEPxvPD1tw.png)

> That is, whenever ChatGPT does a prediction and returns a word to you, it has done precisely what I have described: **a set of updates on the attributes of a vector embedding that “becomes” the new word**.

_But what does ChatGPT need to make a prediction? How does the model know what transformations to execute?_ Two things, **context** and **memory**.

I will spare you repeating myself because I recently explained this in way more detail [here](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/looped-transformers-chinas-surprise-for-2026-39737d2d0396>), but the point is that Transformers, **the underlying architecture behind ChatGPT or Gemini,** **behaves like a “knowledge gatherer.”**

It has two mechanisms: **one looks at the sequence** (to gather context), **and the other at the model’s knowledge**(to gather memory). Basically, the model asks itself two questions:

1. _“What information does the user’s sequence tell me?”_
2.  _“What internal knowledge do I possess that could help?”_

These “questions” are represented as ‘layers’: an attention layer for context and an MLP layer for model knowledge, where each two-question block is known as a ‘Transformer block’.

The vector we are transforming (it’s always the last predicted token, or the last token in the sequence you give it) **passes through this sequence of blocks and is progressively updated with new information** , both at the sequence level (what does my context say?) and from the model’s knowledge (have I ever seen something similar?)

> Using our pirate example, in “The red pirate”, ‘pirate’ will attend to ‘red’ to absorb the red attributed and thus become ‘red pirate’.

Intuitively, you can think of this process as molding shapes with clay.

* The model gets asked, “ _What’s another popular shape besides a cube?_ ”
* To predict the next word, the model now has the word ‘cube’ to work with. Thus, you can imagine it as starting with a clay cube.
* Over several layers, the model starts giving this clay figure a new shape, not necessarily knowing what that shape will be, **only adding information based on what the user asks and what the model knows about shapes**. Figuratively, you can imagine the model smoothening the edges to get a more round shape.
* This “new shape”, **a sphere, is semantically similar to what it had previously** , a cube, so it’s good enough for what the user asked for. Therefore, the word ‘sphere’ comes out.

![Source: Author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*SJT3NaUsH7Fq8L-dklEiEg.png)

This is done dozens of times for the larger models (today's large language models are mostly a concatenation of Transformer blocks), “giving the model time” and enough compute to perform the necessary transformations on the vector embedding so that it becomes the correct one.

![Source: Author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*40g9pGbldk3GSgGZ.jpeg)

> This is one of the core reasons why larger models are better: they allow for more transformations, aka more “knowledge gathering”.

Circling back to our original example, _“Give me a food that is similar to a hot dog”,_ the model received the word ‘hot dog’ and was tasked with finding the closest thing. As this was a toy example, the model just requires a very small transformation to get to ‘shawarma’ (just subtract a little bit of ‘sandwichness’) to get a ‘hot dog’.

Bottom line, models figure out what to predict next by taking the vector of the last word in the sequence and updating it using information from both the previous words in the sequence and their internal knowledge. Making the next prediction based on context and memory.

As you may guess from your interactions with ChatGPT, this works amazingly well, **but it’s surprisingly inefficient**.

And to understand why we need to take a look at a precursor to the Transformer: **n-gram models** , which not only explain why Transformers are inefficient but are also critical to today’s topic.

### n-gram models, local but effective

An n-gram model is a type of natural language model that uses the ‘n’ previous words to predict the next one.

* A 2-gram model will just use the previous word to predict the next one
* A 3-gram model will just look at the previous two words to predict the next one

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*YhXwmtHh5A6dnlFs.png)

N-grams store these combinations and their frequencies in tables they learn during training, and simply retrieve them dynamically.

If this sounds moderately familiar, yes, Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT do something similar: **they behave like an ‘infinity-gram’, looking at all previous words to make a new prediction** , and they compute probabilities dynamically instead of retrieving them from a table.

That is:

* **n-grams are local** (they can only consider combinations of a few words), but can retrieve those combinations immediately without explicit computation,
* Instead, **LLMs are global** (they can consider a potentially infinite number of words for a prediction), and they are forced to compute even the simplest of word relationships.

_But why is this important?_ Let’s look at a simple example. The words “Alexander the Great” go clearly together to form a more complex concept referring to the Macedonian king.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*UK0MXGUa4V2lqK4tMSzOBw.png)

If you’re using a 3-gram model, you can immediately retrieve the embedding that summarises this entire concept from the table, **while a Transformer has to “figure out” the concept in real-time instead of retrieving it from a pre-computed state**.

In other words,**the Transformer has to perform the knowledge-gathering exercise we have just described above every single time** , despite the fact that every time these three words are together, it’s always going to refer to the same entity, the legendary figure Alexander the Great.

> As the DeepSeek researchers perfectly summarise it, Transformer LLMs, aka all LLMs, _“inefficiently simulate retrieval through computation.”_

Which is to say, they reach the outcome shown in the above image, **but have to perform that exercise every time instead of directly retrieving it from memory** , which begs the question: _why don’t we blend both?_

And this, my dear reader, is exactly what DeepSeek has done.

## Engram models, too good not to stay

As I was saying, while Transformers are knowledge gatherers and can retrieve information from their memory, **they can’t retrieve it instantly, and instead have to recompute the ‘Alexander the Great’ concept again and again every time** , despite being a clearly retrievable (or memorizable) relationship.

This is as if you had to reason Paris is the capital of France every single time to remember it, instead of simply, well, retrieving it from memory. LLMs can remember it, but have to go through the “process of remembering” (“Paris sounds like a French word and is the place where the Eiffel Tower is, so it must be France’s capital” instead of your brain going “Paris -> France”).

And Engrams come to fix that.

### Adding retrieval

Ironically, what DeepSeek proposes isn’t that revolutionary; it’s mostly common sense and something that has been explored in some form earlier, [with examples like n-grammers](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.06366>).

> N-grammers did suggest linking n-grams with Transformers, but work at different abstraction layers (n-grammers work with latent representations, with the embeddings directly), which is not what Engram does, as you’re about to see.

The idea is to add an ‘Engram’ module to some Transformer blocks. Under the hood, it’s just an n-gram module that takes in words in the input sequence as pairs and triples and retrieves them from the n-gram tables.

The point is what we mentioned earlier: retrieving known facts or concepts from memory without the need to recompute them on the fly. If we know ‘Alexander the Great’ will be identified as a single entity by the model anyway,_why not simply store that relationship and retrieve it immediately?_

![Engram’s architecture. Source: DeepSeek](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*INSJqkO1-SNXUUPtIdbHtg.png)

Basically, just like humans can immediately retrieve ‘precomputed’ facts, Engram can decide at runtime whether a fact can be retrieved via computation or simply fetched from memory.

> Engram includes a ‘gating’ mechanism that checks whether the retrieved memory is useful. If the engram module retrieves “apple” as in the fruit and we are talking about “Apple” the company, the memory is discarded, just like humans can retrieve the wrong memories but acknowledge and correct the mistake.

The results are impressive,**unsurprisingly given how logical this proposal is** , with the Engram model beating the standard Transformer across almost every metric despite being the same size.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*IFo27D6ZKgyMUMxmRqhaEg.png)

However, this is nonetheless a striking result, **as it’s rare to see an architectural change so overwhelmingly better than the previous approach.**

But the Engram offers a set of particularities that make it even more unique, going beyond the software itself.

### Long, fast, and global

The first relevant result that can’t be understated is the superiority of the Engram architecture for long sequences. In layman’s terms, **the Engram works better with long sequences,** meaning it can pick up global patterns better.

The reason is that, as the model can now retrieve local dependencies directly from memory (i.e., not having to process ‘Alexander the Great’ as a single entity and simply retrieve it),**it can allocate its compute to more global patterns required to process longer sequences**.

Put simply, the sequence-level operator, the attention mechanism (the question ‘what is my context?’) that we saw earlier,**can allocate compute more wisely toward more interesting global patterns instead of trying to recall every single local pattern** , like names, using that same computation.

**This is not only pretty intuitive but also observable**. Using two similarity methods, LogitLens (left) and CKA maps (right and center), we can see that earlier layers in the Engram model are very similar to deeper layers in the standard Transformer.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*zxNOZZlcgoBoFYzooDXing.png)

_But what does that mean?_

Recall that these layers are just knowledge-gathering steps that add information both at the sequence and from the model’s knowledge to the embedding we are “shaping” to become the new prediction, with each layer contributing information.

Interestingly, the earlier layers in the Engram model are quite similar to deeper layers in the standard Transformer, which is to say:**the Engram model gets to the prediction faster** , which, in AI terms, is the closest thing we can use to describe a model as being _“smarter per unit of size”_ than the other.

> Just like smarter humans get to answer faster, so does Engram.

But Engram has perhaps an even more interesting facet: **its effect on the underlying hardware.**

### The emergence of fast storage

In AI, we always talk about compute, but memory is just as important. Or more.

As with any other computer in the Von Neumann architecture, computation and memory are separated. Thus, **for things to be computed, they have to be brought in and out of memory** , which takes time.

Therefore, **memory bandwidth, or speed, becomes absolutely essential to performance**. For that reason, even though AI systems have four different memory layers (on-chip memory (SRAM), GPU memory (HBM), CPU memory, flash storage, and disk), **AI has mostly been SRAM and HBM-only** , meaning the rest of the memory layers were completely avoided unless strictly necessary due to their slowness.

> Memory layers go from smaller to larger, and from faster to slower; SRAM the fastest but smallest capacity, disk slowest but biggest capacity. As memory speed is the bottleneck, AI has been mostly SRAM/DRAM-only.

This has caused a massive demand for HBM memory, largely explaining why the three companies capable of building HBM, Sk Hynix, Samsung, and Micron, are skyrocketing in stock price.

Without getting into too much detail, the cache eventually spills into CPU memory, which is still DRAM (but slower, mainly LPDDR instead of HBM, which explains why NVIDIA has decided to start quoting memory specs using the term “fast memory” that includes both GPU and CPU memory (the latter also known as ‘host memory’).

However, the two other memory types, known as storage, **are still mostly ignored because they are too slow** (flash memory is sometimes used to hold parts of the cache, too, in very tight settings).

But the point here is that **Engram might have made all other memory tiers beyond HBM much more attractive and relevant**. Because n-gram access follows a Zipfian distribution shown below (meaning some n-grams like ‘Alexander the Great’ are more accessed than others), **rarely accessed n-grams can be stored in fast storage without a severe penalty** (only >3% overhead).

![A Zipfian distribution](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:576/1*LUcFCWNT-vMWFqtDeNmtzg@2x.jpeg)

> A question remains on practicality. If we know memory speed is the bottleneck, **is moving the workload more into the memory domain a good thing?**
>
> The good news is that this retrieval is deterministic (we know what words to retrieve from memory from the get-go), so we can (and we do) overlap this retrieval with the first parts of the model’s computation. That is, we pre-fetch the n-grams while the model is computing other stuff, explaining why the fast storage penalty is so small.

_And what does all this mean?_

Simple, if Engram becomes a common architectural design for LLMs, **which I believe it will** , **fast storage (flash memory) has just become instrumental to progress** , making yet another memory layer, and the companies like produce it, fully-fledged “AI companies”.

_Guess who had the best stock performance in 2025?_ **That company was Sandisk, a top flash producer.**

And DeepSeek’s research only makes Sandisk and other flash companies' outlook look even better, so I wouldn’t be willing to bet against them this year either (even if I believe this particular stock might be overextending).

## Research that just makes sense

In AI, **a science so experimental that some people don’t consider it as such** , we have grown accustomed to seeing things working and just not knowing why.

Therefore, it’s very refreshing to see research that comes across as “obvious” after reading the first few pages.

AIs today are still brute-forcing their way forward, doing things correctly but inefficiently.

> **They are imitating our brains' outputs, but without the elegant demeanor and efficiency that characterizes brain processes**.

Thus, when you see research that calls this out and says, ‘look, some things can be stored and avoided from being recomputed every single time,’ you can’t help but say, ‘ _how has this not been a thing until now?_ ’

But what worries me the most is that, once again, the wake-up call comes from China. **And that’s like the 20th time I’ve written those precise words in the last year.**

Efficiency-wise, China is king.

So let me ask you once again: _for how long are we going to pretend money, compute, and energy are infinite, Western folk?_
