There’s only so much we can do to make models more accurate and efficient, as we push boundaries of what’s possible with neural networks. Architectural changes, multi-layer fusion and model compilation are key components, but are seldom enough to create a model small and accurate enough to deploy on edge.

Broadly speaking, there are three common approaches followed by researchers to make models smaller while retaining accuracy —

* **Model Quantization** — reduces the memory footprint and computational requirements of neural networks by representing model weights with lower precision, such as converting 16-bit float to 8-bit integers.
* **Model Pruning** — removes less important neurons or weights from a trained neural network to simplify architecture without significantly affecting performance.
* **Knowledge Distillation**(aka **Teacher Student Training**) —a smaller, more efficient model (aka **student**) is trained to mimic the soft predictions (aka knowledge transfer) of a larger, more complex model (aka **teacher**). Soft-labels allow the student model to generalize well as soft-labels represent a higher level of abstraction and understanding of similarity across different categories instead of peaky one-hot-encoded representation as in the larger model.

In this article, we’ll be diving into what model quantization is, the two techniques for quantization, and how to implement them using PyTorch.

## So What Is Quantization?

Representative mapping from FP32 to INT8 [[Source](<https://medium.com/sharechat-techbyte/neural-network-compression-using-quantization-328d22e8855d>)]

Quantization is one of the more powerful, and easily applied techniques for optimizing neural networks. It works by reducing the computational and memory requirements of neural networks by converting model data — both network parameters and activations — from high-precision floating-point numbers (typically 16-bit) to lower-precision representations (typically 8-bit int). The benefits of doing this is multi-fold, namely:

* GPUs employ the faster and cheaper 8-bit cores ([Tensor Cores](<https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/tensor-cores/>) for Nvidia GPUs) to compute convolution and matrix-multiplication operations, effectively yielding more throughput.
* Some layers are bandwidth-bound (memory-limited), meaning their implementation spends most of its time reading and writing data, and therefore reducing their computation time does not reduce their overall runtime. Bandwidth-bound layers benefit most from reduced bandwidth requirements
* A reduced memory footprint means that the model requires less storage space, parameter updates are smaller, cache utilization is higher, etc.
* Finally, moving data from memory to computing elements takes time and energy. Reducing precision from 16-bit to 8-bit results in 2x data reduction, which saves power consumption.

There are multiple methods to map a higher precision number to a lower precision number (eg. zero point quantization, absolute maximum (_absmax_) quantization, etc), however, we won’t be diving into them here. ## Approaches for Quantization

Image by Author

There are two approaches taken to quantize models:

### 1\. Post Training Quantization (PTQ)

Performed after a model is fully trained, PTQ is a technique that converts a model from higher precision to lower precision without retraining, using a small calibration dataset to determine optimal quantization parameters . It involves collecting statistics about model activations and calculating appropriate quantization parameters to minimize the difference between floating-point and quantized representations.

PTQ is more resource efficient, faster to implement and deploy, and suitable for scenarios where retraining is not feasible. The models, however, are less accurate, and require careful calibration and parameter tuning, so are mostly suited for quick prototyping and not deployment.

There are two approaches to handle this:

### **1.1) Dynamic Post Training Quantization**

This involves fine-tuning the activation ranges on the fly during inference, based on the data distribution fed to the model at runtime.

### **1.2) Static Post Training Quantization**

In this approach, an additional calibration step is involved, wherein a representative dataset is used to estimate the range of activations using the variations in the dataset. This estimation happens in full precision to minimize errors, after which the activations are then scaled down to lower precision data types.

### 2\. Quantization Aware Training (QAT)

QAT is an approach where quantization effects are simulated during the model training process by introducing “**fake quantization** ” operations that mimic the impact of lower precision on weights and activations. In essence, training is done with quantization constraints. The model learns to adjust to quantization noise during training using techniques like the __**_Straight-Through Estimator (STE)_** for gradient computation , allowing it to better adapt to the reduced precision environment.

QAT generally achieves better accuracy as the model can adapt to quantization effects during training itself, hence is more suitable for models that are sensitive to quantization errors. This also means that they require more computational resources and time due to the need for retraining, and are generally more complex implementation-wise.

## How Quantization Aware Training Works?

Press enter or click to view image in full size

How QAT works in PyTorch [[Source](<https://pytorch.org/blog/quantization-aware-training/>)]

The benefits of QAT are clear from the previous section — unlike PTQ, which applies quantization after training, QAT inserts “fake quantization” modules during training. This means the model “sees” quantization noise and learns to compensate for it, resulting in a quantized model that closely matches the accuracy of its full-precision counterpart. The QAT workflow looks as follows:

1. **Preparation** — Replace sensitive layers (eg. Conv, Linear, Activations) with wrappers that simulate quantization. In PyTorch, this is done using `prepare_qat` or `prepare_qat_fx` .
2. **Training** — During each forward pass, weights and activations are “fake quantized” — rounded and clamped as they would be in INT8/INT4. The backward pass uses a STE so gradients flow as if quantization were the identity function.
3. **Conversion** — After training, swap fake quantization modules for real quantized kernels using `convert` or `convert_fx`. The model is now ready for efficient `int8 / int4` inference.

### The Mathematics Behind Fake Quantization

Naive Quantization showcasing rounding and clipping [[Source](<https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/>)]

Without diving too deep into the different techniques, here’s a simplified breakdown of the quantization process.

Let `x_float` be a real-valued activation. Uniform affine quantization uses:

``` scale = (x_max – x_min) / (q_max – q_min)zeroPt = round(q_min – x_min / scale)x_q = clamp( round(x_float / scale) + zeroPt, q_min, q_max )x_deq = (x_q – zeroPt) * scale ```

During QAT, the **fake quantization** operation is:

``` x_fake = (round(x_float/scale)+zeroPt – zeroPt) * scale ```

So `x_fake` is still float, but now confined to the same discrete lattice an `int8` tensor would occupy.

### Gradient Flow — Straight-Through Estimator

_QAT fake-quantization operators in the training forward-pass_ (**L**)_and backward-pass_ (**R**) [[Source](<https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/>)]

Rounding is non-differentiable, so PyTorch uses:

``` dL/dx_float ≈ dL/dx_fake ```

In backward — literally treating the fake-quant module as the identity for gradients, letting the optimizer tweak upstream weights to counteract quantization noise.

The result — learned weights that naturally land near integer centers, plus tuned `scale`/`zeroPt` that minimize total reconstruction error

## Hands-On Implementation

PyTorch provides three different modes of quantization:

### 1\. Eager Mode Quantization

This is a beta feature. The user needs to do fusion and specify where quantization and de-quantization happens manually. Also, this only supports modules and not functionals.

The following snippet shows every step from model definition to QAT prep, to finally `int8` conversion.

```python import os, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.optim as optim# 1. Model definition with QuantStub/DeQuantStubclass QATCNN(nn.Module): def __init__(self): super().__init__() self.quant = torch.quantization.QuantStub() self.conv1 = nn.Conv2d(1, 16, 3, padding=1) self.relu1 = nn.ReLU() self.pool = nn.MaxPool2d(2) self.conv2 = nn.Conv2d(16, 32, 3, padding=1) self.relu2 = nn.ReLU() self.fc = nn.Linear(32*14*14, 10) self.dequant = torch.quantization.DeQuantStub() def forward(self, x): x = self.quant(x) x = self.pool(self.relu1(self.conv1(x))) x = self.relu2(self.conv2(x)) x = x.flatten(1) x = self.fc(x) return self.dequant(x)# 2. QAT preparationmodel = QATCNN()model.qconfig = torch.quantization.get_default_qat_qconfig('fbgemm')torch.quantization.prepare_qat(model, inplace=True)# 3. Tiny training loopopt = optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=1e-2)crit = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()for _ in range(3): inp = torch.randn(16,1,28,28) tgt = torch.randint(0,10,(16,)) opt.zero_grad(); crit(model(inp), tgt).backward(); opt.step()# 4. Convert to real int8model.eval()int8_model = torch.quantization.convert(model)# 5. Storage benefittorch.save(model.state_dict(), "fp32.pth")torch.save(int8_model.state_dict(), "int8.pth")mb = lambda p: os.path.getsize(p)/1e6print(f"FP32: {mb('fp32.pth'):.2f} MB vs INT8: {mb('int8.pth'):.2f} MB") ```

**Expected output** : ~4× size drop and ≤1 % accuracy loss on MNIST-like data.

**Why it works:** `torch.quantization.prepare_qat` recursively wraps each eligible layer with `FakeQuantize` modules, and the default `FBGEMM` qconfig selects per-tensor weight observers and per-channel activation observers ideal for server/edge CPUs.

### 2\. FX Graph Mode Quantization

This is an automated quantization workflow in PyTorch, currently in maintenance mode. It enhances Eager Mode Quantization by supporting functionals and automating the quantization process, although users may need to refactor their models to ensure compatibility.

A major caveat is that it may not work with arbitrary models due to potential issues with symbolic traceability, which also breeds the need to be familiar with `torch.fx`. An example snippet using this is:

```python import torch, torchvision.models as modelsfrom torch.ao.quantization import get_default_qat_qconfig_mappingfrom torch.ao.quantization import prepare_qat_fx, convert_fxmodel = models.resnet18(weights=None) # or pretrained=Truemodel.train()# 1-liner qconfig mappingqmap = get_default_qat_qconfig_mapping("fbgemm")# Graph rewritemodel_prepared = prepare_qat_fx(model, qmap)# Fine-tune for a few epochsmodel_prepared.eval()int8_resnet = convert_fx(model_prepared) ```

FX mode operates at the **graph level** : operators like `conv2d`, `batch_norm` and `relu` are fused automatically, producing leaner kernels and better latency on CPU.

### 3\. PyTorch 2 Export Quantization

If you plan to ship an exported program to a C++ runtime, PT2E (PyTorch 2 Export) is for you. It is the new full graph mode quantization workflow, released in PyTorch 2.1 and is built for models captured by `torch.export`. The full flow can be implemented only in a few lines as:

```python import torchfrom torch import nnfrom torch._export import capture_pre_autograd_graphfrom torch.ao.quantization.quantize_pt2e import ( prepare_qat_pt2e, convert_pt2e)from torch.ao.quantization.quantizer.xnnpack_quantizer import ( XNNPACKQuantizer, get_symmetric_quantization_config)class Tiny(nn.Module): def __init__(self): super().__init__(); self.fc=nn.Linear(8,4) def forward(self,x): return self.fc(x)ex_in = (torch.randn(2,8),)exported = torch.export.export_for_training(Tiny(), ex_in).module()quantizer = XNNPACKQuantizer().set_global(get_symmetric_quantization_config())qat_mod = prepare_qat_pt2e(exported, quantizer)# Fine-tune the model ...int8_mod = convert_pt2e(qat_mod)torch.ao.quantization.move_exported_model_to_eval(int8_mod) ```

The resulting graph is ready for `torch::deploy` or ahead-of-time (AOT) compilation into mobile engines.

### Bonus — Large-Language-Model Int4/Int8 Hybrid Demo

While not really an explicit API, `torchao`/`torchtune` ships prototype quantizers for extreme compression as well:

```python import torchfrom torchtune.models.llama3 import llama3from torchao.quantization.prototype.qat import Int8DynActInt4WeightQATQuantizermodel = llama3(vocab_size=4096, num_layers=16, num_heads=16, num_kv_heads=4, embed_dim=2048, max_seq_len=2048).cuda()qat_quant = Int8DynActInt4WeightQATQuantizer()model = qat_quant.prepare(model).train()# ––– Kathy-like micro-fine-tune –––optim = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), 1e-4)lossf = torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss()for _ in range(100): ids = torch.randint(0,4096,(2,128)).cuda() label = torch.randint(0,4096,(2,128)).cuda() loss = lossf(model(ids), label) optim.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optim.step()model_quant = qat_quant.convert(model)torch.save(model_quant.state_dict(),"llama3_int4int8.pth") ```

Model activations run `int8`, weights run `int4`, giving > 2× speed-up and ~60 % memory cut on a single A100 GPU with < 0.8 pp drop in perplexity.

## Best Practices

To maximize weight savings of your model with minimal accuracy give-away, keep these pointers in mind:

* **Warm-up with PTQ first** — If PTQ only loses <2 %, a short QAT fine-tune (5–10 epochs) is usually enough.
* **Perform Ablation** — perform ablation analysis to figure out which layers are more sensitive to quantization. If quantizing a layer leads to a large draw-down in performance, consider keeping the weights as is.
* **Fuse Ops Early —** `Conv + BN + ReLU` fusion stabilizes observer ranges and measurably bumps accuracy.
* **Freeze Batch Normalization Stats** —**** After a few epochs, call `torch.ao.quantization.disable_observer` then `freeze_bn_stats`. This prevents oscillating ranges.
* **Monitor Histograms —** Use `torch.ao.quantization.get_observer_state_dict()` or [**Netron**](<https://netron.app/>) to spot outliers.
* **Tune Learning-Rate Schedule —** Small LR (≤ 1e-3) avoids overshooting when STE approximations kick in.
* **Per-Channel Weight Quantization** —This halves error versus per-tensor and is used as a default for `conv`.
* **Mix Precision** – keep first and last layers in `**fp16**` for safety if accuracy still dips.
* **Hardware Checks** – `FBGEMM` for x86, `QNNPACK/XNNPACK` for ARM; choose matching qconfig.

## Summary

Model deployment often requires a multi-faceted approach — coming up with an accurate model is often the easy part; deployment at scale is where it gets tough. If your model can’t afford the accuracy hit of naive PTQ, QAT is the solution. That said, you do need to keep a lot of factors in mind for a successful deployment — including the target platform and the operations supported by it. PyTorch, with its mature QAT toolchain, comes in really handy, making it pretty easy to quantize any model — from a toy CNN, all the way up to a billion-parameter language model.
