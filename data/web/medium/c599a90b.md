AI research has for the longest time been about building new models that performed better than earlier models.

But now, we are entering a new era. The era of _interpretability research_.

AI safety is currently a global concern. Mechanistic interpretability is growing to become a hugely important area of research, because the more we understand how AI works, the more we can use it for the betterment of our society.

In this blog, I cover 4 areas of mechanistic interpretability research that have caught on in the last few years.

## 1\. Activation Patching

Activation patching is a method used to identify which activations are responsible for a specific behaviour.

Let’s say an LLM is given the question:

> “What’s 2+2?”

We could corrupt the entire input hidden state representation first. We’d expect an incorrect output due to the corruption, like:

> “5er”

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Corrupting the first layer’s hidden state leads to the incorrect output. (Image by author)

Then, we could individually bring back uncorrupted activations at different positions of the LLM.

For example, we might bring back the uncorrupted activation at the 12th layer and 5th token position, and keep everything else corrupted.

If the correct output comes back after bringing back the uncorrupted activation at that specific position, then the activation must have something to do with the behaviour.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Restoring a specific hidden state at the lth layer and ith token position restores the original output. (Image by author)

Thus, activation patching can be used to figure out _causal effects_ – for eg, in the above case, we might be able to determine which parts of the LLM are responsible for accurate mathematical calculations.

## 2\. Activation steering

Activation steering is a method that is often used to make an LLM behave in a specific “desired” way. For example, Anthropic recently released their “Golden Gate Claude” model — an LLM that somehow manages to mention the Golden Gate Bridge in its responses no matter what the question is.

The idea is to “steer” the internal states along a specific direction representing _the_ _Golden Gate Bridge_ , hoping that the LLM becomes “obsessed” with _the_ _Golden Gate Bridge (_ and can’t stop talking about it).

I used this idea in my internship at Adobe Research last summer, enabling me to increase an LLM’s contextual alignment by up to 20%. So technically, if it weren’t for activation steering, I might not have been able to convert my internship into a full-time offer.

> **Contextual alignment** is the act of enabling LLMs to remain faithful to a provided contextual paragraph. If the LLM answers a question with an answer that is supported within the context provided in the question, the LLM is considered to be “contextually aligned”.

## 3\. Finding neurons in a haystack (superposition)

This point is quite similar to point 2, though there are some key differences that make them fairly separate concepts.

In simple terms, this method is used to figure out _which neurons of the transformer are related to, say, the Golden Gate Bridge._

This means those same neurons light up every time the LLM talks about the Golden Gate Bridge.

It also means that by increasing the intensity of those neurons, we could get the LLM to become more “obsessed” with the golden gate bridge, just like in point 2.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Boosting a specific neuron makes the LLM always talk about the Golden Gate Bridge. (Image by author)

So how is this related to activation steering?

Boosting a specific neuron is equivalent to adding a standard basis vector to the activation vector.

A standard basis vector is just a vector where all the components are zero except for one of them.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

A standard basis vector. (Image by author)

Adding this vector to the activation vector (scaled by some multiplier α) will effectively lead to boosting the golden gate neuron.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Boosting a neuron is equivalent to adding a steering vector. (Image by author)

> **Conclusion:
> ** Boosting a neuron in this way is equivalent to steering the model using a standard basis vector.

In regular activation steering, we could add any arbitrary vector. The vector could involve multiple neurons, or only one neuron.

If a specific neuron is involved in the behaviour, then that neuron is _monosemantic._ However, if a group of neurons collectively are related to the behaviour, those neurons are _polysemantic._

## 4\. Rank-One Model Editing

This method involves attributing a behaviour to a specific component of the transformer (for eg, a specific MLP or Attention layer). For example, we might state that facts are stored in a specific MLP in the model.

We could then show how these facts can be edited by changing those specific MLP weights in the model.
