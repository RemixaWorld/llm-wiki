Vanishing and exploding gradients are much less of a problem today than they used to be. But they can still be a problem. Having the techniques to directly investigate and troubleshoot these issues speeds up development and makes it easier to experiment with novel activation functions.

Progress in deep neural networks took off around 2010 when almost everyone switched to a simple, consistent rule: use the ReLU activation function with He-normal weight initialization, and only use sigmoid in the last layer. Much of the experimentation with activation functions reduced to the search for subtle variations of the ReLU activation function.

More recently, we’ve seen an explosion in the kinds of non-linearities that are being experimented with. Not just for standard activation functions, but also for the attention mechanism of the Transformer network. ELU, GELU, Swish, Mish, ReGLU, GEGLU, SwiGLU, just to name a few.

And problems of vanishing and exploding gradients didn’t entirely go away even when using ReLU. They are still a common problem for sequence models.

In this article, I will explain the four causes of vanishing and exploding gradients. I will show you how to directly measure and visualize their effect. And I will show you techniques for troubleshooting and resolving them.

By the end of this article, you will no longer be afraid of vanishing or exploding gradients. You will be able to confidently use any non-linear activation function. You will even be able to use the old-fashioned sigmoid and tanh activation functions within the internal layers of your deep network.

## Vanishing Gradients

Vanishing gradients were a problem in the past when most models used the _logistic sigmoid_ or _hyperbolic tangent_ (_tanh(x)_) as the activation function. The use of sigmoid was originally inspired by observations of biological neurons. It worked acceptably for small numbers of layers (eg, 1 hidden layer), but progress for many hidden layers stalled until everyone switched to the ReLU activation function.

At first glance, the sigmoid function seems perfect for classification problems. It behaves like a smoothed-out switch — large negative or positive values become clear zeros or ones. It also has a linear region in the middle, which is useful for classification tasks to indicate uncertainty and for providing a gradient for learning.

However, [Glorot & Bengio (2010)](<https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/glorot10a.html>), who introduced the famous Glorot Initialization scheme, explained that there are three problems when using sigmoid and similar activation functions in deep networks:

* Non-unit variance and network depth
* Saturation
* Non-zero mean

### Non-unit variance and network depth

When dealing with gradients, you need to be aware that they are predominantly balanced distributions with a mean at zero. Thus, to identify the general _scale_ of the gradients, it is common to use metrics like stddev, variance, or the L2-norm. We also initialize weights with mean zero, and they tend to stay that way during training.

Example distributions of weights and gradients

The key point of the Glorot & Bengio paper was to identify a rule about the scale of backprop gradients in relation to the scale of initialized weights, and how network depth (the number of layers) affects those scales.

During the execution of the backprop algorithm, each layer undergoes two stages of calculations. The first is to calculate the backprop gradients received from the subsequent layer, technically computing the change in the loss (_J_) w.r.t to the pre-activation output (_Z_) of the target layer. The second stage computes the loss w.r.t to the weights and other model parameters. So, Glorot & Bengio are referring to the first calculation.

pre-Glorot initialization

It turns out that this gives a very simple and consistent result for the scale of the initialized weights (assuming all layers are the same size):

Scale of pre-Glorot initialized weights

Now, refer back to the backprop scale equation above. We can ignore the scale of the dataset, _Var[X]_ , because it is fixed. The scale of the layers, from first hidden layer to output layer, is then given as:

Power law: the scale of backprop gradients changes from first layer to last according to powers of the base variance

For a network with 5 layers, the first layer gets a variance of only 0.004 while the last layer gets 0.333.

Glorot & Bengio solved this by proposing a slight modification to the uniform initialization, by averaging across the fan-in and fan-out, and normalizing the range:

Glorot initialization

This results in a per-layer weight variance (_nVar[W]_) of 1.0, and all powers of 1.0 result in 1.0, so now all the layers get the same scale of backprop gradients.

To give you a feel of the change, the pre-Glorot initialization of a 5-unit layer would draw samples in the range +/- 0.45, while a Glorot initialization would draw samples in the range +/- 0.77. It might not seem like a big change, but its effect is significant once your network gets deeper.

Later, [Schoenholz _et al_(2016)](<https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1611.01232>) extended the same reasoning to draw additional important lessons that apply more generally:

* Both the variance of the activations (forward pass) and the variance of the backprop gradients (backward pass) must be _preserved_ through the layers. Otherwise, the maximum depth of the network will be limited.
* The exact value of the variance is not too important, so long as it is maintained from layer to layer.
* A model can still train with some deviation, but it will converge more slowly.
* Different activation functions will influence that preservation in different ways. For example, many activation functions (eg, sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU) will _compress_ the variance (ie: reduce the range of values), but by different amounts. Use of the appropriate weight initialization is needed to balance this effect.

In other words, we shouldn’t fixate on looking for a variance of 1.0. Rather, we should expect to see that the variance is consistent across the layers, whatever value it takes. If the variance reduces as it propagates, you have a vanishing gradients problem. If the variance increases as it propagates, you have an exploding gradients problem.

### Saturation

Another problem with the sigmoid function is that its gradients approach zero in its saturated regions. This is true of the tanh activation function as well, another popular activation function which is still used in some networks today.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Earlier work by [Bengio et al (1994)](<https://doi.org/10.1109/72.279181>) explained how this leads to the same kind of exponential decrease in backprop gradients as you move from last layer to first.

The explanation can be understood intuitively. In classification problems, you want a strong 0 or 1 output signal from each unit in the final layer. Thus, a sigmoid layer will tend towards the saturated regions. These have near-zero gradients. As you backprop that through each layer, the same power law takes effect that was discussed earlier, and so the first layer receives a large power of a small gradient, leading to an exponentially smaller gradient than the last layer.

But there’s more. The saturated regions of sigmoid and tanh are achieved with large positive or negative values, which requires large weights. As we’ve identified, the gradients in those regions are small, so there’s minimal force to move the weights. Thus, once the weights become large, they will tend to stay large — they will become “stuck”. You should be able to identify this problem by the presence of large weights.

This particular problem with sigmoid and tanh was an important driver for the popularity of the ReLU activation function.

### Non-zero mean

The sigmoid activation function has an imbalance between its input range and output range. It accepts values in the range of +/- infinity, but outputs in the range 0.0 to 1.0. This is convenient for a single layer, but it becomes a problem if you try to string these together in a chain.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Chain of sigmoid functions operating on each other’s outputs, with reducing input and output ranges at each step

If you subsequently feed that same range of values as input into a second sigmoid function, you get output values in the range 0.5 to 0.73. This range becomes increasingly narrow and positive the more sigmoid functions you chain together.

Alone, the increasingly narrow output range is not a problem in a neural network, where the weights can re-amplify the range before passing to the next sigmoid activation function. But the increasingly positive mean is a problem.

Each layer includes weights and a bias. Perhaps the bias could help. Unfortunately, there’s only a single bias for each unit, and multiple inputs, and the bias is applied _after_ the weights. So the bias is unable to rebalance the mean of the layer inputs. Additionally, the bias is applied _before_ the activation function, which always produces values in the range 0.0 to 1.0. So the bias is also unable to rebalance the mean of the layer outputs.

In principle, weights can switch some inputs to negative, allowing for more balance in the output range of each layer. But in practice, a chain of sigmoid layers is a problem without additional mechanisms. I’ll talk more about those mechanisms shortly.

Tanh is a much better alternative as it produces positive and negative values with a zero mean. Surprisingly, while the mean of a ReLU activation function is technically non-zero, it doesn’t cause the same issues. I suspect this is because a zero input leads to a zero output. Contrast that with a sigmoid function — a zero input leads to a 0.5 output.

## Example Model

We’ve seen so far that a problem like vanishing gradients (and exploding gradients) can have multiple causes and different associated symptoms. This emphasizes a point I made in the [intro article](</better-ways-to-monitor-nns-while-training-7c246867ca4f>) of this series:

* You need a high-level overview that covers many aspects of the model behavior.

If you only have one or two kinds of information about your model, then you risk missing important information that would otherwise help you identify and troubleshoot problems faster.

## Visualizing Vanishing Gradients

The first potential cause relates to the variance of the output activations and the backprop gradients. To understand that, we need to examine how the variance changes from layer to layer. A very effective way to visualize this is to plot the variance against the layers along the x-axis:

Layer propagation in baseline sigmoid model

I’ll refer to these as _propagation plots_ for later reference.

Here I’m using the stddev instead of variance as I find the scale of values it produces to be more intuitively related to the scale of the data. The meaning that we gain from the plots is unchanged.

Notice that the stddev of layer outputs becomes increasingly attenuated as values propagate through the network during the forward pass (first layer to last). The backprop gradients also become increasingly attenuated as they propagate in the opposite direction.

I’ve also included the stddev of the weight gradients. I’ve seen other blog posts using weight gradients for this kind of plot, so I wanted to provide a comparison. Weight gradients are affected by both the variance of the activations and of the backprop gradients, so they show an aggregate effect. The issue is that the effects from activations and weight gradients can mask each other if they are both attenuated but in opposite directions — something you see in this very case. There’s a clear issue when looking at either the activations or the backprop gradients, but it’s far from obvious when looking at the weight gradients.

## Digging Deeper — The Real Cause

We can see that this model is affected by vanishing gradients, but we still lack an explanation of why. To understand that we need more information still.

The introductory article introduced several visualization techniques to gain both an overall and a detailed understanding of model dynamics during training. One such technique is to plot the distributions of values in individual layers. Use of distributions balances the need for detailed information against the fact that typical layer tensors contain tens of thousands of elements across many dimensions — too much to plot directly.

We’ve identified problems with both layer activations and backprop gradients, so let’s start with activations:

The above is one of several standard plots that I use. Each of the small plots gives the distribution of output values for a single layer as a function of training epoch. The top-left summarizes the range of values found across all layers by giving the distribution of layer medians, while the top-right provides a similar summary but stacks the individual layer norms on top of each other.

The first thing to note here is that successive layers have a narrower output range. The first layer outputs approximately in the range 0.2 to 0.8, while the second to last layer outputs in the range 0.4 to 0.55. The last layer is an extreme case, producing a constant output that varies slightly throughout training. This is consistent with the output propagation plot from earlier.

The second thing to note is that all layer outputs fall around the area of 0.5, the sigmoid mean. Furthermore, none of the layer outputs reach close to 0.0 or 1.0, the sigmoid saturated regions.

In other words, this is a clear example of the _non-zero mean_ _problem_ with the sigmoid activation function.

In contrast, we see that the output gradients span a very wide range of scales from 1e-2 to 1e-4. That’s an indication that you would want to look deeper.

## Solving Vanishing Gradients with Initialization

Now that we have a clear understanding of the cause, we can experiment with different solutions.

Given that we know sigmoid is not a suitable activation function for hidden layers, you might be tempted to jump straight to the use of ReLU. But I want to show you something — it turns out that you can tame sigmoid and make it work for you, and the same techniques for taming sigmoid can be useful in more real-world model architectures.

The first thing we’ll try is to experiment with the initialization scheme, and we’ll start with a negative case.

Glorot initialization was introduced to enable deeper networks to work. Before that, we used a similar uniform initialization scheme, but with a slightly smaller range. That smaller range led to lower variance in the weights and thus lower variance in the backprop gradients.

## Solving Vanishing Gradients with Normalization

By exaggerating the range of weight initialization, we were able to improve the sigmoid model, but only by a small margin. This is because adjusting the initialization was inappropriate for our particular problem.

The problem we have is of non-zero mean.

And a more appropriate solution is normalization.

The issue with sigmoid is that it outputs in the positive range with a mean at 0.5, but wants its inputs in the positive/negative range with a mean at zero. BatchNormalization is a perfect solution, as it is designed specifically for that outcome.

To illustrate, we’ll return to our baseline model and this time add BatchNormalization layers. There are two options available for where to place the normalization layers:

* **between** — a normalization layer, inserted _between_ each of the existing sigmoid layers
* **within** — a normalization layer, inserted _within_ each of the existing sigmoid layers, before the activation function.

As we have the tools to visualize and compare, we can experiment with both variations:

``` between_model = tf.keras.Sequential([ layers.Input(shape=(2,)), # block 1 layers.Dense(5, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense1"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm1"), # block 2 layers.Dense(5, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense2"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm2"), # block 3 layers.Dense(5, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense3"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm3"), # block 4 layers.Dense(5, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense4"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm4"), # output layer layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="class")]) ``` ``` within_model = tf.keras.Sequential([ layers.Input(shape=(2,)), # block 1 layers.Dense(5, kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense1"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm1"), layers.Activation(activation="sigmoid", name="sigmoid1"), # block 2 layers.Dense(5, kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense2"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm2"), layers.Activation(activation="sigmoid", name="sigmoid2"), # block 3 layers.Dense(5, kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense3"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm3"), layers.Activation(activation="sigmoid", name="sigmoid3"), # block 4 layers.Dense(5, kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense4"), layers.BatchNormalization(name="batchNorm4"), layers.Activation(activation="sigmoid", name="sigmoid4"), # output layer layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="class")]) ```

Furthermore, its min/max values extend out to about -2.0 to +2.0. That’s perfect as an input to the subsequent sigmoid layer. The behavior here is a little more nuanced. If you look only at the inputs to each sigmoid activation function, they receive values with zero-mean and stddev 1.0. So this approach would appear equally effective. However, the inputs to the pre-activation calculations (ie, the ”dense#” layers) are centered around 0.5. In conclusion, for the resolution of non-zero mean, we prefer the normalization layers to be added _between_ the existing layers (or in other words, _after_ the activation function).

And when we do that, something amazing happens — the multi-layer sigmoid model becomes functional. Who knows how deep you could make this! And if the model starts to exhibit vanishing gradients due to saturation, you could always play with the initialization ranges.

## Solving Vanishing Gradients with Tanh

Another way of solving the non-zero mean problem is to switch to the tanh activation function. It has a very similar response curve to sigmoid, but outputs in the -1.0 to +1.0 range, with a mean at zero.

Like before, we can train a new model variation using the tanh activation function in all of the hidden layers. We remain with a sigmoid for the last layer as we still want a classification result in the range 0.0 to 1.0:

``` model = tf.keras.Sequential([ layers.Input(shape=(2,)), layers.Dense(5, activation="tanh", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense1"), layers.Dense(5, activation="tanh", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense2"), layers.Dense(5, activation="tanh", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense3"), layers.Dense(5, activation="tanh", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="dense4"), layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="class")]) ```

This continues to use Glorot initialization because the rules that Glorot & Bengio (2010) identified apply equally to tanh.

## Solving Vanishing Gradients with ReLU

Finally, we come to the ReLU activation function. This seems almost like an academic exercise in completeness now, as we’ve already solved the problems without the use of ReLU. However, the ReLU activation function is much more popular, and it has some nuances of its own, so it’s useful to take a look.

Firstly, the ReLU activation function compresses the output variance differently from sigmoid and tanh, and needs a slightly different initialization scheme, as introduced by He et al (2015). They applied the same logic from Glorot & Bengio, but adapted it specifically for ReLU (and also for its parameterized leaky versions). This led to the use of a Gaussian distributed initialization scheme configured as follows (where _n_in_ is the number of inputs to each unit in the layer):

He-Initialization scheme

The key change is in the normalization factor, taking into account the characteristics of the ReLU response curve. The choice of Gaussian over uniform distribution was initially little more than a historical artifact. He _et al_ took inspiration from the common use of Gaussian initialization within the vision learning community, whereas Glorot & Bengio took inspiration from the sequence models community. These days, the most common strategy seems to be a blend of Gaussian and uniform — a Gaussian distribution with the tails clipped.

The ReLU model is as follows:

``` model = tf.keras.Sequential([ layers.Input(shape=(2,)), layers.Dense(5, activation="relu", kernel_initializer="he_normal", name="dense1"), layers.Dense(5, activation="relu", kernel_initializer="he_normal", name="dense2"), layers.Dense(5, activation="relu", kernel_initializer="he_normal", name="dense3"), layers.Dense(5, activation="relu", kernel_initializer="he_normal", name="dense4"), layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid", kernel_initializer="glorot_uniform", name="class")]) ```

## Exploding Gradients

The maths introduced at the start of this article suggests that exploding gradients are just the flip side of vanishing gradients. They’re two opposite but related outcomes of the same three causes. In practice, however, those causes result in vanishing gradients more often than exploding gradients.

A quick review helps. The three problems of the sigmoid function were:

1. **Non-unit variance** is taken to a power that depends on network depth. Poor weight initialization could result in variance that is too large or too small. So this could indeed lead to exploding gradients.
2. **Saturation** of the activation function leads to near-zero gradients at affected layers and results in vanishing gradients over the model. This does not lead to exploding gradients.
3. **Non-zero mean** leads to compressed value distributions. This also does not lead to exploding gradients.

So, of the three problems, only one causes exploding gradients. This explains why exploding gradients are much less of a problem in general.

The techniques already discussed that work for non-unit variance can equally be applied to exploding gradients. Add normalization. _Reduce_ the variance of the weight initialization.

There is, however, a fourth problem. Not one that affects the sigmoid activation function specifically, but one that has more to do with gradient propagation in general. The hint is in the backprop scale equation introduced at the start:

Backprop gradients

When discussing the issues with sigmoid, we focused on the first part of this equation (the part with _nVar[W]_). The second half involves the variance of the training data set (_Var[X]_). We ignored that at the time on the basis that it’s a constant. Another difference is that it doesn’t depend on network depth. However, if the variance of the dataset is very large (or, less commonly, very small), then it too can have a detrimental effect on training.

In summary, we have one more item for our list of causes:

4\. **Badly scaled training data** can lead to exploding gradients, and also vanishing gradients (less common).

One curious fact is that the weight gradients are affected significantly more than the layer outputs and backprop gradients. Let’s review the equations provided by Glorot & Bengio one last time, this time also looking at the weight gradients:

Gradient scale equations

Remember that _d_ is the depth of the network, and we have no activation function in the final layer, so _dJ/dZ_d_ is the gradient against the model output.

In our setup, _Var[X]_ = 200, so the backprop gradients (_dJ/dZ_) should be 200x what they ought to be. We’ve typically seen well-behaved models yield backprop gradients somewhere between 1e-1 and 1e-2, so the 1e+1 range that we see above fits with being about 200x the ideal.

Schoenholz _et al_ didn’t yield such simple equations, so we don’t have a guide for the expected scale of output activations, but we’ve seen that they typically follow a similar scale to the backprop gradients. That is more or less what we’ve got here, with the outputs in the 1e+2 range.

The weight gradients, however, are around 1e+4 to 1e+5. I think what’s happening here comes down to the three components that make up the weight gradients, though I don’t think we can take the equation too literally. If the base of the first term (_nVar[W]_) is greater than 1, then it gets larger with network depth. The middle component multiplies everything by 2e+2 (ie, 200), and the third component is about 1e+2. So the weight gradients should be in about the 1e+4 range — more or less consistent with what we see.

## Solving Exploding Gradients

For the sloping power-law forms of exploding gradients, the solutions mentioned already can work well:

* **BatchNormalisation**
* **Reduce variance of weight initialization**

Another common approach is **gradient clipping**.

However, if exploding gradients are caused by large values in your dataset, there are two solutions that you might consider:

* **Re-scale the data**
* **Normalization**

The best option is to rescale the data so that all features and outputs have a stddev around 1.0, and a suitably small mean. Most of the academic literature on neural networks assumes a variance of 1.0, and the work of Glorot & Bengio and others has shown that you have problems otherwise.

Perhaps you don’t want to rescale your data because you want your model to work against real data in its natural scale. The next best thing is to add custom input and/or output layers that do the same rescaling. It’s probably best to treat these as untrainable layers that you simply hard-code with the right scaling factors. If they’re trainable, there’s a risk that their gradients may influence the scale of gradients throughout the model.

Your last option might be to use trainable normalization layers such as BatchNormalization, but there are some gotchas.

With the high _Var[X]_ dataset and the regression model used here, a BatchNormalization layer added immediately after the input layer solves the problem and enables the model to learn optimally. The equation above would suggest that _Var[X]_ should still cause a problem, but it would seem that the _X_ in the _Var[X]_ component becomes the normalized input rather than the raw input. And so the model behaves well.

In contrast, a BatchNormalization layer is unable to help our regression model against the high _Var[y]_ dataset. A final linear layer can produce outputs in any range, and can learn to multiply its inputs accordingly. BatchNormalization acts to reduce that range. I tried adding it _before_ the output layer, in the hope of allowing the hidden layers to maintain a normal variance and limiting the multiplication effect to the last layer. This was unsuccessful, it did just as badly as the results we’ve seen above.

The take-home message for exploding gradients is this:

* Exploding gradients may appear in the same form (but opposite) as we saw for vanishing gradients, with a power-law of propagation causing variance to increase as outputs are propagated forward or gradients are propagated backwards. Normalization and adjustments to weight initialization should help.
* Exploding gradients can also appear as a uniform amplification of activations and/or gradients. This is usually caused by poorly scaled training datasets, and the solution is to explicitly rescale the data. Normalization layers won’t always help.
* Weight gradients sometimes give a stronger signal about exploding gradients than layer outputs or backprop gradients. This means that you need to monitor all three properties: layer output variance, backprop variance, and weight gradient variance.

## Choice of Metrics

All of the visualizations discussed here require that you introspect model training to collect the appropriate stats. This has a computational cost and can noticeably increase training time if not carefully optimized.

The propagation plots depend on having a measure of the _scale_ of the output activations and gradients. The literature uses variance, based on mathematical fundamentals. But that isn’t the only measure of scale available.

When you’re building your own techniques for monitoring model training, you’ll be wanting to check for much more than just vanishing/exploding gradients. At the end of the day, you’re likely to choose a small number of metrics that minimize computational cost while providing the broadest visibility.

To aid you in that, here are some metrics that you could consider for propagation plots (formulas are all listed below):

* **variance** or **stddev** : variance is the most common metric in academic discussion on vanishing/exploding gradients and it has a strong mathematical basis for that. Statistically, layer outputs and gradients contain many thousands of random variables, and the [Central Limit Theorem](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem>) says that a mixture of distributions will tend towards the Gaussian distribution. Variance and stddev are the most appropriate way to measure such distributions.
* **L2-norm** : the L2-norm, aka Euclidean norm, is also a very popular choice for measuring the scale of gradients in academic literature. The basic L2-norm, however, is dependent on tensor size, and so it is also quite common to apply various means to normalize its value so that you can compare tensors of different sizes. My preferred option results in the root-mean-square (RMS). For gradients, it turns out that RMS and stddev have very similar results. More on that shortly.
* **Magnitude-mean** : you can get a similar indication of scale by using the mean of absolute values. I think of it like the L1-equivalent of RMS. It will often produce similar values, but it differs subtly in its relative sensitivity to smaller and larger values. Sometimes that can be an issue.
* **Percentile approximations** : it’s also possible to approximate the measures above from percentile data if that’s what you’ve got available. In my own experiments, I prefer to collect percentile statistics because they give so much more information. However that comes with a large computational expense already and I don’t want to add additional calculations of stddev or RMS. Gradient distributions tend to be symmetric about their mean, so the median can be a good approximation of the mean. Likewise, the magnitude-median is a good approximation of the magnitude-mean. Alternatively, with some assumptions about distribution shape, you can combine the percentiles to produce smoother estimates. For example, gradient magnitudes tend to follow a log-normal distribution, so a geometric mean of the magnitude percentiles provides a good metric of scale.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

As a general rule, you will want to use stddev or variance for detection of vanishing/exploding gradients. The other metrics can be very useful for other things, and they can be used for detecting of vanishing/exploding gradients, but there are some caveats you must be aware of if you choose to do that. These fall into the following categories:

* Effect of distribution mean
* Sensitivity to small values
* Zeroing out of percentile data

Gradients tend to have a very symmetric distribution, with a mean at or very close to zero. This means that the E[X] term in the equations above usually vanishes. Thus stddev and RMS are usually the same for gradients. For example, here’s stddev and RMS from the backprop gradients in the baseline sigmoid model used above:

The reason is that the output layer in that model produced a near-constant with mean 0.5. That translates to a near-zero variance, but a 0.5 RMS.

When considering between stddev/variance and RMS/norm, I think of it this way:

* stddev/variance — measures the scale of variation relative to the mean of the distribution. Good for getting an idea of the _width_ of the distribution.
* RMS/norm — measures the scale of variation relative to zero. Good for getting an idea of the typical _magnitude_ of values.

And remember that gradients usually have zero-mean, while layer outputs may not.

When choosing between L2-metrics like stddev/variance/norm/RMS and L1-metrics like magnitude-mean, it turns out that their relative sensitivities to smaller values can have a large effect.
