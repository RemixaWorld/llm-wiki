## Addressing a Problem

Basketball strategy has evolved over the past decade to enhance player performance, with a strong focus on improving shot selection. While analytics have crept into almost every decision made in the modern era of basketball, one area that remains medieval is choosing defensive matchups. Since the inception of man-to-man defense, the common philosophy has been to match players with similar heights to create the most balanced defensive lineup. While this is the standard approach, there are certainly slight tweaks made, such as adjusting matchups based on defensive ability and assigning the best defender to the opposing team’s top scoring threat.

I’m not naïve to the fact that numbers play a role in determining matchups especially at the NBA level, but it’s not to the degree that it should be. Some offensive players struggle to score against certain physical measurements that are different from the typical body type that they’re accustomed to competing against, such as a short and quick defender or one with long wingspan.

My goal for this project was to develop a model that identifies the optimal physical attributes needed to minimize offensive production against each offensive player in the NBA. Then, I identified the current player whose attributes most closely align with these optimal dimensions, making them the best candidate to effectively defend the opposing player.

## Matchup Impact Model

My first step was attempting to answer the question of how well a defender guards an offensive player strictly through numerical data. There is a very valuable function in the hoopR package that allows me to load detailed data for every individual matchup. This dataset included the offensive and defensive players involved, the amount of time they matched up, and key box score stats such as shot attempts, points scored, fouls, blocks, turnovers, and more.

I loaded every offensive/defensive matchup over the past 5 seasons with a minimum 25 possessions for that season. I chose this threshold because I wanted to exclude small sample sizes, but still leave a low enough bar to include non-typical matchups such as a smaller defender guarding a larger offensive player and vice versa. I separated the matchups by year and made each of the box score stats on a per possession basis.

To quantify the effectiveness of the defender, I built a multiple linear regression model using **points per possession** as the target variable. After testing for multicollinearity and removing a few box score stats, the final model included the normalized values of **field goal percentage** , **shooting fouls per possession** , **blocks per possession** , and **turnovers per possession.**

After running the model, I used the coefficients as weights to create a new metric called **Matchup Impact Score**. This statistic consolidates all aspects of the matchup into one number based on the importance of each input. I believe this is a better way to evaluate a matchup compared to stand-alone metrics such as points or FG% allowed since it gives more context.

For perspective on the scale, Matchup Impact Scores span from -5 to 12 (A lower score means better job defensively) with a median of -0.05.

## Data Preparation

I wanted to add more context to the offensive and defensive players in the matchups data frame. Therefore, I loaded all NBA Combine physical measurements from 2000–2024 to extract each player’s height without shoes and wingspan. I noticed that there were two main issues with this data: some players, such as Victor Wembanyama, did not participate in the Combine, and players may have experienced slight growth spurts in the years after they were measured during their rookie season. To address this, I downloaded the heights and wingspans for all players for the current season from[ CraftedNBA](<https://craftednba.com/>).com. The matchup data I was using in this project was for the last five seasons, so between the data from CraftedNBA and the Combine, I was able to gather information for about 99% of the players.

Next, I wanted to describe the shot profile and quality of each offensive player. I extracted shot locations for each player and categorized each shot as either a Rim, Short Mid-Range, Long Mid-Range, or Three-Point attempt. Then, I calculated the frequency and efficiency with which they shot in each zone for that particular season.

I then joined this data to the matchups data frame, adding the height and wingspan characteristics for both offensive and defensive players, as well as the offensive statistics for the offensive players only.

## Optimizing Matchups

I’m pretty familiar with creating predictive models at this point, but this next section was uncharted territory (shoutout to Nick Kalinowski for introducing me). Using **Bayesian Optimization** , I sought to find the optimal height and wingspan of the defender to minimize the expected Matchup Impact Score. Essentially, I created a function where you input an offensive player, and it finds the physical dimensions that would defend the player most effectively. Running this machine learning function is a relatively lengthy process, as it takes roughly 5 minutes to calculate for each individual player.

After acquiring the defender’s optimal height and wingspan, I wanted to see which current NBA player mirrors these measurements most closely. To accomplish this, I assembled another function. **This function does not account for the quality of the defender; it simply compares the physical characteristics.** I chose not to include defender quality because the model would overly favor better defenders when I wanted the focus to remain on the opponent’s size.

In addition to player deployment strategies, these models and visualizations can also be used for identifying players to acquire. For example, let’s say a team wants to beat the Thunder but doesn’t have a great on-ball guard defender to guard Shai Gilgeous-Alexander. They can use this model to get creative and acquire someone via trade or free agency to attempt to stifle the MVP candidate. You can also view this more generally and pursue free agents or draft prospects who fit the mold of an archetype that players tend to struggle against.

## Next Steps and Ways to Improve

Moving forward, the logical next phase would be to build an application (or simply a list) of each offensive player’s optimal defensive physical measurements and the corresponding player who aligns most closely. There could also be a team filter to help solve the problem of “How should the Brooklyn Nets guard Jayson Tatum?” — where you choose a team, and it will match Jayson Tatum’s optimal defender measurements with someone on their roster instead of the entire league. The major roadblock to building this application is how long the model takes to run, so I may need to come up with a faster way to make it work.

There’s definitely room for improvement in building the models. For the Matchup Impact Score metric, the weight in the formula for turnovers was much lower than I anticipated, since forcing a turnover should be worth more than allowing a shot. Ideally, I would determine how much value forcing a turnover in a specific matchup has on winning the game (please let me know if you have any ideas). This would make the Matchup Impact Score a much better representation of defensive value.
