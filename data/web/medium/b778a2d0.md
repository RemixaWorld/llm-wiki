A couple days ago I was chatting with a search engineer managing a billion-row vector index. They’d done all the sensible things: on-disc HNSW, a 748-dimensional embedding model. Yet their cloud bill was through the roof — easily tens of thousands per year. Even worse, they had a team of over 5 engineers dedicated to search! The reason wasn’t because they chose the wrong embedding model or even the wrong vector database. It was simple: their vectors were far too large.

## Why a Billion Embeddings Cost So Much

At 768 dims, each float32 vector is 768×4 B = 3 072 B, so 1 billion of them is 3.07 TB raw. If you factor in ~15 % for HNSW overhead, metadata, etc., you end up with ≈ 3.5 TB on disk.

On AWS gp3 at the base rate ($0.08 /GiB-month, about $80 /TB-month), that’s ~$280/mo per node. But to sustain sub-10 ms vector searches you’ll typically provision extra IOPS and throughput — so your cost will look closer to $400–450 /TB-month. At $450 /TB-month, 3.5 TB costs about **$1575/mo per node** just for storage. Add replicas, dev/test clusters, ingest pipelines and compute nodes, and you’re easily paying in the low five-figure range every month for search. The curse of dimensionality makes this worse. Higher dims mean sparser data. To keep recall high, the index needs to examine more candidates, driving up CPU and I/O. In short, every extra dimension is a brick in your cost wall.

But the real cost of large indexes isn’t even the infra bill — it’s maintenance and paying a large search team to build it out and take care of it. Reducing your index size 10x can save you 2–6 employees dedicated to search!

## The Cost of Underquantization

“Underquantization” is keeping your embeddings larger than they need to be. In 2024 it was common to swallow default model outputs — 768 or 1,536 dims — because “more is better.” More importantly, quantization techniques weren’t as advances when many of these search pipelines were being created, and by the time they were built they were already bloated compared to the SOTA. Vector databases were not incentivized to educate developers on proper quantization, as a lack of quantization increases their profits!

Modern benchmarks show that with the right embedding model, 64-dim binary vectors can maintain most of the performance of 1,024-dim ones for many search tasks. While binary quantization (1 bit per dimension) helps, but it can’t overcome an overly wide vector.

To understand the massive space savings you can get from quantization at internet-scale, let’s do the math for 2 billion vectors:

* **748 dims, float32**
• Raw = 2e9 × 748 × 4 B ≈ 5.98 TB
• Indexed (~25 % overhead) ≈ 7.48 TB
* **128 dims, float32**
• Raw = 2e9 × 128 × 4 B = 1.024 TB
• Indexed ≈ 1.28 TB
* **64 dims, float32**
• Raw = 2e9 × 64 × 4 B = 0.512 TB
• Indexed ≈ 0.64 TB
* **64 dims, binary (1 bit/dim)**
• Raw = 2e9 × 64 bits / 8 = 16 GB (0.016 TB)
• HNSW pointer overhead = 2e9 × 256 B ≈ 0.512 TB
• Total indexed ≈ 0.016 TB + 0.512 TB = 0.528 TB

That’s an order of magnitude less data, and will result in significantly fewer costs and faster searches. More importantly, this is more manageable for a tiny search team to handle.

## 2025 Quantization and On-Disc Index Advances

In 2025 we have new tools to make these cuts safely. Models trained with nested embeddings (Matryoshka Representation Learning) output vectors whose first 64 or 128 dims carry nearly all the semantic weight. You can truncate the rest without retraining a separate low-dim model. Often they are trained to respond well to binary and scalar quantization as well.

On the index side, KX’s qHNSW on-disc engine keeps most of the vector data on SSD and only minimal metadata in RAM. Millions of 64-dim vectors fit in a few gigabytes, queries stay under 200 ms, and your CPU load falls dramatically.

The trick is a two-stage retrieval. Use the compact, cheap index to fetch the top-K candidates. Then rerank with a cross-encoder or with BM25. You can often regain any minor accuracy loss from quantization. In real benchmarks the recall drop is often under 5 percent. It often makes sense to built an aggressively quantized index quickly, then focus on fine-tuning rerankers later. This makes sense — it’s easier to change a reranking endpoint than re-embed 1B data points!

## Simple Steps to Slash Your Bill

* Start with small dims. Try 64 or 128 instead of default 768 or 1,536.
* Quantize aggressively. Float32→int8 or binary is table stakes; dimension cuts yield the biggest wins.
* Use two-stage retrieval. Coarse filter on compact index, then rerank a handful of candidates.
* Benchmark rigorously if you can. Measure precision, latency and cost before and after each change. But if you don’t benchmark, do NOT use big embeddings. You have no evidence they perform better than small embeddings on your data.
* Override defaults. Many vector DBs ship with high-dim models and in-RAM indexes. Switch to on-disc qHNSW and set dims explicitly.

Vector search isn’t about maxing out dimensions or filling RAM. It’s an engineering optimization problem. In 2025, with robust quantization and on-disc indexes, you can run large-scale search on one or two machines instead of a fleet. Trim your vectors, tune your pipeline, and watch your infrastructure costs collapse.
