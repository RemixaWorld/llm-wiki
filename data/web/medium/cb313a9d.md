# Revisiting Chunking in the RAG Pipeline

## Unveiling the Cutting-Edge Advances in Chunking

Chunking involves dividing a long text or document into smaller, logically coherent segments or “chunks.” Each chunk usually contains one or more sentences, with the segmentation based on the text’s structure or meaning. Once divided, each chunk can be processed independently or used in subsequent tasks, such as retrieval or generation.

The role of chunking in the mainstream RAG pipeline is shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1 : The role of the Chunking process(red box) in the mainstream RAG pipeline. Image by author.

[In the previous article](<https://medium.com/towards-artificial-intelligence/advanced-rag-05-exploring-semantic-chunking-97c12af20a4d>), we explored various methods of semantic chunking, explaining their underlying principles and practical applications. These methods included:

* Embedding-based methods: When the similarity between consecutive sentences drops below a certain threshold, a chunk boundary is introduced.
* Model-based methods: Utilize deep learning models, such as BERT, to segment documents effectively.
* LLM-based methods: Use LLMs to construct propositions, achieving more refined chunks.

However, since the previous article was published on February 28, 2024, there have been significant advancements in chunking over the past few months. **Therefore, this article presents** some of the latest developments in chunking within the RAG pipeline, focusing primarily on the following topics:

* [**LumberChunker**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17526v1>): A more dynamic and contextually aware chunking method.
* [**Mix-of-Granularity(MoG)**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.00456v1>): Optimizes the chunking granularity for RAG.
* [**Prepare-then-Rewrite-then-Retrieve-then-Read(PR3)**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09017v1>): Enhances retrieval precision and contextual relevance by generating synthetic question-answer pairs (QA pairs) and Meta Knowledge (MK Summaries) Summaries.
* [**Chunking-Free In-Context Retrieval(CFIC)**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09760v1>): Eliminates the chunking process by utilizing encoded hidden states of documents.

For each topic, additional thoughts and insights are offered.

## **LumberChunker**

### Key Idea

LumberChunker’s key idea is to dynamically segment long-form texts into contextually coherent chunks using a large language model, optimizing information retrieval by dynamically segmenting text to maintain semantic coherence and relevance.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 2: LumberChunker follows a three-step process. Source: [LumberChunker](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17526v1>).

Figure 2 illustrates the LumberChunker pipeline, where documents are first segmented paragraph-wise, then paragraphs are grouped together until a predefined token count is exceeded, and finally, a model like Gemini identifies significant content shifts, marking the boundaries for each chunk; this process is repeated cyclically for the entire document.

Here’s a streamlined breakdown of its workflow:

1. **Paragraph-wise Segmentation** : The document is first divided into individual paragraphs, each assigned a unique ID. This initial step creates manageable units of text that serve as the foundation for further processing.
2. **Grouping Paragraphs** : These paragraphs are then grouped sequentially into a group (referred to as `Gi`​) until the total token count exceeds a set threshold `θ`, which is optimally set around 550 tokens. This threshold is crucial for balancing the context provided to the model without overwhelming it.
3. **Identifying Content Shifts:** The group `Gi`​ is analyzed by the LLM (e.g., Gemini 1.0-Pro), which identifies the precise point where the content shifts significantly. This point marks the boundary between the current chunk and the next.
4. **Iterative Chunk Formation:** After identifying the content shift, the document is iteratively segmented, with each new group starting from the identified shift point in the previous group. This ensures each chunk is contextually coherent.
5. **Optimizing Chunk Size:** The chosen threshold `θ` ensures that chunks are neither too small, risking loss of context, nor too large, risking model overload. The balance around 550 tokens is found to be optimal for maintaining retrieval accuracy.

### Evaluation

LumberChunker was rigorously evaluated using the GutenQA benchmark, a collection of 3000 question-answer pairs derived from 100 public domain narrative books.

Figure 4 highlights LumberChunker’s superior performance in retrieval tasks, with a 7.37% improvement in DCG@20 over the most competitive baseline.

Compared to other chunking methods like semantic chunking, paragraph-level chunking, and recursive chunking, LumberChunker consistently outperforms these baselines, especially in retrieval tasks.

LumberChunker’s dynamic approach ensures that each chunk is contextually cohesive, offering a significant advantage over proposition-level chunking, which can be overly granular for narrative texts.

### Computational Trade-offs in LumberChunker

The computational efficiency of LumberChunker compared to other chunking methods is shown in Figure 5. The key takeaway is that while LumberChunker significantly improves retrieval performance, it does so at a higher computational cost, especially with larger documents.

Figure 5: The time required to apply LumberChunker or one of the baselines to each book. Source: [LumberChunker](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17526v1>).

* **Recursive Chunking** : This method is the fastest due to its simplicity and lack of reliance on LLMs. Even though its processing time increases with document size, it remains efficient because it doesn’t involve complex API requests.
* [**HyDE**](<https://medium.com/@florian_algo/advanced-rag-06-exploring-query-rewriting-23997297f2d1>): Despite using LLMs, HyDE maintains a constant processing time for all document sizes because it limits the number of LLM queries to a fixed number (30 per book).
* **LumberChunker, Semantic, and Proposition-Level chunking** : they show significant increases in completion time with larger documents. Notably, both Semantic and Proposition-Level chunking can be optimized through asynchronous OpenAI API requests, greatly reducing completion times. However, LumberChunker’s dynamic LLM queries prevent such optimization. Despite LumberChunker’s superior retrieval performance compared to other baselines, there remains room for further improvement.

### Thoughts and Insights about **LumberChunker**

By dynamically adjusting chunk sizes based on semantic shifts, LumberChunker offers a novel approach that addresses key limitations in existing chunking methods, particularly in handling long-form narrative texts.

However, its reliance on LLMs, while effective, introduces computational overheads, making it potentially less scalable for large-scale applications. Additionally, its performance in more structured texts, like legal documents, remains an open question.

Future improvements could focus on optimizing the computational efficiency of LumberChunker or exploring hybrid approaches that combine its strengths with other chunking techniques.

## Mix-of-Granularity(MoG)

### Overview

Consider a scenario where a medical RAG system retrieves information from both textbooks and medical knowledge graphs. The optimal chunk size for retrieving data from these two sources differs significantly due to their distinct data structures — textbooks have long, contextually rich passages, whereas knowledge graphs consist of short, interconnected terms. Using a fixed chunk size may result in irrelevant or insufficient data retrieval, thus hampering the performance of the LLM.

Figure 6: The Mix-of-Granularity (MoG) mechanism can automatically select the optimal granularity when extracting information from the reference knowledge database (scenarios 1 and 2), achieving both high information pertinence and coverage. The selected text is passed to LLM for generation. When relevant information is dispersed across distant sections (scenario 3), MoG-Graph (MoGG) is applied to effectively retrieve these separate pieces of information. In MoGG, the reference documents are pre-processed in the form of a graph, and MoGG retrieves information with the best hopping range. The text corresponding to the selected subgraph is then fed to LLM for RAG. Source: [MoG](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.00456v1>).

The [Mix-of-Granularity (MoG)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.00456v1>) method, which dynamically adjusts the chunking granularity of the reference database based on the user’s query. MoG is inspired by the Mix-of-Experts (MoE) approach, where a router selects the most relevant granularity level, thereby improving the pertinence and coverage of the retrieved data.

### Chunking

The reference documents within the knowledge database are pre-processed and divided into chunks at multiple granularity levels.

For instance, the text might be segmented into fine-grained chunks (smaller, more detailed snippets) and coarse-grained chunks (larger sections of text). Each granularity level captures different amounts of information, with finer granularity providing more specific details and coarser granularity offering broader context.

### Router

Router is a core component of the Mix-of-Granularity (MoG) method. Its primary function is to dynamically select the most appropriate chunking granularity level based on the user’s input query, thereby optimizing the relevance and coverage of the retrieved information.

The implementation of Router is shown below.

1. **Input Encoding** : The user’s query is first encoded into a vector representation (embedding) using a pre-trained model, such as RoBERTa. This vector representation is then fed into the router.
2. **Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)** : The router is essentially a MLP neural network composed of several hidden layers. The MLP maps the input query encoding to a set of weight vectors, corresponding to different granularity levels.
3. **Weight Generation** : The router generates a weight​ for each granularity level based on patterns learned during training. These weights represent the relative importance of the chunks retrieved at each granularity level.
4. **Combining Relevance Scores** : For the retrieved chunks, the system uses these weights to adjust their relevance scores by calculating a weighted sum. This process determines which chunks are ultimately selected for the final generation.
5. **Training Method** : The router is trained through a supervised learning approach, with the objective of maximizing the semantic similarity of the selected segments. To achieve this, MoG introduces soft labels to bypass the non-differentiable top-k selection process and optimizes the router’s parameters by minimizing the Binary Cross-Entropy loss function.

### Retrieval Process

Figure 7 provides a deeper look into the specific workflow of the MoG method. It focuses on explaining how the system retrieves information at different granularity levels and dynamically selects the most relevant segments through the router.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 7: Mix-of-Granularity (MoG) mechanism prioritizes the chunks retrieved from optimal granularity level, which is determined by the router based on the user input query. Source: [MoG](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.00456v1>).

1. **Multi-Level Retrieval:** For each granularity level, the system retrieves a set number of top-ranked snippets (e.g., top-3 or top-5) based on their similarity to the encoded query. This process is conducted in parallel across all granularity levels, resulting in multiple sets of candidate snippets.
2. **Relevance Scoring:** Each snippet retrieved from the various granularity levels is assigned a relevance score based on its similarity to the user’s query. Since the snippets are retrieved using the same query embedding, their relevance scores are directly comparable across different granularity levels.
3. **Weighting and Summing:** The router-generated weights for each granularity level are then applied to the relevance scores. This means that the relevance scores of snippets from granularity levels deemed more important by the router are amplified, while those from less relevant levels are diminished. The snippets with the highest weighted relevance scores are selected to be passed on to the next stage.
4. **Top-k Selection:** After applying the weights, a final top-k selection is performed. The top-k snippets with the highest weighted relevance scores are chosen. These selected snippets are considered the most relevant to the user’s query and are then prepared for input into the LLM.

### **Evaluation**

The effectiveness of MoG is evaluated through extensive experiments on Medical Question-Answering datasets.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 8: Accuracy of Medical Question-Answering task with MoG (trained with MedCorp), best results marked in bold. Source: [MoG](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.00456v1>).

The results show that MoG consistently improves the accuracy of RAG systems across different LLMs.

For example, the accuracy on the PubMedQA dataset improved significantly when using MoG, particularly with smaller, less knowledgeable models like Qwen1.5, which benefited more from the enhanced retrieval process.

### **Thoughts and Insights about** Mix-of-Granularity

This approach presents a significant advancement in the field of RAG systems, particularly in its ability to dynamically adjust retrieval strategies based on the nature of the query.

However, there are still some challenges:

1. **Complexity and Training Costs** : The router’s dynamic nature increases system complexity and requires significant computational resources for training, which could be challenging for large-scale applications.
2. **Manual Granularity Selection** : The current reliance on manually labeled granularity levels may limit adaptability to new domains. Automating this process could enhance flexibility.
3. **Query Adaptability** : While effective, MoG’s granularity selection might struggle with very short or very long queries, suggesting room for further refinement.

## Prepare-then-Rewrite-then-Retrieve-then-Read(PR3)

The [Prepare-then-Rewrite-then-Retrieve-then-Read(PR3)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09017v1>) framework was introduced as an innovative and structured approach to optimizing the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) tasks.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 9: Data-centric workflow for Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) systems. Prior to inference, documents are augmented with Claude 3, and clustered into metadata-based sets of synthetic questions and answers for personalized downstream retrieval. Meta Knowledge Summaries are used to guide the query augmentation step with clusters information. Source: [PR3](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09017v1>).

In the PR3 framework, the traditional method of document chunking is effectively bypassed and replaced with a more sophisticated approach that relies on generating synthetic question-answer pairs (QA pairs) and using these pairs for retrieval, rather than dividing documents into smaller chunks.

### No Direct Document Chunking

Unlike traditional methods that break a document into fixed-size chunks (e.g., 256 tokens with overlapping segments), PR3 avoids this by not chunking the document directly. Instead, it analyzes the entire document to extract key content.

### Synthetic QA Generation

For each document, the system generates metadata and synthetic QA pairs. These QA pairs summarize the critical points of the document in a question-and-answer format. This step replaces the need for traditional chunking because it captures the essence of the document in a more structured and retrievable form.

For example, rather than having chunks of text, the system might generate a QA pair like:

* **Question:** “What is the impact of reinforcement learning on traffic optimization?”
* **Answer:** “Reinforcement learning significantly improves real-time traffic flow management by optimizing decision-making processes under dynamic conditions.”

### Embedding and Retrieval Using QA Pairs

The generated questions from these QA pairs are embedded into a high-dimensional vector space.

During retrieval, instead of matching user queries to document chunks, the system matches them to these embedded QA pairs. This approach mitigates the information loss that can occur with document chunking and allows for more precise retrieval based on the user’s query.

### Meta Knowledge Summaries

Additionally, PR3 introduces Meta Knowledge Summaries (MK Summaries), which aggregate and summarize knowledge across multiple documents related to specific metadata.

For example, in the case of a query related to “Reinforcement Learning,” the system first retrieves the MK Summary for that topic, then augments the user’s query based on this summary, and finally searches the filtered database to retrieve the most relevant information.

### Evaluation

The PR3 framework outperforms traditional methods across key metrics, including recall, precision, and specificity. The evaluation involved generating 200 synthetic user queries and comparing the PR3 framework against traditional chunking-based RAG systems and a simpler QA-based retrieval system.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 10: Performance benchmark across 200 Synthetic user queries. Source: [PR3](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09017v1>).

The results, as shown in Figure 10, indicate that the PR3 framework, particularly when using MK Summaries, outperforms other methods in terms of breadth and depth of retrieved information, significantly enhancing the quality of the final answers provided by the LLM.

### Thoughts and Insights about PR3

The PR3 framework does not perform traditional chunking; instead, it generates and relies on synthetic QA pairs and metadata-driven summaries.

This method allows the system to preserve the semantic integrity of the information and improves the retrieval precision by focusing on the key content and context provided by the QA pairs and MK Summaries.

However, there are potential limitations:

* **Reliance on Metadata Quality:** The effectiveness of PR3 heavily depends on the quality of the metadata and the generated QA pairs. If the metadata is poorly generated or lacks depth, it could result in less effective retrievals.
* **Complexity in Implementation:** While PR3 offers significant benefits, its implementation is more complex than traditional RAG systems. The need to generate and manage synthetic QA pairs and MK Summaries requires sophisticated processing and careful tuning, which might be challenging for some applications.
* **Potential for Overfitting in Narrow Domains:** In very narrow or specialized domains, there is a risk that the system’s reliance on synthetic QA pairs could lead to overfitting, where the generated QA pairs may not fully capture the nuance of user queries that fall outside the pre-generated contexts.

## Chunking-Free In-Context Retrieval(CFIC)

### Comparison

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 11: Comparison of Chunking-Based and Chunking-Free Methods. The left panel illustrates the chunking-based method, involving chunking a lengthy document into smaller passages followed by refinement through passage ranking. The right panel depicts the chunking-free method proposed in this paper, where grounding text is directly decoded by LLMs without the need for document chunking. Source: [CFIC](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09760v1>).

Traditional methods often rely on document chunking, where large documents are split into smaller passages. While this makes it easier to manage the text, it introduces several problems:

* **Semantic Disruption** : Chunking can break the natural flow of text, leading to a loss of semantic coherence, as depicted in Figure 11.
* **Noise and Inaccuracy** : Filtering relevant content from chunks often results in sub-optimal evidence retrieval, impacting the accuracy of the generated response.

### Chunking-Free

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 12: Overview of CFIC. The middle part shows the Constrained Sentence Prefix Decoding strategy which ensures the generated text prefixes originate from the input article. The right part shows the Skip Decoding strategy which bypasses decoding the intermediate tokens while terminating generation at the position with the best likelihood of [eos] token. Gray tokens in the figure are bypassed during generation. Source: [CFIC](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09760v1>).

[The CFIC method](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.09760v1>) achieves chunking-free processing through several key mechanisms:

1. Encoding Entire Documents Using Transformer Hidden States: It avoids the traditional approach of chunking documents by encoding the entire document into hidden states using a transformer model, such as LLAMA2–7B-chat. Instead of dividing the document into smaller chunks, CFIC encodes the whole document, preserving its semantic integrity and continuity.
2. Auto-Regressive Decoding Based on Hidden States: When a user query is received, CFIC uses the encoded hidden states of the entire document combined with the query to perform auto-regressive decoding. This process allows the model to directly generate the exact relevant text needed to answer the query without having to rely on pre-chunked text segments.
3. Constrained Sentence Prefix Decoding: This strategy constrains the generation of text to the sentence prefixes found within the original document. This ensures that the generated text is faithful to the source material and originates directly from the document. By constraining the generation space to valid sentence prefixes, CFIC can maintain the relevance and accuracy of the retrieved text.
4. Skip Decoding: Once a suitable sentence prefix is identified, CFIC employs “Skip Decoding,” which bypasses unnecessary intermediate tokens and directly decodes the most probable endpoint, typically marked by the [eos] (end-of-sequence) token. This strategy improves efficiency and ensures that the generated text is concise and directly relevant to the query.
5. Multiple Candidate Prefixes and Ranking: To ensure that the most relevant text is retrieved, CFIC generates multiple candidate sentence prefixes and ranks them based on their generation probabilities. The top-ranked candidates are then used as the supporting text for downstream tasks, such as answering the query.

### Training

**LLAMA2–7B-chat** is the foundational model used in the CFIC method for both training and inference tasks. To tailor the model to specific tasks, CFIC employs Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). The process includes the following steps:

* **Data Preparation** : First, create a custom training dataset comprising long documents, corresponding questions, and precise textual evidence generated by ChatGPT. These data are structured as triplets `(query, document, evidence)`.
* **Training Process** : During training, the LLAMA2–7B-chat model receives input pairs of `(query, document)` and generates the corresponding evidence text. The model is optimized using the Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) loss function, which measures the difference between the generated text and the target evidence text. The learning rate is set at `1e-5`, with a batch size of 1, and gradient accumulation steps set at 8 per GPU. The model was trained for 600 steps on 8 Nvidia A800 80GB GPUs.
* **Optimization Strategy** : To enhance training efficiency and save computational resources, the training process utilizes DeepSpeed’s Stage 2 Zero Optimization technology and a warm-up strategy to gradually adjust the learning rate.

In this case study, we examine how the CFIC method outperforms other models, such as GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LongAlpaca-7B, in retrieving accurate and relevant evidence for a complex query.

The query, “What hedge fund’s collapse in 1998 highlighted the need for regulation of derivatives?” requires precise retrieval of historical financial information.

### Thoughts and Insights about CFIC

By encoding the entire document and leveraging advanced decoding strategies, CFIC eliminates the need for document chunking. It directly retrieves and generates the most relevant text, maintaining the semantic coherence of the document and improving the accuracy and efficiency of the retrieval process.

However, there are some potential limitations:

1. **Computational Resources** : While CFIC is more efficient than traditional methods, it still requires significant computational resources, particularly when dealing with very long documents. The need for advanced hardware, such as GPUs with large memory, might limit its accessibility to smaller organizations or individual researchers.
2. **Training Data Dependency** : The model’s reliance on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with a self-constructed dataset raises concerns about generalization. The quality and diversity of the training data are critical, and any biases in the data could potentially affect the model’s performance across different domains or types of queries.
3. **Complexity of Implementation** : CFIC’s advanced decoding strategies and the need for custom training could make it more challenging to implement and integrate into existing systems compared to simpler, chunking-based methods. The complexity of PR3’s implementation might limit its adoption in settings requiring simpler solutions.

## Conclusion

The article delves deeply into various chunking techniques used in the RAG pipeline, including LumberChunker, Mix-of-Granularity (MoG), Prepare-then-Rewrite-then-Retrieve-then-Read (PR3), and Chunking-Free In-Context Retrieval (CFIC).

From dynamic methods like LumberChunker, which optimizes chunk sizes based on semantic shifts, to the innovative Mix-of-Granularity approach, which tailors chunking granularity to the nature of the query, these techniques address specific challenges in information retrieval. The PR3 framework and Chunking-Free In-Context Retrieval (CFIC) method further push the boundaries by introducing synthetic QA pairs and bypassing traditional chunking altogether, enhancing the precision and relevance of retrieved information.

However, while these methods offer significant improvements, they also introduce new complexities, such as increased computational costs and implementation challenges.

Future research and development could focus on several key improvements:

1. **Optimizing Computational Efficiency** : Reducing the computational overhead of these methods, possibly through more efficient model architectures or distributed processing techniques, would make them more scalable and accessible.
2. **Automating Granularity Selection** : For methods like MoG, automating the process of granularity selection could enhance flexibility and reduce the need for manual intervention, making the system more adaptable to new domains.
3. **Enhancing Metadata Generation** : Improving the generation of metadata and synthetic QA pairs, possibly through more advanced NLP techniques, could enhance the performance of methods like PR3 in less structured domains.
