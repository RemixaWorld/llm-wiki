## What is Information Retrieval?

> Information Retrieval is organizing and ranking documents, articles, or web pages based on how relevant they are to what you’re looking for.

Let’s say you are searching for information on a particular topic. You enter a few keywords into the search engine, and it then generates a list of results for you. The search engine uses algorithms to determine which results are the most relevant to your query, and it ranks them accordingly. So, when you see those search engine results pages (SERPs) with the title, snippets, and links, they are all part of the Information Retrieval process.

## How do we evaluate rankings from searches/Search Engines?

Before we dive into the metrics, let’s get comfortable with some of the key terminologies used in evaluating information retrieval systems

### Actual vs. Predicted

When evaluating the performance of the IR system, we will be comparing _actual_ vs. _predicted_ conditions, where:

* **Actual condition** refers to the true label of every item in the dataset. These are _positive_ (_p_) if an item is relevant to a query or _negative_ (_n_) if an item is _ir_ relevant to a query. This is called ground truth.
* **Predicted condition** is the _predicted_ label returned by the IR system. If an item is returned, it is predicted as being _positive_ and, if it is not returned, it is predicted as _negative._

### Relevance

> Relevance is how well a piece of information addresses what you’re looking for.

Relevance can be measured in two ways: binary or graded scores.

**_Binary scoring_** is pretty straightforward. For each item, you decide if it’s relevant or not based on user actions. Did the user click on it? Relevant. Did they skip it? Not relevant. Did they like it, add it to their cart, or start listening to it? Relevant. You simply keep track of what the user does and match it with the recommended item.

**_Graded scoring_** , on the other hand, involves assigning specific values to different user actions. For example, you could give a score of “1” if the user clicks on an item, “2” if they save it as a favorite, “3” if they add it to the cart, and “4” if they buy it. This scoring system requires careful design and can be challenging to maintain in practice.

Sometimes, the grading can happen naturally. For instance, when users give 1–5 star ratings to items they buy or movies they watch, you can use those numerical scores to measure relevance.

In certain scenarios, like internet searches, human assessors may be involved in providing structured grading. They can review parts of documents for specific queries and manually assign scores to indicate how relevant they are.

You could also use user’s data to create ground truth. Let’s say you trained the movie recommendation model and using the user’s viewing history you generated a set of predictions for each user. You can compare the recommendations (movies the system expected the users to like) against the remaining watch history (what the users watched outside of the training data). In this case, the record of the movies users watched later provides the “ground truth” for the model evaluation.

### Relevance Judgments:

> Relevance judgments, also referred to as **_qrels_** , are essentially a collection of triples (q, d, r), where ‘r’ represents a relevance judgment. This judgment is a human-made annotation on a pair of query ‘q’ and document ‘d’.

In simpler terms, these relevance judgments are the human-created labels that categorize a document’s relevance to a specific query. These judgments are typically stored in text files and can be downloaded as part of a test collection, serving as a sort of “ground truth”. .

The judgment ‘r’ can be a binary variable indicating whether a document is relevant or not to the query. However, it’s not uncommon to use a three-tier scale of ‘not relevant’, ‘relevant’, and ‘highly relevant’. For web search, a five-point scale is often used (perfect, excellent, good, fair, and bad) often abbreviated as PEGFB. These non-binary judgments are known as graded relevance judgments, where ‘graded’ implies a position on a scale of ranks or qualities.

Relevance judgments play a dual role. They help train ranking models in a supervised setting and also evaluate these models. Although it may seem strange to those familiar with applied machine learning, this dual role mirrors the training, development, and testing split of a dataset. Historically, information retrieval test collections were not large enough to train ranking models beyond simple parameter tuning. However, the release of datasets like MS MARCO has provided enough relevance judgments for training models in a supervised setting. Hence, in this discussion, we use the terms relevance judgments, test collections, and training data almost interchangeably.

### Top-K parameter:

When it comes to ranking quality evaluation, you typically need to define the **K parameter.** The “K” represents the number of top recommendations (ranked items) you want to assess.

## Evaluation metrics:

Organizations often use offline and online metrics to measure the performance of their IR systems. The offline metrics are used specifically to predict the system’s performance _before deployment_. The online metrics on the other hand are used _after deployment_ to evaluate the model’s performance after deployment to understand user reactions to the search results or recommendations.

## Offline metrics:

Offline metrics are measured in a controlled, isolated environment before deploying a new Information Retrieval (IR) system. These metrics provide insights into the system’s performance without exposing it to real-world traffic or user interactions. The focus here is on assessing whether the system returns a specific set of relevant results based on predefined queries or test datasets. Offline evaluation allows us to determine how well the system retrieves relevant items compared to ground truth data, often using metrics like precision, recall, and F1 score.

### **Precision@K**

Precision@K measures how accurate our retrieved results are. It’s all about measuring how many of the top K items we found are actually relevant to what we’re looking for. Precision doesn’t care about the order of the results, it simply focuses on the number of relevant ones. So, to calculate precision, we take the number of relevant items within the top K results and divide it by K.

```python from ranx import Qrels, Run, evaluate, compare#actual output or ground truth (created by experts)qrels_dict = { 'Colorful pet birds' : { 'African_love_birds' : 1, 'Sun_conure' : 1, 'Budgies': 1, 'Monk_parrot' : 1, 'Macaw' : 1, 'Indian_Blue_Ring_neck_parrot' : 1, },}#predicted output (predicted by model)run_dict = { 'Colorful pet birds' : { 'African_love_birds' : 0.95, 'Cocktails' : 0.9, 'Sun_conure' : 0.8, 'Finches' : 0.7, },}qrels = Qrels(qrels_dict)run = Run(run_dict)results=evaluate(qrels, run, ['precision@4'])print(results) ```

### **Recall@K:**

**Recall@K** metric helps us understand how well we’re doing at retrieving all the relevant items from a given dataset. Recall doesn’t pay attention to the order of the results, it’s order unaware. It solely focuses on the number of relevant items we managed to find. To calculate recall, we divide the number of relevant items within the top K results by the total number of relevant items in the entire dataset. It gives us a sense of how effectively we’re capturing all the relevant information.

**Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)**

Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) metric helps us understand how well a system performs in finding relevant results and placing them at the very top. It focuses on the order of the first relevant result, rather than the number or order of other relevant results. To calculate the MRR@K, we take the reciprocal rank (RR) of the first relevant result for each query (in the case of information retrieval) or user (in the case of recommendations), and then average them across the evaluated dataset. It gives us a measure of how consistently the system finds relevant results as the top result.

**Mean Average Precision (MAP)**

Mean Average Precision (MAP) metric evaluates how well a system can return relevant items in the top K results while prioritizing more relevant items at the very top. To calculate the MAP@K, we average the average precision at K across multiple queries (in information retrieval) or users (in recommendations) in the evaluated dataset. It helps us understand the system’s ability to provide accurate and relevant results, giving higher importance to the most relevant items.

The average precision@K (AP@K) metric measures the precision values at all the relevant positions within K and averages them.

## Online metrics:

Online evaluation revolves around analyzing user behavior through log data to gauge system performance in real-time. It measures how real users interact with an Information Retrieval (IR) system in a natural setting. Instead of depending only on controlled tests or expert judgments, it focuses on implicit signals like clicks, dwell time, and other behaviors that users naturally exhibit. Unlike offline evaluations, which often use predefined relevance judgments, online evaluation offers a more realistic view by capturing actual user experiences.
Online evaluation is often paired with A/B testing, that compares two versions of the same application — variant ‘A’ and variant ‘B’ — through a randomized experiment. The goal is to determine which version leads to better user engagement or conversions. By splitting users into different segments for the test, we can measure the impact of specific changes on user behavior.

When it comes to evaluating an Information Retrieval (IR) system, the granularity can vary. Some might focus on the overall quality of the ranking system (list level) or zoom in on how well individual documents meet user needs for a specific query (result level). The choice of approach depends on the specific questions we want to answer about system performance and user experience.

## Document level: Are documents returned by this system relevant?

### **Click-through rate (CTR):**

> Click-through rate (CTR) is a metric used to measure the average number of times a document is clicked when it appears on the Search Engine Results Page (SERP) for a particular query.

The CTR metric is calculated by dividing the number of clicks a document receives by the number of times it is shown on the SERP. For example, if a document is shown 100 times and receives 10 clicks, the CTR would be 10%.

However, it’s important to note that the data collected for CTR can be noisy and biased. One major factor contributing to this bias is the position of the document on the SERP. Documents that appear higher on the page tend to receive more clicks compared to those lower down. This bias can skew the CTR metric and make it less accurate in representing the true engagement of users.

### Dwell time

> Dwell time is a user experience metric that measures the length of time a user spends looking at a webpage after they have clicked a link on a SERP (Search Engine Results Page) before they return.

In the context of improving search quality, dwell time helps to estimate how satisfying the click was for the user. If a user spends more time on the page, it’s generally assumed they found the content helpful or interesting, which is a good indication of a high-quality document.

A common cutoff for dwell time is 30 seconds, based on log analysis results. However, it’s worth noting that the “satisfaction” point can vary depending on the nature of the content. For example, a user might spend longer on a detailed research article compared to a short blog post.

Below is a simple Python code example to calculate dwell time.

```python import pandas as pd# Assuming df is your DataFrame and it has a column 'entry_time' and 'exit_time'# Convert them to datetime if they are not alreadydf['entry_time'] = pd.to_datetime(df['entry_time'])df['exit_time'] = pd.to_datetime(df['exit_time'])# Calculate dwell timedf['dwell_time'] = (df['exit_time'] - df['entry_time']).dt.total_seconds()# Filter dwell times that are longer than 30 secondssatisfied_clicks_df = df[df['dwell_time'] >= 30] ```

## Ranking level: How good is this system for an average query?

### **Click rank:**

> Click Rank measures the position of the clicked document in the search results. The logic behind this metric is simple.

If a user clicks on a document that is higher up in the search results (i.e., it has a lower click rank), it’s assumed that the IR system has performed well.

For example, if a user clicks on the first result, the click rank is 1. If they click on the third result, the click rank is 3, and so on. Therefore, a lower mean click rank indicates better performance of the IR system since it suggests that users are generally clicking on results that appear higher up.

However, this metric has its limitations. If you are comparing two ranking systems, one with few relevant documents and the other with many, a low mean click rank may lead to incorrect conclusions. This is because a system with fewer relevant results may have a lower mean click rank simply because there are fewer options for the user to click on, not necessarily because the system is performing better.

A variant of click rank is the reciprocal rank, which is the inverse of the click rank. For instance, if a user clicks on the first result, the reciprocal rank is 1/1 = 1. If they click on the third result, the reciprocal rank is 1/3 ≈ 0.33. The higher the reciprocal rank, the better the performance of the IR system.

Here’s a simple Python code example to calculate click rank and reciprocal rank.

```python import pandas as pd# Assuming df is your DataFrame and it has a column 'click_position'df['click_rank'] = df['click_position']# Calculate reciprocal rankdf['reciprocal_rank'] = 1 / df['click_rank'] ```

### CTR@k

> CTR@k is a metric that measures the ratio of clicks to impressions (the number of times a link is shown) within the top k positions in a list of search results.

For example, if we have CTR@10, it means we’re considering only the first 10 positions in the search result. If 5 out of those 10 results are clicked, the CTR@10 would be 5/10 = 50%.

CTR@k is useful for understanding user engagement with a search results page. A higher CTR@k indicates that users are finding the search results within the top k positions more relevant or attractive.

### pSkip

> pSkip is a metric that measures the probability of a user skipping over any result and clicking on a result that is lower in the search rankings.

In other words, it gives an idea of how often users bypass the top results to click on lower-ranked ones.

It’s a more advanced variant of the click position metric, and it provides more insight into user behavior and the relevance of search results. If the probability of skipping is low, it suggests that users are finding what they need at the top of the search results, indicating a high-quality search ranking.

Here’s a simple Python code example to calculate pSkip using a pandas DataFrame:

```python import pandas as pd# Assuming df is your DataFrame and it has a column 'click_position'# representing the position of the clicked result in the search results# Calculate total number of clickstotal_clicks = df['click_position'].count()# Calculate number of clicks that are not at the first positionskipped_clicks = df[df['click_position'] > 1].count()# Calculate pSkippSkip = skipped_clicks / total_clicks ```

### Time to click

> Time to click is a metric that measures the overall time from when the Search Engine Results Page (SERP) is shown to the user, to when the user interacts with the Information Retrieval (IR) system by clicking on a result.

It provides insights into how quickly users are able to find relevant information on the SERP.

There are different variants of this metric. For instance, ‘time to first click’ measures the time it takes for a user to click on any result after the SERP is displayed. On the other hand, ‘time to last click’ refers to the time from when the SERP is displayed to when the user makes their last click on the SERP.

Generally, the shorter the time to click, the better the performance of the IR system. This is because a shorter time to click suggests that users are finding relevant results quickly, indicating that the search results are well-matched to their queries.

Here’s a simple Python code example to calculate time to first click and time to last click

```python import pandas as pd# Assuming df is your DataFrame and it has columns 'serp_time' and 'click_time'# Convert them to datetime if they are not alreadydf['serp_time'] = pd.to_datetime(df['serp_time'])df['click_time'] = pd.to_datetime(df['click_time'])# Calculate time to first clickdf['time_to_first_click'] = df.groupby('user_id')['click_time'].transform('min') - df['serp_time']# Calculate time to last clickdf['time_to_last_click'] = df.groupby('user_id')['click_time'].transform('max') - df['serp_time'] ```

## Closing thoughts:

In conclusion, evaluating an Information Retrieval System (IRS) using both offline and online metrics is important for understanding its performance and effectiveness. Offline metrics provide valuable insights into the system’s internal workings and can help assess its efficiency, accuracy, and robustness. These metrics, such as precision, recall, and F1 score, are based on predefined test collections and can provide a comprehensive understanding of how well the IRS performs.

On the other hand, online metrics offer a more user-centric evaluation by considering real-time user interactions with the system. Metrics like click-through rate, dwell time, and conversion rate provide insights into user satisfaction, engagement, and success in finding relevant information. Online metrics capture the dynamic nature of user behavior and can help identify areas for improvement, user preferences, and overall system performance in a real-world setting.

By combining offline and online metrics, a more comprehensive evaluation of an IRS can be obtained. Offline metrics provide a solid foundation for assessing the technical aspects of the system, while online metrics offer a more user-oriented perspective. By analyzing both sets of metrics, system developers can gain a deeper understanding of the IRS’s strengths and weaknesses and make informed decisions for system enhancement.
