Recently, I tried to figure out which of the many GenAI product ideas that come to my mind are actually worth exploring. Initially, I wrote several versions of prompts for ChatGPT, asking for the reasons behind the success and failure of contemporary AI products across a broad spectrum (both B2C and B2B).

[Here’s the response from GPT-4o](<https://chatgpt.com/share/a08c7c15-726b-4dd8-b96b-395b80a96edd>) that I found to be the best of the three. If you’re satisfied with this answer, feel free to stop reading here ;-)

However, this superficial answer hardly addresses the distinctive features of **generative** AI. The reason is obvious: the topic is new, and there was insufficient data on the success factors of GenAI products used in GPT-4o’s training.

In such cases, conventional Google research proves more effective. Here are the long-reads I found most compelling:

1. [Winning the AI Products Arms Race](<https://www.reforge.com/blog/ai-products-arms-race#the-ai-survival-curve-where-lucrative-opportunities-live-93801b235d73>) by Aniket Deosthali. The author explores not only GPT-based but also traditional AI products, offering an in-depth analysis from various perspectives. His ‘AI Survival Curve’, based on the Consideration x Context framework, is particularly noteworthy.
2. [Which AI product ideas are worth exploring?](<https://www.visma.com/blog/which-ai-product-ideas-are-worth-exploring/>) This piece examines selecting potentially successful GenAI product ideas based on technical feasibility. I especially liked the clear definition of AI performance, which forms the basis of their analysis. High performance is indeed the key to success.

Both articles highlight the **lack of quality data (context)** as a reason for the failure of many AI products. No doubt that “The better the data you feed the LLM, the better the output will be”. Nevertheless, many tasks can be tackled using GenAI without requiring **large** amounts of data. Just a prompt and a small file are often sufficient for GenAI to solve the task with high quality.

> _The fact that GPT models are_** _pretrained_** _is a huge advantage over traditional AI, which always requires training for a specific class of tasks. This is an advantage we need to leverage._

## What You Will Find Below

I offer an alternative perspective on which LLM-powered products are worth developing.

* My assumption: many product ideas can be implemented **without** a **large** amount of quality data (as noted above).
* I also do NOT consider other **technological** constraints that may hinder product quality.

> _Instead, I focus on_** _user behavior_** _. My analysis also draws more from the_** _unique features of LLMs_** _rather than patterns common to all innovative products._

The question “What GenAI products are worth developing?” is incredibly broad, even if we exclude GPT-based products without LLMs (TTS, STT, diffusion transformers, etc.). Therefore:

1. I’ve divided the answer into two parts. The first part, discussed in this article, focuses on **user attitudes** towards products. The [second part](</genai-squared-how-can-a-product-avoid-the-downfall-of-most-llm-driven-startups-183619ab7883>) is devoted the business analysis of product ideas, including **competition** aspects.
2. In both articles, I only consider two business models: **B2C and B2B2C**. This means I exclude B2B products and in-house developments; such products are more closely tied to company needs and policies than to the needs of end users, making their survival dependent on different factors.

I address the following questions related to software products (**end-user applications**) based on large language models:

* How is the success of a GenAI application linked to users’ tolerance for errors?
* What features of a specialized AI copilot enable it to compete with popular AI chatbots like ChatGPT?
* How can we overcome users’ natural reluctance to adapt their habits to new technologies?
* What should a ‘whole’ (complete) LLM-powered product look like?
* What is important for GenAI products to “cross the chasm” besides completeness?

So, let’s examine two types of applications that are likely to fail 🚫 and two types that have a high chance of success ✅.

## 1\. Applications With High Quality Standards or Costly Quality Monitoring May Fail 🚫

LLM outcomes are unpredictable and difficult to evaluate:

[Slides by Anyscale](<https://speakerdeck.com/anyscale/evaluating-llm-applications-is-hard>), December, 2023

Even if users initially rate the product highly, a different user group (or the same users in different contexts) may evaluate the product very differently. Moreover, as the knowledge base expands, the results can degrade in the same scenarios where they were previously good enough. Such degradation can occur even when switching to a supposedly “higher quality” LLM.

> _Therefore, monitoring the performance (quality) of an LLM-based application is crucial for its success._

A product may be unsuccessful if its performance cannot be monitored by humans, and model-graded evaluation does not provide enough error prevention. (Actually, it is rare to find scenarios where LLM can be trusted to monitor the quality of LLM outputs.)

In fact, it depends on the users’ tolerance for errors:

* You can apply model-graded evaluation or go completely without monitoring if the quality of the results is significantly higher than the users’ tolerance for errors.
* There is no point in making LLM-based applications in areas where error tolerance is high (for example, if 1% error rate is deemed unacceptable). However, using LLM can still be justified in this case, if the product is profitable despite the cost of personnel required to fix LLM errors.
* An intermediate scenario is as follows. Error tolerance is roughly on par with the current application’s performance. Then, human intervention is still necessary, but it should come from the end users, not the employees of the development company. The next section is devoted to this topic.

## 2\. Specialized Copilots Are in Demand ✅

LLMs lack systems thinking and a comprehensive understanding of broader contexts. They often struggle with rare situations that fall outside data and the system prompt’s coverage. Therefore, LLM outputs need human review (having model-graded evaluation in mind as discussed earlier). Ideally, users of the product should serve as human evaluators.

> _However, for this to be effective, users should not have unrealistic expectations regarding output quality. Users must view AI as an assistant rather than an external provider obligated to deliver a guaranteed quality service._

These considerations have led the market to the concept of **AI copilots**. While some might equate this term with AI assistants, I lean towards the [perspective](<https://www.blobr.io/post/ai-chat-ai-assistant-ai-copilot-ai-sidekick-guide-ai-definition>) that copilots represent an advanced subset of assistants. Copilots don’t just inform and advise; they’re capable of executing intricate tasks within the product they’re designed for.

Most well-known copilots are quite versatile, as they are built into multi-purpose products that function more as platforms than applications. For instance, Github is a ‘platform’ for all programmers, Microsoft Office 365 is a ‘platform’ for all office workers and knowledge professionals, and they both have copilots. Copilots are also developing for operating systems like Windows or iOS.

Despite the seemingly saturated copilot market, I believe there’s still enough room for new products to emerge. However, startup-developed copilots shouldn’t aim to be as versatile as giants like GitHub Copilot X or Shopify Sidekick.

> _The true potential lies in_** _specialized_** _copilots that can assist professionals in specific fields_** _far more effectively_** _than any general-purpose AI assistant. This is not easy thing to do, but it’s worth it._

Consider a copilot tailored for trainers (experts, course creators). Such a tool should outperform ChatGPT in enhancing course quality while significantly reducing the effort required:

* It should reflect the unique expertise and teaching style of the trainer.
* When the goal extends beyond session planning to include learning materials and student assignments, the copilot should be capable of generating these in various formats, not just text.
* To truly streamline the process, users shouldn’t need to rely on copy-pasting the copilot’s results. Instead, the copilot should seamlessly integrate with LMS (Learning Management Systems), messengers, or other course delivery tools.

To effectively compete with tech giants developing universal AI assistants and embedding them into their platforms, specialized copilots must offer a **substantially higher** value proposition to their target user groups. The benefits should be not just incremental, but transformative.

The rationale behind this statement is in the following section.

## 3\. Marginal Effort-Saving Apps Don’t Cut It 🚫

While generative AI-powered applications promise innovative user experiences, we must acknowledge a reality: most users are resistant to changing their habits, whether consciously or subconsciously. I believe that the advantages of “conversational UX design” are exaggerated, while LLM-based “personalization” and “adaptation to user needs” can be unpredictable and annoying.

Consider the interactions in many LLM-powered applications: users are required to input text or voice commands, often engaging in long chats. This **chat-based interface** , while novel, can be challenging for typical users accustomed to simple point-and-click interactions. The **voice** chat offers a partial solution of this problem, but many users find it even more difficult than the text chat.

Another hurdle lies in the iterative improvements required to get quality LLM outputs. This isn’t necessarily due to LLM imperfections, but rather stems from users gradually refining their requirements. To understand why this is a problem, let’s compare the old and new workflows:

* **Traditional method** (without an assistant): Users can instantly incorporate new ideas or requirements directly into their draft, as it’s easy to localize edits for the draft author.
* **New “human + assistant” process** : Each new requirement triggers a complete regeneration of the draft, with changes scattered unpredictably throughout. This shift can be uncomfortable, as it requires users to spend more time reading and reviewing than actively writing. Of course, many writers prefer crafting to reading content.

> _Given these challenges, new AI applications must offer truly compelling benefits to gain widespread user acceptance._

Mere incremental time savings, e.g. in the range of 10–30%, is not a sufficient incentive to adopt the application. This stems from our innate resistance to changing established habits. To overcome this inertia, the efficiency gains need to be transformative — not just marginal improvements, but **multifold** reductions in effort.

Moreover, it’s better to go beyond just saving time. Imagine an application that empowers users to become the person they’ve always aspired to be. For instance, an AI-powered app could transform someone with limited presentation skills into an influential thought leader.

## 4\. Applications “Smartly” Integrating LLMs into Familiar Workflows Can Cross the Chasm ✅

Truly effective applications combine LLMs with other features to create a ‘**whole product** ’ — a concept popularized by Geoffrey Moore in his book, [Crossing the Chasm](<https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/crossing-the-chasm-en>). He pointed out that only whole products which address user needs end-to-end rapidly gain traction among mainstream users (the majority). In contrast, core products (or ‘generic products’) primarily appeal to innovators and early adopters who enjoy assembling complete solutions on their own.

>  _Creating an intelligent_** _whole product_** _typically requires a ‘smart’ approach to integrating AI capabilities. This means seamlessly blending LLM functionalities with other features within the application or even bridging gaps between multiple applications._

For example, let’s consider Personal Knowledge Management (PKM) tools like Obsidian. These are typical **core** products, primarily attracting tech-savvy users who don’t mind tinkering with plugins and integrations.

Nevertheless, PKM has broader potential, particularly for content creators and bloggers who face the permanent challenge of generating fresh material regularly, even when they lack inspiration to do so. Therefore, focusing on this broader audience, a PKM tool could evolve into a whole product by incorporating AI-driven features. Imagine an intelligent system that analyzes your personal knowledge base, proactively suggesting ideas for articles, videos, or social media posts. Next, the tool could engage in a collaboration with you to generate the complete polished material.

* Certainly, some users could achieve similar outcomes by integrating their knowledge base with universal AI solutions like TextCortex or NotebookLM. However, this DIY approach falls short of a complete product.
* Such solutions often prove cumbersome: you’re adding knowledge to your base in one place while drawing insights in another.
* Moreover, such setups typically require text queries — a time-consuming process with high cognitive load. In contrast, a well-designed application could trigger insights at the push of a button, on a schedule, or based on specific events. This kind of UX design does not require much mental effort.

Thus, simplicity, ease of use, and product completeness are crucial factors in crossing the ‘chasm’ between early adopters and the mainstream market. These criteria can be met by **integrating LLM capabilities into a product that users already know and rely on**.

However, not every integrated solution makes sense. I believe that the most successful “smart” AI integrations are those that seamlessly blend into existing workflows, requiring users to **do** **nothing new** to benefit from AI capabilities. This means no new buttons to press, no new options to select — the AI simply enhances the user’s current experience.

1. As a **good example** , let’s consider an educational app where users simply click a **familiar** “Next” button to move on to the next topic or assignment. Behind the scenes, this action might occasionally trigger AI-generated personalized content or assignments. Students may not even notice this, but they are happy how well the course suits their needs.
2. In contrast, there is a widespread **anti-pattern** of AI integration: the standalone chat assistant that pops up in a corner of the app UI with almost no relation to the main app. While seemingly helpful, such a feature does not create a competitive advantage, i.e. it can’t become a deciding factor in product selection.
3. And here’s another example of an AI-integrated solution that is **better than the 2nd, but still worse than the 1st** , if we look at the user adoption. AI actions are often integrated into existing apps by placing new options in familiar menus. It’s not a perfect solution as users often hesitate to explore unfamiliar options, so the adoption rate for these new AI features remains under 20–40%.

## 5\. New GenAI Products Are Better Suited to B2B2C than B2C

Let’s consider another scenario where you’re starting **from scratch** , without an existing revenue-generating product to enhance with LLM integration (as discussed earlier). In my opinion, launching a new B2C product with LLM as its core feature faces big obstacles. Here are two critical reasons among many:

1. **Data and privacy.** LLMs require high-quality data to deliver meaningful outcomes. In a B2C context, this presents a significant challenge. Using customer data raises privacy concerns among many B2C customers. Another option is using developer-supplied data, and it requires a vast, diverse dataset to cater to various user needs. This dramatically increases product development costs and complexity.
2. **Big Tech’s insurmountable advantage**. Tech giants like Meta, Google, and Apple have a near-unbeatable edge in the B2C market. Even if they enter some niche with LLM-powered product later than a startup, their existing user base of hundreds of millions provides an enormous advantage, and their platforms are where users already spend their digital lives. This scale is impossible for startups to match, even with huge marketing investments.

These challenges are significantly mitigated in a well-designed **B2B2C** application that empowers other companies (particularly SMBs) to swiftly develop valuable end-to-end products for B2C markets:

1. Data and privacy. In B2B2C, quality data can come from the B2B clients rather than individual B2C customers. These businesses have stronger incentives to address privacy concerns, as we’ll explore further.
2. Competition by leveraging existing customer bases. Ideally, each B2B client already possesses its own customer base. The LLM-powered product’s role is to help these B2B clients either boost profitability from their customers or reduce the cost of service delivery.

It’s worth noting that **B2B** products built on local LLMs can also sidestep these issues. However, B2B solutions are beyond the scope of this article.

In addition, a B2C product must be truly massive to succeed, so it should not require users to have specific qualifications or high motivation. This contradicts the typical weaknesses of LLM-based applications which often bring unfamiliar and complicated user experiences (as discussed in section 3).

It is easier to overcome these weaknesses when users have relatively high motivation. In the B2C case, this typically applies to only 10–20% of users — innovators and early adopters — making widespread adoption unlikely (as discussed in section 4).

> _In the B2B2C case, more potential users are highly motivated, since it is not just about saving their effort, but about increasing their profits._

BTW while B2B clients can interact with LLMs, the end users they serve can still enjoy a **familiar** user experience.

## Summary

Products leveraging LLMs may face challenges with output quality and inevitably encounter difficulties in quality assessment (refer to section 1). The feasibility of addressing or working around these issues is closely tied to users’ error tolerance. In particular, users of **AI copilots** tend to have a higher tolerance, so significant investment in the development of such copilots can be justified (see section 2 for details).

For developers of **established** products, it is beneficial to increase the value of the product through LLM integration, **while preserving the familiar user experience**. This approach enables the AI functionality to successfully “cross the chasm” and gain widespread adoption (explore section 4 for more insights).

Nevertheless, maintaining the conventional user experience isn’t always feasible, nor is it necessarily required for **novel** products. An LLM-powered application might feature a “chat interface” that challenges users to modify their habits. In such scenarios:

* The AI must offer transformative benefits to the user (see section 3 for more), **or**
* The product should follow B2B or **B2B2C** model rather than B2C. In this case, B2B clients become the primary users of the GenAI features, as they incentivize their teams to boost profitability (see section 5).

Of course, it is impossible to describe all the factors of products success and failure within a single piece, even when narrowing the scope to one technology (LLM) and two business models (B2C, B2B2C).
