It took me 10 months of hard work to finally get publishable results on this AI project.

It all started with a simple goal of making LLMs hallucinate less in my internship last summer.

And 10 months later, I had some pretty insane results.

There are a lot of ways you can change the behaviour of an LLM, but the most overused method is fine-tuning.

The main problem with fine-tuning is that it takes way too much time and GPU resources to train an LLM, and requires a lot of clean data to even see minor improvements.

Instead, I worked on developing a radically different approach, where I was ultimately able to show performance improvements only at inference time with ZERO training.

## Introduction

The goal here was to make LLMs hallucinate less whenever a RAG context was provided to it.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

On the left, the LLM answers correctly as per the context, while on the right, the LLM answers from its training. (Image by author)

To get the LLM to behave like this, I implemented a method called activation steering. If you want to understand some of the fundamentals behind that method, I’d encourage you to check out this blog:

## [Understanding “steering” in LLMsAnd how simple math can solve global problems.ai.gopubby.com](</understanding-steering-in-llms-96faf6e0bee7?source=post_page-----1455e1539ec0--------------------------------------->)

Through activation steering, we hope to “steer” an LLM’s behaviour so that it behaves however we want it to.

I’ll give you a simple example.

Let’s say you want to make an LLM output “enthusiastic” answers to your questions.

That means if I ask it who the founder of Apple is, it should say something like _“Apple is such an amazing company founded by the talented Steve Jobs!”_ instead of _“Um, Apple was founded by Steve Jobs I guess”_.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

(Image by author)

How would you make it behave in this way?

1. First, you would collect several such “enthusiastic” prompts and several “unenthusiastic” prompts and pass each prompt independently into the LLM.
2. For each prompt, you would extract the activation vectors at a specific intermediate layer in the LLM.
3. Next, you would average all the “enthusiastic” vectors and call it _E_ , and average the “unenthusiastic” vectors and call it _UE_.
4. Finally, you would compute the steering vector (_E_ — _UE_) and add that during inference every time a new token is being computed.

Making an LLM more enthusiastic. (Image by author)

Okay that’s all great. But now, I’ll show you how I apply the same idea to our problem.

## My Approaches

To apply activation steering to the problem of reducing hallucinations in LLMs in RAG scenarios, we do something similar.

We create one “context-sticking” prompt and one “non-context-sticking” prompt, extract the activations and generate the steering vector.

On the left, the LLM answers correctly as per the context, while on the right, the LLM answers from its training. (Image by author)

I worked on two approaches to constructing these prompts which turned out well— the “options approach” and the “contrastive approach”.

### 1\. The Options Approach

In order to generate these two types of prompts, I create a prompt that’s structured like an MCQ question.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

A prompt structured as an MCQ question. (Image by author)

In the prompt, one option represents what I call the “context” answer — the correct answer as per the context. The other answer is called the “memory” answer — the correct answer as per how the LLM was likely trained.

Now we create two versions of this prompt, with the only difference being the last letter. In one version, the prompt ends with an _A_ , while in the other, the prompt ends with a _B_.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Two versions of the prompt, one ending in A and the other ending in B. (Image by author)

Note that the _A_ and the _B_ are a part of the LLM’s answer; we’re sort of inducing the LLM to continue a response that starts with an _A_ or a _B_.

Thus, based on the final letter, some of these prompts will be “context-sticking” prompts while other prompts will be “non-context-sticking” prompts.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The context-sticking vector points from non-context-sticking prompts to context-sticking prompts. (Image by author)

Just like before, we use these prompts to extract activation vectors and generate a context-sticking steering vector which can be added at inference to improve context-sticking behaviour.

(I’ll tell you the results in a bit)

### 2\. The Contrastive Approach

We earlier didn’t think much of this approach but it ended up performing even better than the options approach.

What I did:

I created a prompt consisting of a context and a question, and create two variations of the prompt, one with the context and one without the context.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

A pair of prompts, where one contains the context and the other does not. (Image by author)

The intuition here was that in the former case, the LLM is incited to use the contextual information to answer the question, while in the latter case, the LLM answers the question without any extra information.

Meaning that the prompts represent two poles of the “context-sticking” behaviour and should be just fine to help us generate a steering vector.

I extracted the activation vectors and used them to generate the steering vector just like before, and the results were quite surprising.

## The Results

Without applying any steering, the LLM answered correctly on my test dataset about 50% of the time.

With the options approach, I got that number up to **70%** , and with the contrastive approach, **that number went up to 75%.**

Furthermore, the cosine similarity between the contrastive vector and the options vector was quite low.

Does this mean that two distinct directions were able to improve LLM performance in the same behaviour?

## Conclusion

In this blog, we talked about two approaches to crafting prompts that allow us to generate two distinct steering vectors, and use these vectors to steer our LLM towards a desirable behaviour.

I tried both of these methods and was surprised to see how two very distinct vectors were both able to get significant improvements in performance.

Some limitations:

* While I evaluates the options approach on 3 different models, I only evaluated the contrastive approach on one model. It would probably be interesting to see how robust the latter is.
* I used the NQ-SWAP dataset with about 750 examples to evaluate the method. While 750 is a decent number, it might make sense to evaluate on a diverse set of datasets and use more examples.

But what’s exciting about this is the fact that you can generate a vector in multiple ways, get very different vectors in the process, and still improve LLM performance significantly.
