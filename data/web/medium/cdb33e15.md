# Do you see what I see?

## Comparing brain and AI representations with Representational Similarity Analysis

Going for a walk might appear like an effortless activity, but inside your brain billions of neurons are firing activity patterns that allow you to make sense of the outside world. For instance, your brain needs to make sense of the visual scene by detecting the edges of the sidewalk, the shapes of objects, and potential obstacles like moving cars in your path. It needs to understand the sounds surrounding you by parsing the pitch, rhythm, and, ultimately, the meaning of words spoken nearby. It needs to identify landmarks, checkpoints, and their relative positions to build a mental map of your route. The patterns of neuronal activity encoding these and many other aspects of the outside world are called internal representations and are the means by which your brain internalises the information you are perceiving to effectively process it and direct follow-up behaviour.

Representations also play a crucial role in the effectiveness of deep artificial neural networks (ANNs) powering cutting-edge AI systems. Perhaps the most distinguishable feature of this revolutionary paradigm is its ability to learn useful representations from data to solve the task at hand. For example, a network can learn to encode features like edges, shapes, and textures from images. It can then learn to compose these simple features into more complex ones like faces or vehicle parts in order to recognize the objects in the images. Analogous to how it is done by neuronal activity in the brain, these features are encoded in the patterns of activations of the network’s computational units along the sequential layers of processing by which its input is transformed into new representations that allow the network to accomplish its goal.

Given the conceptual resemblance with which ANNs and the brain encode internal representations and the growing ability of AI systems to replicate (and surpass) human-like performance across tasks in various domains, it is natural to wonder if the representations learned by the layers of computational units in ANNs are similar to those encoded by neuron populations in the brain. Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) is a computational neuroscience approach to quantitatively answer this question.

RSA was introduced by Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Marieke Mur, and Peter Bandettini in [_Representational similarity analysis — connecting the branches of systems neuroscience_](<https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/systems-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/neuro.06.004.2008/full>). While it is not the only approach to compare representations in the brain and in computational systems, it is one of the most widely used in the academic literature because of its simplicity and flexibility. RSA has other applications, mentioned in the paper, like comparing representations across species and relating neural recordings to behaviour, but these will not be our focus. Specifically, in this article, we will delve into how RSA works, explore its role in comparing representations between neuron populations and ANNs, and discuss key factors to consider when using this method.

## The RSA approach

The core idea behind RSA is that two representations are similar if the patterns of differences in how they encode distinct stimuli are alike. Given a set of stimuli, the method summarizes a representation as a matrix that measures how dissimilarly it encodes all the different pairs of stimuli. Then, to compare different representations we can simply compare their signature dissimilarity matrices. In the rest of this section, we unpack this procedure.

To illustrate each step of RSA, we construct a very simple example. Suppose we have four stimuli consisting of images displaying a blue bar. The bar on each image has a different orientation and color intensity as shown in Figure 1.

Image by author.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:400/1*jk35eEftzUkNNWWYMNie8Q.png)

Then, let’s consider three distinct stylized populations of neurons:

1. The “Preferred Orientation” population consists of 10 neurons. Each has a distinct preferred orientation to which it maximally responds. The response decays exponentially as the stimulus orientation drifts from the neuron’s preferred one.
2. The “Threshold orientation” population consists of 5 neurons. Each responds only to stimuli with an orientation above its threshold. Once the orientation exceeds the neuron’s threshold, its activity starts to increase following an “S-shaped” logistic curve.
3. The “Preferred intensity” population. Analogous to the preferred orientation population, each of the 10 neurons in this population has a preferred color intensity. Each neuron’s response also decays exponentially as the stimulus color intensity drifts from its preferred one, independently of the orientation.

The tuning curves showing the response of each neuron in the populations to the stimulus property to which they are selective are shown in Figure 2.

![Figure 2. Tuning curves of neurons population. Each curve represents the response of a neuron in the respective population depending on the parameter (orientation or color intensity) value of the stimulus. Image by author.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*n5diQ6HVGMifapL5WO3Osw.png)

Now, to perform RSA, we first need to obtain the internal representation of the populations of interest. In the case of the brain, this is obtained by recording the activity of the populations that we suspect would encode some properties of the stimuli when exposed to them. Crucially, RSA is agnostic to the recording modality. For instance, it can compare direct measurements of neuronal electrical activity obtained via electrophysiology to correlated signals, such as the hemodynamic response measured with fMRI. In our example, since we are simulating the neurons’ responses, we can simply evaluate their tuning curves on the respective parameter value (orientation or intensity) of each of the four stimuli. These are shown in Figure 3.

Then, given each population’s responses, the next step is to summarize the representation into a dissimilarity matrix, adequately called Representational Dissimilarity Matrix (RDM). An RDM is a square matrix of size _S×S,_ where _S_ is the number of stimuli. Each entry in the matrix quantifies the dissimilarity between the representations of a pair of stimuli. For instance, the entry in the first row and second column measures the dissimilarity between the population response to the first and second stimuli. The ordering of the stimuli can be arbitrary, but an ordering based on some property, like color or orientation, can make a visual analysis of the RDM easier, especially when the set of stimuli is large.

To compute the dissimilarity values between the pairs of stimulus-evoked responses that fill the RDM, we need to choose a dissimilarity or distance metric. This is an important design choice as it can have a meaningful impact on the conclusions of the analysis. In many cases, the correlation distance is a good choice, because, as mentioned in the paper introducing RSA, “it is invariant to differences in the mean and variability of the dissimilarities.” This distance metric is computed as:_1-r,_ where _r_ is the Pearson correlation coefficient between the two sets of responses corresponding to the pair of stimuli being compared. It can range between 0, indicating the lowest dissimilarity, and 2, indicating the highest dissimilarity. Note that, by construction, the RDM will be symmetric, as long as an appropriately symmetric distance metric is used.

Following along with our example, we compute the RDM for each of the three populations described above using the correlation distance suggested in the paper. These are shown in Figure 4. Note that for the orientation-selective populations, the representations between stimuli with similar orientation are less dissimilar, whereas for the other population, stimuli with the same color intensity have a dissimilarity of 0. In this simple example, a visual inspection of the RDMs immediately shows that the two orientation-selective representations have closer dissimilarity patterns between themselves than to the population selective to color intensity.

RDMs of the three populations. Each entry in the matrix represents the dissimilarity between the representations of the respective pair of stimuli. By construction, the matrices are symmetric and have zero dissimilarity along the diagonal (since each stimulus representation is identical to itself). Image by author.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*LcFY6LUQ_ITFAmkLPqWfKw.png)

By summarizing the representations into an RDM, we abstract away details like the size of the populations, the modality used to record from them, or even if we are dealing with biological or artificial populations. In every case, we end up with an RDM representing the differences in how each population encodes the different stimuli and serving as the signature of its representations. This enables us to directly compare two sets of representations by simply comparing their RDMs, and if the RDMs are similar we consider the respective representations to be similar. In other words, we consider the representations to be similar if the differences in how they encode different stimuli are similar, or, as the RSA paper puts it, we “assess second-order dissimilarity: the dissimilarity of dissimilarity matrices”.

So, to conclude with the RSA, we perform the comparison of RDMs using a similarity metric. This metric does not need to be the same as the one used to construct the RDMs. In fact, the the RSA paper suggests using the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for this purpose, since, besides being robust to differences in the mean and variance of the variables compared, like the Pearson correlation coefficient used for the RDMs, it can also capture monotonic non-linear associations between them. Since the RDMs are symmetric, we just need to compute the similarity between the values of their upper (or lower) triangular. This will result in a single number ranging from -1 to 1 which we interpret as a score of how similarly the two populations encode the different stimuli presented to them.

In our example, we proceed in this way to obtain a similarity of 0.88 between the preferred orientation and threshold orientation populations, of -0.36 between the preferred orientation and preferred intensity populations, and of -0.43 between the threshold orientation and preferred intensity populations. These results are unsurprising, since the two populations which, by design, are responsive to the orientation of the bar encode similar representations and have a high representational similarity. Moreover, both of them have a low representational similarity with the population that responds to color intensity instead of orientation.

In a simple example like this, the conclusion was straightforward to see without going through the trouble of performing RSA, but it served to illustrate the rationale behind RSA and how to perform it. In practice, RSA is a powerful approach that can deal with more complex populations encoding more complex features from more complex stimuli.

## RSA with artificial neural networks

Now, we’ll see how RSA allows to compare representations encoded by neurons to representations encoded by computational units in ANNs. Deep ANNs are composed of multiple layers of processing. The initial layer receives the raw stimulus and maps it to a new representation, which is then passed on to the next layer. In this way, each subsequent layer transforms the representation it receives into a new one, which is forwarded to the following layer. So, each layer encodes a representation which can be examined by analyzing the activation values of its units which collectively produce the output representation.

The purpose of the transformations done across the layers of the network is to progressively build a representation that enables the network to accomplish its objective task. So, the network must be trained on a task where the stimulus properties we want to analyze are relevant. For instance, if we are interested in visual features, we should train the network on a visual task for which these features are useful and, therefore, are likely to be encoded in the network’s representations. Moreover, the representations built by deep networks are hierarchical: earlier layers tend to encode simple features which are then composed into more complex ones in later layers. For example, the encoding of simple features like edges is more likely to occur in the earlier layers of a convolutional network, whereas more complex features like faces are more likely to be encoded in the final layers.

We continue with the oriented blue bars example to showcase how we can use RSA with an ANN. For this purpose, we train a simple convolutional neural network (CNN) consisting of a convolutional layer with max pooling followed by a fully connected layer to identify the orientation of the bar in the stimuli images. However, four examples won’t suffice to properly train the network, so we generate more images of this kind, but with different orientation and color intensity values. Then, after training the network, we can inspect its representations of the four stimuli we used for the RSA example as shown in Figure 5.

![Figure 5. Activation values of the units in the final hidden layer of the CNN for the different input stimuli. Image by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1000/1*SY-_xDJcmcNv7tMeW0zndw.png)

Here, we consider only the activations of the 160 units preceding the final fully connected layer as these should contain the encoded orientation of the bar in the image required to make the final prediction, but in practice, for more complex cases, it is better to consider the activations from various different layers as it is difficult to anticipate exactly what kinds of features each layer will encode.

Now, we can proceed to construct the RDM for these representations. As described in the previous section, we compute the correlation dissimilarity across the four sets of activations corresponding to each stimulus. The resulting RDM is shown in the left panel of Figure 6. Finally, we compare this with each of the RDMs of the three neuron populations from the previous section by computing the Spearman rank correlation between the values in their respective upper (or lower) triangular. The resulting similarity scores are shown in the right panel of Figure 6.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2531/1*M9SHoOEmFdijUOTHHg-e5w.png) ![Figure 6. Left: RDM of the final hidden layer of the CNN. Right: Representational similarity between the CNN’s final hidden layer and the neurons populations. The hidden layer’s representations of bars with similar orientations are less dissimilar which translates in a higher representational similarity with the orientation-selective populations. Image by author.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*mN99xc379OAK_xTFSCjfLA.png)

The CNN has a high representational similarity with the orientation-selective populations, which indicates that they all encode similar features of the stimuli. In this case, we know that the shared feature is precisely the orientation of the bar. Notably, the population with the highest representational similarity to the CNN is the threshold orientation population. This is, most likely, because each computational unit in the CNN performs a threshold-based, non-linear computation to generate its output. However, in more complex cases one must be cautious when interpreting common computational mechanisms in RSA, since these cannot be inferred from representational similarity alone. On the other hand, the CNN has the lowest similarity with the population selective to color intensity, which indicates that the network is not encoding the color intensity as it is not required to solve its orientation prediction task.

Beyond our didactical example, there are studies using RSA in the academic literature to explore interesting similarities between biological and artificial representations. A notable example is the work by Seyed-Mahdi Khaligh-Razavi and Nikolaus Kriegeskorte in [_Deep Supervised, but Not Unsupervised, Models May Explain IT Cortical Representation_](<https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1003915>), where they compare the representations from CNNs and other computational models of the visual system with those recorded from the [inferior temporal (IT) cortex](<http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Inferior_temporal_cortex>) of humans and monkeys, which is an area that serves a primary role in visual recognition. Their main conclusion is that the final layer of a deep CNN pre-trained to perform object recognition has the highest representational similarity with both the human’s and monkey’s IT representation of the same set of images.

## Limitations of RSA and conclusion

RSA is not without its limitations. As the RSA paper warns, the method might produce unreliable results when the stimuli set is small. We can see this in the oriented blue bars example. Note (in Figure 1) that as the bar rotates around its fixed lower-left corner, its upper edge position changes accordingly (as if it were the end of a clock hand). So, a representation encoding the orientation angle would be highly correlated with a representation encoding the coordinates of the edge position, making them indistinguishable through the lens of RSA. Given their high representational similarity, we could interpret that both encode the same feature of the stimuli while in fact they do not.

We can mitigate this issue by expanding the stimuli set, for instance, by varying the position of the bar’s centre of rotation, so that we include images with equally-oriented bars on different positions of the image. RSA with this enriched stimuli set could disentangle a representation that encodes orientation from one that encodes position. In general, the more varied the stimuli set, the more effective RSA will be.

Finally, we should be careful to not overinterpret the results of RSA. As we mentioned before, a high representational similarity does not imply that the two systems being compared reached their representations by a common mechanism. In general, RSA alone cannot even determine exactly what features of the stimuli the representations share. Two representations can be correlated and yet encode different aspects of the stimuli as we explained above with the orientation and position of the bars in the example. This is a particularly insidious risk with more complex stimuli. For instance, if two representations discern equally well human faces from animal faces there are multiple features that can be used for this purpose and RSA, even with a very rich stimuli set, probably will not be able to completely distinguish the ones that the representations are considering.

Despite these caveats, RSA remains an effective, simple, and highly flexible method to compare representations from different populations of neurons, both biological and artificial. The approach relies on measuring the representational similarity of stimuli dissimilarities. It can compare representations across brain areas, recording modalities, species, and between biological and artificial networks of neurons. It enables a wide range of use cases based on understanding how different neural representations relate to each other.
