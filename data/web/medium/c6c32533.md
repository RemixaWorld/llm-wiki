LLMs (as their name states) originally aim at dealing with unstructured data in text format. But can they be applied to structured information, understand, extract and transform tabular data? The answer is “Yes, but…”. Let’s deal with it together. In this article you’ll find the answers to the three main questions:

1. How to transform tabular data before feeding to LLMs?
2. How to create context and a prompt?
3. What kind of framework should be used?

… and of course you’ll see a lot of examples!

## How to transform tabular data?

I’ll address this question based on a wonderful article by [Fang et al. “Large Language Models on Tabular Data: Prediction, Generation, and Understanding — A Survey” (2024)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.17944>) which I highly recommend for those who want to dive into the topic. The survey splits existing researches into three groups of table serialization techniques:

* text-based methods,
* embedding-based methods,
* graph-based & tree-based methods.

**Text-based serialization methods** includes a variety of approaches:

* **DFLoader** — simply use Pandas DataFrame as an input to an LLM (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **JSON** — transform your data table into a JSON format (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Sui et al. ([2023b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **Data Matrix** — transformation into a data matrix (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **Markdown** — transform the table into Markdown, that’s one of the most popular approaches (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Liu et al. ([2023e](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Zhang et al. ([2023d](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Ye et al. ([2023b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Zhao et al. ([2023d](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Sui et al. ([2023b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **X-Separated** — transform the table into X-separated values where X could be a reasonable delimiter like tab or comma (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Narayan et al. ([2022](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **Attribute-Value pairs** — transform the table into pairs like **_name:helen ; age:47_ **(Wang et al. ([2023c](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **HTML** — use transformation to HTML (Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Sui et al. ([2023c](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>); [b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* **Sentences** **—** convert the table into a text like **_“name is helen, age is 47”_**(Yu et al.([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Hegselmann et al.([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Gong et al. ([2020](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))).

Alternatively one can employ **table encoders, which were fine-tuned from pre-trained LMs** , to encode tabular data into numerical representations as the input for LLMs. There is a number of examples of using encoders built on:

* **BERT**(TAPAS (Herzig et al., 2020), TABERT (Yin et al., 2020b), TURL (Deng et al., 2022a), TUTA (Wang et al., 2021), TABBIE (Iida et al., 2021), UTP (Chen et al., 2023a)),
* or **LLMs**(UniTabPT (Sarkar & Lausen, [2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) (based on T5 and Flan-T5 models), TableGPT (Gong et al., [2020](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) (based on GPT2), TableGPT2 (Zha et al., [2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) (based on Phoenix (Chen et al., [2023b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)))).

Finally, the third but rarely used option is to **convert a table to a tree or a graph**. However, when working with sequence-to-sequence models, these structures must still be converted back to text. For Zhao et al. ([2023a](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)), after converting the table into a tree, each cell’s hierarchical structure, position information, and content was represented as a tuple and fed into GPT3.5.

**So which serialization technique to choose?**

As always in Data Science, there’s no easy answer and one must experiment to select what’s best suits the data, model and task. But there are a few considerations from other researchers:

* Singha et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) find that for fact-checking and table transformations **DFLoader** and **JSON** work the best,
* Sui et al. ([2023a](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) state for Tabular QA and FV tasks for GPT it’s better to use **HTML** or **XML** ,
* Sui et al. ([2023b](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))) also found markup languages, specifically **HTML** , outperformed X-separated formats for GPT3.5 and GPT4. Their hypothesis is that the GPT models were trained on a significant amount of web data and thus, probably exposed the LLMs to more HTML and XML formats when interpreting tables.

> I suggest to experiment with DFLoader and HTML first, though in my experience DFLoader performs great enough with powerful models. Remember also, that transformation to HTML means larger number of tokens passed to an LLM which leads to larger costs and issues with context limits.

## How to create context and a prompt?

Let’s turn to the second question related to prompt engineering.

As we pass tabular data as context in a prompt, we immediately face an issue for **context size**. Some models have short context windows and your table may not fit in it. But even it does, LLMs are inefficient in dealing with long sequences paying attention to the beginning or end of the context window mostly. And of course, more tokens means higher costs.

There’s no straightforward solution. Some researches (Herzig et al. ([2020](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)); Liu et al. ([2022c](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))) propose to naively truncate the table which has little sense in practice. Others seek to implement additional instruments to first select only relevant tables, rows, or columns. See examples at Sui et al. ([2023c](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2#bib.bib139>)), cTBLS Sundar & Heck ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2#bib.bib143>)), Dong et al. ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2#bib.bib37>)).

However there are some **useful tips regarding prompt engineering** :

* Include **additional information** about tables (schemas and statistics) (Sui et al. ([2023c](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* Use **in-context learning** (1-shot or 2-shot learning benefits the model (Chen ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))),
* Experiment with **Chain-of-Thought, Self-Consistency** techniques (e.g., Chen ([2023](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))) and role-play (Zhao et al. ([2023a](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>))).

## What kind of framework to build?

Our final question is about the modeling unit. We have a few options:

* **Numeric QA** is a framework consisting of just an LLM meaning that we will directly ask the model a question like “What is the average payment volume per transaction for American Express?”. Zhao et al. ([2023d](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.17944v2>)) claims that GPT-4 outperforms open-source models in such a framework,
* **Operation-based QA** is a framework which includes an additional step of deciding which kind of operations, tables, rows, or columns to use. An example is Chain-of-Table framework (Wang et al. ([2024](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04398>))) that we’ll discuss later,
* **Text2SQL** : SQL generation is popular in the industry, with many open-source fine-tuned models available.

In the next section we’ll compare the three approaches. Ready to get your hands dirty?

## Operation-based QA

I was curious to test Chain-of-Table framework that was proposed just a few months ago (Wang et al. ([2024](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.04398>))).

![Chain-of-Table explanation from Wang et al. (2024)](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*htxpsPuEAErg9siOaUqcXg.png)

The idea is very daunting. Once a user question is passed to the framework, it selects the most suitable operation from the operation pool. Possible operations are sorting, adding a column, selecting a row and grouping. Then the framework generates new arguments for the selected operation (like a name of a new column) and thansforms the data. Next, it checks whether next operations are required or the task is complete.

Anyway I recommend to catch up cases when the SQL query returns too large data samples to escape high costs from LLM usage.

## Conclusion

Large language models are dedicated to unstructured data and their direct application to tabular data is possible only for really simple tasks. If any kind of table manipulation (like sampling, aggregation, sorting or merging) is required, I suggest to use Text2SQL approach with one or two LLM calls depending on the task.
