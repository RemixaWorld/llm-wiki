# A Human Brain Inspired RAG Approach Has Reached The New State-of-the-Art

Human brains store immense amounts of knowledge to thrive in their environment.

New experiences continuously update this knowledge without losing track of the previous.

LLMs, despite being remarkably intelligent, lack such continuously updating long-term memory.

Researchers have developed many methods to update LLM memory, such as [Fine-tuning](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuning_\(deep_learning\)>), [Knowledge Editing](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02129>) and currently popular [Retrieval augmented generation (RAG)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401>), but these are far from perfect.

One could argue that the human brain reached this point through millions of years of evolution. Conversely, training a high-performing LLM takes only a few months.

But we don’t have a million years to wait for this magic to happen.

_How about we borrow and apply these biological insights to our current LLMs?_

A [recent research pre-print in ArXiv](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14831>) does exactly this.

These researchers developed a new framework called ‘HippoRAG’ by drawing inspiration from how the human brain’s two regions — the [Neocortex](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neocortex>) and [Hippocampus](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocampus>), [process ever-updating long-term memory](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3008780/>).

When tested on [Multi-hop question-answering](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09140>) tasks, HippoRAG outperforms the current state-of-the-art RAG methods by up to 20%.

It performs Multi-hop reasoning in a single retrieval step better than the iterative state-of-the-art [IRCoT (Interleaving Retrieval with Chain-of-Thought Reasoning)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10509>) approach while being 10–30 times cheaper and 6–13 times faster.

Here is a story in which we deep-dive into how this biologically inspired framework works and how it surpasses the current RAG approaches to emerge as the new state-of-the-art.

Let’s begin!

## Let’s Talk A Bit About RAG

RAG, or [Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11401>), is an information retrieval technique that allows an LLM to produce more accurate and up-to-date responses using private datasets specific to the use case.

RAG Visualised (Image from author’s book [‘AI In 100 Images’](<https://bamaniaashish.gumroad.com/l/visual_ai>))

RAG works by [chunking a dataset](<https://js.langchain.com/v0.1/docs/modules/data_connection/document_transformers/>) and then [indexing](<https://js.langchain.com/v0.1/docs/modules/data_connection/text_embedding/>) these chunks in a [vector database](<https://js.langchain.com/v0.1/docs/modules/data_connection/vectorstores/>) for [efficient retrieval](<https://js.langchain.com/v0.1/docs/modules/data_connection/retrievers/>) during the generation process.

Conventional RAG performance greatly suffers on important real-world tasks in Law, Medicine, and Science and on [Multi-hop question-answering](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09140>) tasks.

This is because these tasks involve integrating knowledge across different chunks.

Previous research (including [Self-Ask](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03350>) and [IR-CoT](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10509>)) addresses this problem by using multiple retrievals and LLM generation steps iteratively to join the knowledge of different chunks.

However, these approaches are still not sufficient for tasks involving **Path-finding multi-hop questions** , where information is scattered across chunks without obvious direct connections.

(This is unlike conventional Path-following multi-hop question-answering tasks where information is present in sequential chunks.)

Let’s take an example.

We want to find a Stanford professor who does Alzheimer’s research from a pool of potentially thousands of Stanford professors and Alzheimer’s researchers.

We call him Professor Thomas.

Conventional RAG will struggle with this task unless a passage mentions both aspects together.

This won’t be the case for humans who can quickly recall Dr. Rockman if they know him.

This is due to their brain’s associative memory capabilities, which are thought to be driven by their [Hippocampus](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocampus>).

And that’s where the insights to develop HippoRAG arise.

## It’s Time For ‘Hippocampal Memory Indexing Theory’

The [Hippocampal Memory Indexing theory](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3008780/>) is a well-established theory that describes how human long-term memory works.

It involves three regions of the brain, namely —

* [Neocortex](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neocortex>)
* [Parahippocampal regions (PHR)](<https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/127/5/1211/303019>)
* [Hippocampus](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocampus>)

These work together to achieve two main objectives —

* **Pattern Separation:** making sure that different experiences are stored as separate memories
* **Pattern Completion:** allowing the retrieval of full memories from partial cues

The process begins in the Neocortex when it receives sensory inputs such as sights, sounds, or other perceptual experiences.

These raw sensory inputs are processed here into more abstract features that are easier to manipulate and work with.

This is called the **Memory encoding** process.

These features are then passed through the Parahippocampal regions (PHR) to the Hippocampus.

This is where these features are indexed or stored as a unique memory and associated with each other.

Next, when a person attempts to recall a memory, the Hippocampus receives partial cues related to the original experience.

These are matched with the stored memories using the densely interconnected networks of neurons present in the [Hippocampal CA3 sub-region](<https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4318343/>).

This allows the Hippocampus to reconstruct the full memory from these partial cues, which are then routed to the Neocortex (through the Parahippocampal regions) for simulation or conscious recall.

This is called the **Memory retrieval** process.

These processes together allow the [integration of new information](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17696170/>) by changing only the Hippocampal index instead of directly updating the neocortical representations.

_What an incredible evolutionary miracle!_

## Here Comes ‘HippoRAG’

HippoRAG’s approach is centred on the processes described above.

It has two phases:

* **Offline Indexing,** which corresponds to memory encoding
* **Online Retrieval** , which corresponds to memory retrieval in human cognition

### Offline Indexing

This phase mimics how the human brain encodes memory.

It uses a strong instruction-tuned LLM that functions as an artificial Neocortex.

This LLM extracts schemaless knowledge graph (KG) triples (consisting of the subject, predicate and object) from a retrieval corpus using a one-shot prompting strategy.

This process is called [Open Information Extraction (OpenIE)](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_information_extraction>).

For example, _“Einstein was born in Germany”_ will be expressed as:

* **Subject** : _“Einstein”_
* **Predicate** : _“was born in”_
* **Object** : _“Germany”_

Note that this approach extracts noun phrases from the corpus rather than generating its dense vector representations.

This maintains a higher level of detail and the required distinction between different concepts or entities.

These knowledge graph (KG) triples form an **artificial Hippocampal index**.

Once the basic knowledge graph structure is created, a fine-tuned Retrieval encoder is used to introduce additional connections (edges) to it.

These edges are added when the [cosine similarity](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine_similarity>) between two entity representations exceeds a pre-defined threshold.

This step links similar concepts and improves graph connectivity, a process similar to the function of the Parahippocampal regions in the human brain.

### Online Retrieval

This phase mimics how the hippocampus retrieves memories using partial cues.

First, the LLM-based first extracts a set of named entities from a given query. These are called **Query-named entities**.

These entities are then linked to nodes in the knowledge graph called **Query nodes**.

Query nodes are the most relevant nodes selected by the Retrieval encoders, and they serve as partial cues for the artificial Hippocampus to complete patterns.

Reserachers also introduce a Node specificity system, which is similar to [Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tf%E2%80%93idf>).

This system assigns more weight (specificity score) to unique nodes within the dataset.

The [Personalized PageRank (PPR)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05198>) algorithm is then applied to the Query nodes weighted by their specificity score.

This algorithm is a variant of the [PageRank algorithm](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PageRank>) designed to focus on specific nodes within a graph rather than distribute attention evenly across the entire graph.

The algorithm outputs the distribution probability across the knowledge graph but with a bias towards the Query nodes.

This means that the algorithm is more likely to explore paths that are more relevant to these nodes.

By doing so, it activates related nodes and pathways in the knowledge graph that are contextually relevant to the query, similar to how the Hippocampus functions.

The output of the PPR algorithm is then used to rank the original passages in the corpus.

This guides the retrieval system in prioritizing passages that are most likely to contain the information needed to answer the query.

## How Well Does ‘HippoRAG’ Perform?

HippoRAG is evaluated on three popular multi-hop question-answering (QA) datasets:

* [MuSiQue](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00573>)
* [2WikiMultiHopQA](<https://github.com/Alab-NII/2wikimultihop>)
* [HotpotQA](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09600>)

**HippoRAG outperforms all baseline methods on single-step retrieval tasks** on these datasets, with [Recall scores](<https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10693v2>) improving by up to 20%.

**Similar performance is noted in the multi-step retrieval tasks,** especially when combined with [IRCoT](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10509>).

It is also seen that single-step HippoRAG outperforms IRCoT while being **10–30 times cheaper and 6–13 times faster** during online retrieval.

These improvements in retrieval performance lead to **better question-answering performance with HippoRAG**.

This is seen with its higher [Exact Match (EM)](<https://huggingface.co/spaces/evaluate-metric/exact_match>) and [F1 scores](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-score>) compared to baseline methods.

HippoRAG’s phenomenal performance over conventional RAG methods is due to its ability to perform **Multi-hop retrieval in a single step**.

**HippoRAG’s ability to perform single-step multi-hop retrieval** is a major advantage that allows it to solve complex questions better than conventional RAG methods (that require multiple retrieval steps).
