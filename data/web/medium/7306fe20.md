# You Don’t Need Normalization In Transformers Anymore

## A deep dive into the internals of Layer Normalization, and how a simple function called Dynamic Tanh (DyT) can replace them entirely in the Transformer architecture without any loss in performance.

Normalization layers are everywhere.

They are considered essential and irreplaceable, and all neural network architectures, including Transformers, use them as the default.

A group of researchers from Meta has [just published new research](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10622v2>) that challenges this norm.

They introduce a simple element-wise operation called **Dynamic Tanh (DyT)** , which can easily and entirely replace normalization layers in Transformers.

Experiments show that such replacements result in an architecture that **matches (even exceeds)** the performance of conventional Transformers with normalization, without requiring any hyperparameter tuning.

Similar results are observed in all kinds of experiments, ranging from recognition to generation, supervised to self-supervised learning, and computer vision to language models.

Here is a story where we take a deep dive into how Normalization works internally and how its function can be replicated and replaced by using the simple Dynamic Tanh (DyT) operation in various neural network architectures.

## To Start With, What Really Is Normalization?

Neural networks are notoriously hard to train.

During training, the distribution of inputs to each layer in a neural network can shift as the parameters in earlier layers change. This is called [Internal covariate shift](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batch_normalization#Internal_covariate_shift>).

To fix this unwanted distribution shift, a technique called **Normalization** is used, which adjusts and scales the outputs (activations) of neurons in the neural network.

Take an input tensor `x` with shape `(B,T,C)` where:

* `B` is the batch size/ number of samples
* `T` is the number of tokens in a sample
* `C` is the embedding dimension

For example, a batch of 32 sentences (`B`), each tokenized into 128 tokens (`T`), and each token represented as a 768-dimensional vector (`C`), has a shape of `(32, 128, 768)`.

It was in 2015 when researchers at Google [published a research paper on Batch Normalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03167>).

Batch Normalization ([_BatchNorm_](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batch_normalization>) or simply BN), which was primarily intended to be used in [CNNs](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolutional_neural_network>), involves computing the mean `μ` and variance `σ²` for each channel `C` indexed by `k` across both the batch (`B`) and token dimensions (`T`) as:

(_Note that we use the term “Channel” to refer to_` _C_` _when we talk about CNNs/ Vision models. It is also called “Feature dimension” in a general machine learning context and “Embedding dimension” in the context of language or sequence models._)

_BatchNorm_ soon began to be applied in [a variety of vision models](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385>) and became widely successful (the paper has been awarded the [ICML Test Of Time Award 2025](<https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/39855>)).

Following it came many other types of normalization layers, namely:

* [Instance Normalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.08022>) (in 2016)
* [Group Normalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.08494>) (in 2018)
* [Layer Normalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06450>) (in 2016) and [RMS Layer Normalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07467>) (in 2019)

As we previously discussed, the difference between each lies in how the mean and variance are calculated over the input.

While _BatchNorm_ calculates them across the batch (`B`) and token (`T`) dimensions for each channel (`C`), they are calculated in:

* _InstanceNorm_ : across tokens (`T`), for each sample (`B`) and each channel (`C`)
* _GroupNorm_ : across groups of channels (`C`) and tokens (`T`), for each sample (`B`)
* _LayerNorm_ : across all channels (`C`), for each sample (`B`) and each token (`T`)

_In the context of CNNs, LayerNorm computes these statistics across both channels (_`_C_` _) and spatial dimensions (_`_H x W_` _), for each sample (represented by_` _B_` _or_` _N_` _in the image), as shown below._

_RMSNorm_ is used today in [LLaMA](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971>), [Mistral](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06825>), [Qwen](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16609>), [DeepSeek](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437>), and [OpenELM](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14619>) series of LLMs. [GPT-2](<https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf>) on the other hand, uses _LayerNorm_.

It is seen that normalization layers [help optimize training](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11604>) and enable neural networks to achieve [faster convergence](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06878>) with [better generalization](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07085>).

Additionally, there has been a lot of work that replaces attention or convolution layers in deep neural network architectures (such as [MLP-Mixer](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01601>) and [Mamba](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752>)), but replacing normalization layers isn’t talked about much.

A natural question arises at this point: **_What do Normalization layers do internally that leads to such impressive results?_**

Let’s talk about this next.

## What Do N** _ormalization Layers Do Internally?_**

To answer this question, researchers take three different Transformer models, namely:

* a [Vision Transformer model (ViT-B)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929>) trained on the [ImageNet-1K](<https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5206848>) dataset
* a [wav2vec 2.0 Large Transformer model](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11477>) trained on the [Librispeech](<https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7178964>) audio dataset
* a [Diffusion Transformer (DiT-XL)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748>) trained on the [ImageNet-1K](<https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5206848>) dataset

All of the above models have _LayerNorm_ applied in every Transformer block and before the final linear projection.

It is clear that there is a linear input-output relationship (seen as a straight line in the plot) in the earlier _LayerNorm_ layers of all three models.

But something interesting happens when we go towards the deeper _LayerNorm_ layers.

It is seen that these plots are no longer a straight line, but rather resemble an S-shaped curve, similar to a [hyperbolic tangent (tanh) function](<https://www.medcalc.org/manual/tanh-function.php>).

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Plots of the tanh(αx) function with three different α values

This is quite strange.

One would expect that _LayerNorm_ would linearly transform the input tensor since its constituent operations (subtracting the mean and dividing by the [standard deviation](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_deviation>)) are linear operations.

_Why do we see an S-shaped curve then?_

This is because _LayerNorm_ normalizes in a per-token manner.

It linearly transforms each token’s activations.

Since different tokens in an input tensor can have different mean and standard deviation values, when the results are plotted collectively, the total input tensor is seen not to be transformed linearly.

Note how _LayerNorm_ brings the extreme values of token activations (for example, input values larger than `50` or smaller than `-50`) in line with the majority of points. This leads to the non-linear ends of the S-shaped curve.

On the other hand, smaller values (closer to zero) still contribute to the linear shape in the central part of the S-shaped curve.

This disproportional squashing effect of _LayerNorm_ on extreme values aligns with [how biological neurons function](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1514868/>) and could be one of the reasons that make normalization layers [achieve better results](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01255>) in neural network training.

_Given that LayerNorm resembles the hyperbolic tangent (tanh) function, can we replace LayerNorm with it?_

* `x` is the input tensor
* `γ` and `β` are learnable parameters (per channel/embedding dimension) that scale and shift the output

Surprisingly, DyT can be a **drop-in replacement** for _LayerNorm_ in the Attention blocks, FFN blocks, and the final normalization layer (basically everywhere in the LLM architecture).

The integration is so straightforward, with one DyT layer replacing one _LayerNorm_ layer, that **one doesn’t need to tune any hyperparameters** used in the original architectures for DyT to perform well.

## Is Dynamic Tanh Really As Good As LayerNorm?

To answer this question, researchers test out multiple Transformers-based and other modern deep architectures on a variety of tasks.

In each experiment, _LayerNorm_ or _RMSNorm_ in the original architectures are replaced with DyT layers, followed by training and testing them, without changing any hyperparameters.

Here is what the results tell.

### Performance Comparison In Vision Tasks

[ViT (Vision Transformer)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929>) and [ConvNeXt](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03545>) models of different sizes are trained on the [ImageNet-1K](<https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5206848>) dataset for this evaluation.

The results show that DyT **matches or slightly outperforms** _LayerNorm_ across both architectures and model sizes in the image classification task.

To evaluate self-supervised learning, researchers train two Vision Transformer-based architectures:

* [MAE (Masked Autoencoders)](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.06377>) that uses Reconstruction loss
* [DINO](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294>) that uses [Joint-embedding loss](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=BZ5a1r-kVsf>)

Both of these models are pretrained on ImageNet-1K without labels, and then fine-tuned with labels using a classification head.

The fine-tuning results show that DyT **consistently matches** the performance of _LayerNorm_ -based models in self-supervised learning tasks.

## Where Does Dynamic Tanh Struggle?

DyT isn’t a one-size-fits-all solution.

While the DyT layer performs well in replacing _LayerNorm/ RMSNorm_ , experiments show that it struggles to replace _BatchNorm_ in CNNs such as [ResNet-50](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385>) and [VGG19](<https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.1556>).

Press enter or click to view image in full size

ImageNet-K1 classification accuracy of BatchNorm (BN) and DyT-based ResNet-50 and VGG19 models

A reason for this could be that _BatchNorm_ layers occur more frequently in these CNNs (once with every weight layer), compared to _LayerNorm_ , which appears once per multiple weight layers in Transformers.

DyT also does not offer any speedup when [compiled or optimized](<https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/intermediate/torch_compile_tutorial.html>), compared to models with _LayerNorm_ , even when it is computationally simpler.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Inference and Training latencies for LLaMA 7B with compiled RMSNorm or DyT

Despite these limitations, DyT matches or exceeds the performance of its normalized counterparts and challenges the conventional use of normalization layers in neural networks.
