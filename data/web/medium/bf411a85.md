Understand that ALL state-of-the-art video generators often struggle with generating longer videos.

And from what we have now, the maximum length of video generation is

* 20 seconds by Sora (OpenAI).
* 16 seconds by MovieGen (Meta).
* 10 seconds by Ray2 (Luma).
* 8 seconds by Veo 2 (Google).

Things get worse when you generate different scenes with different angles or background.

But things have changed.

Just by adding some ‘magical’ layers directly to a pretrained model. It is now able to generate 1-minute-long videos that are **multi-scene** and **story-rich** in a SINGLE prompt.

This is incredibly significant, especially when the previous ‘Best Video Generators’ were only able to generate short clips of single scenes without complex stories.

So, what’s the magic behind it?

## Test-Time Training (TTT)

It is a plugin layer that can be trained during ‘**inference** ’.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

[Source Article](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05298>)

Let me put it this way.

1. We take a pre-trained video generator model.
2. Add in the TTT layers.
3. Fine-tune the entire model on some Tom & Jerry data.
4. Now, for the most crucial step. We freeze the entire model, except the TTT.

Pay attention, as the last step is what makes the model ‘real time adaptable’, therefore, capable of generating a long and consistent video. Let me give you an example to clear things up.

Say we prompt “Generate a video of Tom running on a field.” And the model has a thousand ways of generating that video.

Meaning when generating the video at second 5, the model might decide to give Tom sports shoes because the prompt says he is running. But later, at second 25, when the camera angle changes and it reinterprets the prompt, it might remove the shoes. Each second, it forms a new understanding of the prompt.

> _My example might be a bit of an exaggeration, but you get the point, videos generators are flawed at staying consistent on those little details._

However, if the model can adapt to the current prompt and previous scenes, (because sports shoes weren’t in the original prompt but were generated by the video generator) it will update itself and decide that this prompt always includes sports shoes, keeping things consistent.

It’s like making the model stick to the scene it started with. If it shows Tom wearing sports shoes in the first second, it keeps that idea going. Just like how a real artist wouldn’t suddenly change the outfit or drawing style halfway through.

This is the importance of real-time adaptation.

We will unravel how TTT works and the math behind it.

## Test-Time Training Architecture

### 1\. Pre-trained Diffusion Transformer (CogVideo-X 5B)

Theoretically, TTT layers can work with any backbone architecture, thus, a Diffusion Transformer was chosen as a demonstration.

The reason for choosing a well-pretrained model is that training one from scratch is super expensive. Also, not necessary, since the paper is about TTT layers, not a novel video generator. 😉

### 2\. Test-Time Training Layers

After each attention layer, we insert a small TTT layer with some gating functions.

We’ll cover the math behind TTT in the next parts.

### 3\. Gating the TTT Layer

When you add a new, randomly initialised layer like TTT into a working model, it might break things. Because it is an ‘outsider’.

And when I say break, think of it like this. You have a music band that’s been practising for months for a show. The day before, you throw in a wild musician known for his unpredictable style, hoping to add some spark.

However, without any practice or reconciliation, there’s no doubt the band will completely blunder their performance the next day. The new guy just can’t sync that well, yet.

Same goes for the TTT layer, it wasn’t pre-trained the same way the rest of the model was. So, it ends up like an outsider that doesn’t quite fit in with the other layers. And yeah, that’s not good.

Thus, the way out is to apply a **“gate”** function to control how much the TTT output influences the model.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

[Source Article](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05298>)

* `**tanh(α)**` is close to zero at the start, allowing the model to slowly “trust” TTT more as training continues.

This is done during fine-tuning, so it helps the TTT layer better mingle with the rest of the model.

### 4\. Global vs Local Attention

CogVideo-X originally used global attention to generate all its videos, which becomes painfully slow for longer video lengths.

Let’s say, for a 60-second video, the model’s ‘mind’ would explode because it needs to remember all previous scenes. As a result, the model eats up a lot more memory and computation power due to all those calculations.

Instead, we can avoid this by simply switching to **local attention** , focusing only on generating a 3-second segment at a time. That way, each segment is faster and cheaper to generate. Then, we merge all those little ‘3-second’ segments into a long video.

An issue emerges immediately: how can these 3-second segments stay coherent when they’re no longer using global attention?

Simple! We add TTT layers as a ‘supervisor,’ ensuring that each 3-second segment is generated coherently back and forth in terms of content, storyline, and style.

Not to mention, by switching to local attention, we can now stack many 3-second segments together to create longer videos.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

[Source Article](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05298>)

The key takeaway here is, we generate 3-second videos individually but supervise them globally with the TTT layer, making it way cheaper and delivering a nice, coherent video up to 63 seconds.

### 5\. Bi-directional TTT

Video generation is **non-causal** — the model can see _future_ & _past_ tokens when predicting a frame.

So, we apply TTT twice: one to look **forward** , one to look **backwards** , using a trick:

* `rev(X)` just reverses the token sequence.

This way, the model sees both forward and backwards contexts when generating videos.

RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks) were traditionally used in video generation, especially in earlier models, serving as a baseline for sequence modelling. However, due to their limitations, using RNNs in Test-Time Training isn’t ideal. And the paper inspires the need for a more expressive approach.

To better understand TTT, we’ll first understand the math behind RNN.

## RNN

First off, know that all RNNs have hidden states.

The hidden state in RNN acts like a ‘memory’, updating with each new input. This continuous update is what allows generated text or video to remain coherent, instead of spitting out mumbo jumbo.

Here’s a simple equation used by standard RNNs:

The way it works is very simple if we break it down into 3 processes, shown below.

> Take note that hidden states and memory are used interchangeably here.

### 1\. Memory from the past

* We start with the previous hidden state, `**ht-1**`, which is the memory **from the last time step.**
* Multiply it by `**Whh**`​, the **hidden-to-hidden weights**.
* This transforms the old memory into a form that can be merged with the new memory.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by author

### 2\. Input from the present

* Next, take the new input at time t, which is `**xt**`.
* Multiply it by `**Wxh**`, the **input-to-hidden weights**.
* This learns which parts of the input matter most for updating the memory.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by Author

### 3\. Final Touches

* Moving on, we add the bias term `**bh**`​.
* This helps adjust the activation.
* Finally, we merge everything and apply the `**tanh**` function, which limits the output between -1 and 1. In other words, no exploding or vanishing gradients.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by Author

With RNN, the model can now remember texts or scenes from earlier on and generate reasonable outputs.

But why isn’t RNN good enough?

2 reasons.

### 🧠 Limited memory (fixed-size hidden state)

When we combine the previous memory (ht-1, ht-2, ht-3, …) and the new input `**xt.**` It might seem like the past memory is still influencing the present output. Yet, due to the nature of recursive update, old memories will eventually fade.

Mathematically, `**Whh**`**​** determines how much of the old hidden state can affect the current hidden state, and as **t** grows, the weights decay the importance of earlier states.

Going this way, Transformers were introduced to solve the sequential bottleneck by processing the entire text at once. I won’t go deep into the weeds of how Transformers work, but here’s an article on ModernBERT that gives a nice breakdown of how attention mechanisms work inside them.

### ⛔ No learning at test time

Despite the fact that the hidden states are updated during training, the weights `**Whh**`​ and `**Wxh**`​ are frozen at test time — no real-time adaptation.

Image by Author

That’s actually the norm for most models. With that said, TTT breaks this convention by allowing the model to adapt its weights even at test time, aiming to adapt to any new scenarios.

### What makes TTT, real-time trainable?

That’s the core idea of TTT, right?

First of all, the entire pre-trained model, including its attention, feedforward, and diffusion components, stays frozen during inference. Meanwhile, the TTT layers aren’t frozen during inference. It’s that simple.

### Now, for a deeper dive into TTT:

Test-Time Training (TTT) operates through **two** loops:

1. The **inner loop** updates TTT in real time, enabling test-time adaptation.
2. The **outer loop** improves the inner loop’s test-time adaptation ability.

## Math behind Outer Loop of TTT

The outer loop contains a model parameter **θ**. This θ is **updated only** in the outer loop during fine-tuning. In other words, this outer loop has general video generation skills and will not change during test time. For now, just treat θ as a connection between the inner and outer loop.

While the paper didn’t provide an explicit equation for the L _outer_ , we can still scrutinise it with this simple formula.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by Author

By the way, the arrow indicates that the right side of the equation updates the left side, that’s all.

The K, Q, V was an idea borrowed from attention, except

* θK​: Decides how to “corrupt” the input for learning.
* θV​: Decides what the TTT layer tries to reconstruct.
* θQ: Decides how the output is generated.

Similar to the inner loop’s gradient descent, this formula tweaks the angle of θ, according to the loss function L _outer_**.**

For instance, if the L _outer_ says increasing θK also increases the loss, we decrease θK slightly instead.

## The Synergy between Inner & Outer Loop

From what we have now, the inner and outer loop equations don’t seem to add up, right? Like, if they are working together inside a TTT layer, how exactly are they updating each other?

Remember when I said this paper proposed an improved version of the equation? Basically, they did it so the inner and outer loops could work in sync.

And the math is simple, just by adding θ into the inner loop’s equations. And by doing so, the inner and outer will have the same parameter, θ.

Inner loop loss function (Image by Author)

The x̃ in the original equation indicates a corrupted input. However, in the new equation, the projection θₖxₜ​ already contains how the input should be corrupted (θₖ), making the x̃ unnecessary.

But how do they actually sync?

Remember when I said the inner loop is in charge of real-time adaptation, and the outer is in charge of improving the inner loop’s adaptation ability. Let’s do a little role play to demonstrate this. I’ll use the same example, watching TV.

Let’s say the outer loop is you and, inner loop is your friend.

Imagine your friend’s job (inner loop) is to sit in front of the TV and change the TV volume as fast as he can according to different channels (different inputs). And he has to do this using the least time he possibly can, because real-time adaptation has to be quick. However, if the starting volume is either 0 or 100, it would be be very inconvenient and time consuming for him to change the volume every time he changes a channel. He doesn’t have time to slowly change the volume, just like I said real-time adaptation must be made quick.

So, your job is to assist your friend, and you’ve got plenty of time. You yourself went through various TV channels and tested their volume before-hand. However, you can only pick one volume (θ) for all channels and share it with your friend. Some channels might have people shouting, some might have people whispering. So, you did your calculation and decided, “Ok, I can only pick one volume for all channels, so the best volume is 25.” And you preset this volume to cater to all channels.

Image generated using Google Labs

Now, let’s say your friend, sitting in front of the TV, wants to watch a comedy and switches to that channel. The volume’s already pretty optimal, and he just needs to tweak it a little, maybe to 27. Your help was crucial because he didn’t have to tune it all the way from 0 to 27, or worse, from 100 down to 27. That would’ve taken longer, and honestly, it’s just annoying if the volume started at 100.

Back to where we were.

The outer loop has also trained itself just like you. It has good knowledge about how to generate a nice Tom & Jerry video and chooses a nice θ = 25 to cater for all video styles. Both you and the outer loop knew the best value to assist your partner, which is 25.

Then, the inner loop takes the θ = 25 and updates its parameters with minimal effort, thanks to the outer loop. Just like how your friend changed the TV volume using such little time, the inner loop updates its parameters with minimal changes.

And this clicks with the example I’ve mentioned previously, if you remember the “Generate a video of Tom running on a field.” That’s right, the inner loop makes sure Tom is wearing sports shoes at every second of the video. While the outer loop assists in the making of a nice video.

Why the extra work, you may ask, why not just go over and turn down the volume ourselves? Why not just use a single loop?

You see, the outer loop is like the foundation builder, it learns general video generation skills and designs the best **θ.** This means the outer loop is made for heavy-duty and has to be pre-trained, which takes time and effort to do. Most importantly, it will stay frozen during inference. While the inner loop requires efficiency and accuracy, because it needs to adapt in real time as fast as it can. So, taking and learning directly from **θ** is the most efficient way to generate a test-time adaptable video.

Each plays an indispensable role in video generation.

All the above align with the concept we have been discussing, which is adapting at inference time.

To summarise: during inference, the model will receive different input prompts from user, therefore the inner has to adapt to that prompt and style as quick as possible. And the outer loop uses θ to teach the inner loop how to adapt.

### **Inner Loop Depends on Outer Loop** :

The inner loop’s self-supervised loss function **ℓ** contains θK and θV, which are set by the outer loop.

For example, θK​ determines how the input is corrupted, and θV sets what to reconstruct. If these are poorly chosen, the inner loop’s updates to `**W**` ​ won’t capture useful context, leading to bad outputs.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by Author

Here’s an example: If the outer loop learns that character motion patterns are critical, it will update the θ matrices to help the inner loop focus on these motion patterns when doing real-time adaptation.

The outer loop’s job is to find values of θ to ‘teach’ the inner loop what to focus on, and how it should actually update the parameters when generating videos. Therefore, we say that the inner loop’s performance depends on the outer loop.

### **Outer Loop Depends on Inner Loop** :

The outer loop’s loss L _outer_ ​ depends on the outputs zt, which are produced by the inner loop using Wt​. The newly added θQ determines how the output is generated.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Modified output rule (Image by Author)

To compute ∇θL _outer,_ the outer loop has to backpropagate through the inner loop, calculating how changes in θQ affect Wt​, then zt​, and finally L _outer_.

Think of it like this, the outer loop is updating θQ by observing the performance of θQ in the inner loop, getting feedback from the inner loop.

Here’s a simple example: the outer loop says, “Okay, I’ve tried using θQ = 1.5, and the inner loop struggles to maintain coherence. But, when I increase θQ​ from 1.5 to 2.0, the inner loop seems to improve on video coherence​. Wow, the inner loop can adapt better and generate more coherent Tom & Jerry videos when I use θQ = 2, which means θQ = 2 is a good value!”

This involves **gradients of gradients** because the inner loop’s update (∇Wℓ) is a gradient itself, and the outer loop differentiates through it.

Voila! Now we have the new and improved TTT for video generation.

### To summarise

The outer loop trains the inner loop to be a good “context learner,” and the inner loop provides the dynamic ‘feedback’ back to the outer loop.

And by adding Test-Time Training layers, the model can now efficiently adapt to the specifics of each input storyboard at inference (test) time, without expensive retraining of the full model.

## Additional Information

### ✍️ Text Prompt Formats (3 Types)

Users can prompt the video generator in 3 formats:

**Format 1** : Short plot summary (5–8 sentences).

**Format 2** : Detailed plot (20 sentences, one for each segment).

**Format 3** : Storyboard (each 3-second segment described in a paragraph, including camera movements, background, actions, etc.)

➡️ **Final model always uses Format 3** , even during training. If you give Format 1 or 2, **Claude 3.7 Sonnet** converts it into Format 3.

### 🧑‍🏫 Dataset Creation

The paper uses 81 Tom & Jerry episodes (~7 hours of video). Surprisingly, there are 166 official Tom and Jerry shorts, along with a few movies.

However, the art style changes quite a bit across the different series, which, as far as I’m concerned, is the reason they can’t take in too much data.
