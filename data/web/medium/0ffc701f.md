A team of Stanford University researchers has presented LoLCATs, a new method that linearizes standard Transformer LLMs, drastically reducing compute requirements while retaining most state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance.

And all of this while requiring a handful of GPU hours accounting for less than $20 in total,**resulting in up to 35,500 times more efficiency in model performance relative to training effort**.

This seems impossible, but it’s not, thanks to the clever use of three different techniques I am showing you today. This extraordinary achievement could soon become table stakes across AI engineering teams striving to achieve world-class performance.

## The Issues of Standard LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have brought massive excitement and capital to the AI industry, which appears to be on a direct trajectory to global dominance as the superior technology that will take civilization to a new age of prosperity (or at least that’s what they want us to believe).

Surprisingly, this massive bet forcing Big Tech companies to invest billions and billions is considerably concentrated in one architecture type, Transformers, which are notoriously inefficient.

### Transformers Are All We Need

In layman’s terms, an AI is nothing but a data-compressing algorithm that takes in data, learns the underlying patterns, and can use this acquired knowledge to make useful predictions.

And among the possible ways to do this, none have come close to what Transformers have offered for two reasons:

1. They are totally parallelizable, making them a great option for training models with massive amounts of data.
2. The larger they grow, the better they become, which has led to an investing and research frenzy on how to make these models and their training budgets larger.

However, the unbeaten expressiveness of Transformer-based models does not mean they are perfect, as their virtues create problems of their own.

### The quadratic problem

The biggest one is that they can’t compress state or memory, so to speak. This means that if you want the model to recall something, it must be stored in its memory in its original state.

This is not how humans remember stuff. We do not remember everything or events we’ve experienced; **we just retain what is considered essential**.

Picture you reading a book. If you’re on Chapter 11, the protagonist might mention something that happened earlier in the book, maybe in Chapter 1. If you’ve paid attention, you might remember that fact. Otherwise, you might have to go back and reread that part. However, that’s not an issue regarding memory burden because if nothing extraordinary happened in Chapter 2, for instance, you are free to forget it.

Transformers do not process information that way. While reading Chapter 11, if they need to recall something from Chapter 1, they remember it immediately because they still have access to the entire 11 chapters. In fact, if it weren’t for the KV Cache we build during LLM inference, which saves tons of compute, **Transformers would effectively be rereading all eleven Chapters to read every new word**.

> _Feels inefficient?_ I hope so, because it is.

_But what does all this mean?_ In simple terms, this memory, which is not compressed, grows the longer the sequence becomes. If you’re in Chapter 15, the previous chapters accumulate, and the memory requirements become much larger than when the model was reading Chapter 8 (only seven chapters).

Worse, due to the functioning of the underlying attention mechanism, which we won’t go into today for the sake of length, we need to store two types of information (the key and query values) for each word in every chapter.

This means that Transformers' compute and memory complexity is _O(n2)_ , meaning that every time you double the sequence, compute and memory requirements increase fourfold (and a tripling implies a nine-fold increase).

> With FlashAttention, a technique developed by some of the guys behind today’s research, we help reduce the memory requirements to a subquadratic complexity by avoiding materializing the entire attention matrix in memory, meaning that the above problem is somehow solved.
>
> However, FlashAttention does not deal with compute, which continues to have quadratic complexity (O(n²*d) specifically, where ’n’ is the sequence length and d the model dimension, or the amount of numbers per vector embedding). The reason is that as we don’t compress memory, each word has to pay attention to every single previous word and, as the main operator in Transformers is a pair-wise multiplication, compute continues to be quadratic with regards to sequence length.

But now, a group of researchers has found a way to extend compute complexity into the subquadratic realm.

_But how?_

## Linearizing Attention

As we mentioned earlier, Transformers rely on a mathematical operation known as the attention mechanism, which is quadratic in complexity regarding sequence length. In layman’s terms, longer sequences skyrocket compute requirements.

However, other attention mechanisms, like linear attention, have linear complexity, although they are less expressive on paper (linear attention models perform worse, basically).

However, _what would happen if we swapped attention layers by training linear attention layers to imitate the behavior of their more compute-intensive counterparts?_

Hence, **the objective of**[**LoLCaTs**](<https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2024-10-14-lolcats-p2>)**is to create linearized LLMs that retain the performance of their quadratic complexity counterparts while doing so cost-efficiently**.

And to do so, they break the problem into three steps.

### Step 1. Swapping Layers

The first step to linearizing a model is inserting a group of linear attention layers and training them to imitate the outputs of the standard attention layers.

As seen below, by a simple MSE (Mean-Squared Error) between the output of both layers, we can train the new layers to behave like the original ones.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*UYEDkeL-ZxSNgbg6kxJHYg.png)

However, at this point, the new layers, while individually imitating their counterparts, have created a model that behaves weirdly. To solve this, we now need to retrain the model in the standard next-token prediction task.

### Step 2. LoRA fine-tuning

Of course, the idea of having to retrain the model again to make it more efficient seems like a hard-to-justify use of resources.

Luckily, doing full-model fine-tunings is not necessary, thanks to LoRA adapters. I’ve covered them multiple times, [especially in this newsletter issue](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/winners-losers-age-augmented-llms>), but the idea is that, for any given task, models are intrinsically low-rank. In a nutshell, **only a small portion of total weights matter for any given prediction**.

Thus, we can add a small set of weights called adapters, which are then added to each layer while leaving the original model weights ‘untouched.’ In a way, these adapters adapt the model's behavior based on the data they have been trained on.

Importantly, these adapters are much smaller than the actual model, so they can be trained quickly and cheaply.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:632/1*b8ct6gCIGDqVGh5iTpVK0A.png)

After LoRA training, we have a model that performs next-token prediction similarly to the original model despite having linear attention layers.

> The model isn’t entirely linear. Actually, they blended standard attention (quadratic) and linear attention so that if a sequence has D = N + M tokens, the last M tokens are computed using standard attention, and the first N, which are many more, are computed using linear attention.
>
> **This strikes a nice balance between standard attention’s expressivity and linear attention’s compute efficiency**. As language usually has proximity bias, meaning that recent words are generally more relevant to predict the next word than words in the past, we use softmax attention (standard) for more recent tokens and linear for the rest.

![The sequence’s M last tokens are computed traditionally, the others in the linear form. Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:428/1*cXEFf6WetRb_H40me7KO5A.png)

While we are almost there, the researchers added one final step.

### Step 3. Layer-wise optimization

During attention transfer, we update all layers in the model together. However, researchers observed that this meant that the last layers in the model had a larger mean-squared error (MSE) than earlier layers, which becomes a problem for deeper models like Llama 3.1 405B, which has multiple more layers than its smaller brothers.

Thus, they train the models in batches of layers to ensure this does not happen.

Now, all things considered, _what are the results?_

## State-of-the-art… and cheap

LoLCATs achieve significant efficiency gains in linearizing large LLMs by optimizing only 0.2% of model parameters over 40M tokens, closing 80% of the quality gap with full softmax (standard) Transformers despite being trained with orders of magnitude less training tokens, **up to 35,500 times more training efficient than a standard fine-tuning would have required**.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*QE7nshvwxEBdhfmnmP5ytQ.png)

Moreover, across all three Llama sizes, the LoLCAT model behaves like the original models but enjoys the significant computing efficiencies of having linear attention layers instead of fully quadratic ones.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*cetaEKrzlns8cENHt3fKAg.png)

Impressively, these results required just a handful of training hours in one single GPU for Llama 3.1 8B (meaning that the training costs were well below 20 dollars).

Of course, **we must acknowledge that this method still requires a pretrained quadratic Transformer to perform this layer distillation** , but inference-based optimizations like LoLCATs will become a staple of any well-functioning AI engineering area of enterprises that wish to adopt Generative AI with great performance while saving tons of money in compute.
