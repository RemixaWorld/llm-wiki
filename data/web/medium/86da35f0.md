**Introduction**

Knowledge graphs (KGs) have emerged as a powerful tool for representing and reasoning over complex domains, with applications ranging from natural language processing to drug discovery. As the size and complexity of KGs continue to grow, embedding models have become increasingly popular for learning low-dimensional representations of entities and relations, enabling efficient computation and downstream tasks such as link prediction. However, despite their success, the question of what KG embedding models actually learn remains largely unexplored. In the paper by Douglas et al., the authors investigate this question by proposing three distinct learning mechanisms and conducting experiments on both synthetic and real-world KGs. In this review, the key findings and insights from the paper are explored.

**Knowledge Graphs and Embedding Models**

A KG represents entities as vertices and their relationships as typed, directed edges. This structure enables flexible information organization and facilitates automated reasoning. KGs have proven valuable in diverse applications, including natural language processing, recommendation systems, and drug discovery, due to their ability to capture complex relationships in a machine-readable format.

Embedding models have played a crucial role in the success of learning and reasoning over KGs. These models aim to map entities and relations to low-dimensional vector representations (embeddings) in a metric space. The proximity between entities under a relation-type-dependent transformation reflects the likelihood of the corresponding edge existing in the KG. In other words, if two entities are connected by a specific relation in the KG, their embeddings should be close to each other after applying the corresponding relation-specific transformation.

Two popular embedding models are TransE and PairRE. TransE represents each entity and relation as a vector in the embedding space. For a given triple (head, relation, tail), TransE aims to make the sum of the head entity’s embedding and the relation’s embedding approximately equal to the tail entity’s embedding. This translation-based approach allows TransE to model simple relationships between entities effectively.

On the other hand, PairRE represents each relation as a pair of vectors (_rᴴ_ ,_rᵀ_) and applies element-wise multiplication (Hadamard product) to the entity embeddings. For a given triple (head, relation, tail), PairRE aims to make the element-wise product of _rʰ_ ∘ _h_ (where _rʰ_ is the head-specific relation embedding) approximately equal to the element-wise product of _rᵗ_ ∘ _t_ (where _rᵗ_ is the tail-specific relation embedding). This approach allows PairRE to model more complex relationships between entities, taking into account the specific roles of the head and tail entities in the relation.

Both TransE and PairRE are trained using gradient descent to minimize the error in satisfying their respective constraints. However, training these models on large-scale KGs can be challenging due to the scale of the problem. As the number of entities in a KG increases, the number of potential triples grows quadratically, making it computationally infeasible to consider all possible triples during training. To address this issue, negative sampling is used to estimate the error for triples not present in the KG. This technique involves generating negative triples by corrupting positive triples (i.e., triples that exist in the KG) by replacing either the head or tail entity with a random entity. The model is then trained to score positive triples higher than negative triples. This approach allows the model to learn from both existing and non-existing relationships while keeping the computational complexity manageable.

**Link Prediction and Evaluation Metrics**

One of the primary tasks for KG embedding models is link prediction, which involves inferring missing links in the graph. There are two common applications of link prediction: Binary link classification and Ranking of potential link completions.

**Binary link classification.** Given a triple (head, relation, tail), the model predicts whether the triple is likely to be true or false. This is useful for tasks such as fact-checking or knowledge base completion.

**Ranking of potential link completions.** Given a partial triple (e.g., (head, relation, ?)), the model ranks the potential candidates for the missing entity. This is useful for tasks such as recommendation systems or question answering.

To evaluate the performance of KG embedding models on link prediction tasks, two common metrics are used:

**Hits@N.** This metric measures the proportion of correct entities ranked in the top N predictions. For example, if the correct tail entity for a given (head, relation) pair is ranked 3rd out of 100 potential candidates, the Hits@10 score would be 1 (since the correct entity is in the top 10), and the Hits@1 score would be 0 (since the correct entity is not the top prediction).

**Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).** This metric computes the average of the reciprocal ranks of the correct entities. The reciprocal rank is the inverse of the rank of the first correct prediction. For example, if the correct tail entity is ranked 3rd, the reciprocal rank would be 1/3.

**Three Learning Mechanisms in KG Embeddings**

The authors propose three distinct learning mechanisms that contribute to the performance of knowledge graph embedding models: unstructured statistical learning, network learning, and motif learning. These mechanisms offer different perspectives on how embedding models capture and leverage the information present in knowledge graphs. By understanding the role of each mechanism, insights are gained into the strengths and limitations of various embedding models, enabling the development of more effective approaches for knowledge graph representation learning.

**Unstructured Statistical Learning.** Unstructured statistical learning is an approach to learning from KGs that focuses on modeling the probability distribution of triples (_h_ , _r_ , _t_) using latent variables. This method does not explicitly consider the graph structure or logical rules between relations. Instead, it relies on the statistical co-occurrence of entities and relations within the KG to make predictions.

In unstructured statistical learning, each head entity, tail entity, and relation is represented by a distinct vector in the embedding space. The model learns these embeddings based on the frequency and patterns of their co-occurrences in the data. The score of a triple (_h_ , _r_ , _t_) is typically computed using the dot product or another similarity measure between the embeddings of the head, relation, and tail.

**Network Learning.** Network learning is a mechanism that focuses on capturing the structural properties of the knowledge graph without explicitly considering the semantic meaning of the relations. This approach leverages the connectivity patterns and the topology of the graph to learn embeddings that reflect the proximity and relatedness of entities.

Embedding models like TransE can potentially learn network patterns by adjusting the entity and relation embeddings based on the observed connectivity in the graph. During training, the model tries to minimize the distance between the embeddings of entities that are directly connected by an edge, while maximizing the distance between entities that are not directly connected. This optimization process allows the model to learn embeddings that reflect the graph structure, with entities that are closer in the graph having more similar embeddings.

For example, consider the simple knowledge graph mentioned above. In this case, a network learning approach would aim to learn embeddings such that the embeddings of A, B, and C are relatively close to each other in the embedding space, as they are all connected. The embedding of A might be slightly closer to both B and C than B and C are to each other, as A is directly connected to both. The embeddings of D and E would be further away from A, B, and C in the embedding space, reflecting their lack of connection to those entities. However, D and E would be closer to each other due to their direct connection.

**Motif Learning.** Motif learning is a mechanism for discovering rules between relations in knowledge graph embeddings. Motifs are small, recurring subgraphs within the knowledge graph that can represent logical rules or constraints between relations.

Knowledge graph embedding models, such as TransE, can learn these patterns by optimizing the relation embeddings to satisfy the constraints implied by the motifs. Again, consider the above simple knowledge graph. The A, B, C triangle can represent a compositional rule between the relations _r_ ₁, _r_ ₂, and _r_ ₃. In the case of TransE, learning this pattern involves finding relation embeddings that satisfy _μᵣ_ ₁ + _μᵣ_ ₂ ≈ _μᵣ_ ₃, where _μᵣ_ ₁, _μᵣ_ ₂, and _μᵣ_ ₃ are the embeddings for relations _r_ ₁, _r_ ₂, and _r_ ₃, respectively.

Motif learning can capture various types of rules beyond composition, such as symmetry (if A relates to B, then B relates to A). The concept of motif learning provides a precise definition of rule learning in knowledge graphs, relying solely on the graph’s structure rather than external information or reasoning. This approach helps capture and utilize the inherent patterns within the knowledge graph to improve embedding quality and predictive performance.

**Experiments on Synthetic Knowledge Graphs and Real-World Datasets**

The authors conducted a comprehensive study to understand and distinguish between three key learning mechanisms in KG embedding models: unstructured statistical learning, network learning, and motif learning. Their approach involved experiments on both synthetic and real-world knowledge graphs, as well as methodological improvements in evaluation protocols.

**Experiments on Synthetic Knowledge Graphs.** The authors constructed synthetic KGs of the form _G_ ∪ _R_ , where _G_ is a highly structured deterministic graph and _R_ is a random graph representing noise. This setup allowed for controlled variations in noise levels and specific structural patterns. To test pattern learning, graphs like _G_ 1 were created with specific pattern such as triangle patterns. The authors measured how well TransE learned these patterns by examining the relationship between learned relation embeddings, for example, checking if _μᵣ_ ₁ +_μᵣ_ ₂ ≈ _μᵣ_ ₃ for triangle pattern.

To isolate network learning, the authors compared TransE with TransE*, a variant with frozen relation embeddings. In TransE*, the relation embeddings are randomly initialized and then kept fixed during training, while only the entity embeddings are learned. This modification effectively disables most mechanisms for motif learning, as the model can no longer adjust relation embeddings to capture rules or patterns between relations. The performance of TransE* thus primarily reflects learning from network structure alone, helping determine the contribution of network structure to the model’s overall performance. Noise sensitivity was examined by gradually increasing noise levels in graphs _G_ 2, _G_ 3, and _G_ 4, allowing the authors to determine how different learning mechanisms perform under varying signal-to-noise ratios. They identified a critical noise threshold beyond which performance significantly degraded, suggesting a point at which the noise overwhelms the model’s ability to learn useful patterns from the graph structure.

These experiments revealed that TransE effectively learns patterns, but its performance degrades with increasing noise. The good performance of TransE* suggested significant contributions from network learning.

**Distinguishing Learning Mechanisms in Real-World Knowledge Graphs.** For real-world KGs such as FB15k, FB15k-237, WN18, and WN18RR, the authors employed several techniques. To measure unstructured statistical learning, they used simple models like RE (Relation-Entity) as baselines, quantifying how much performance can be attributed to statistical patterns alone. The RE model is a simplified embedding model that uses separate embeddings for heads, tails, and relations, but doesn’t model the connections between them. It scores a triple (_h_ , _r_ , _t_) simply as the dot product of the tail embedding with the relation embedding, _eₜ_ · _μᵣ_ , ignoring the head entity. This model can only capture statistical co-occurrences of relations and tail entities, making it a good baseline for unstructured statistical learning. The performance of RE helped assess the contribution of unstructured learning in these datasets.

To test network learning, versions of KGs with all relation types removed were created, allowing the authors to isolate the impact of graph structure on model performance. For pattern learning, the authors analyzed learned embeddings to check if they satisfy expected motif patterns. They also evaluated models on tasks requiring understanding of specific logical rules or patterns.

**Ablation Studies and Model Comparisons.** The authors conducted various ablation studies and model comparisons. These included freezing relation embeddings to isolate the contribution of entity embeddings and network structure, removing edge labels to focus solely on network learning, and adding random noise edges to test the robustness of different learning mechanisms. They also compared models with different strengths, such as PairRE’s robustness in noisy environments, to understand which learning mechanisms are most effective in different scenarios.

These studies helped disentangle the contributions of each learning mechanism, revealing how different models leverage various aspects of KG structure. By applying this comprehensive approach across different types of knowledge graphs and noise levels, the authors gained valuable insights into what these models actually learn and how their learning strategies adapt to diverse data characteristics.

**Methodological Insights for Evaluating Knowledge Graph Embeddings.** The authors proposed improvements to evaluation protocols. They suggested increasing the number and diversity of negative examples in link prediction tasks to provide a more challenging and representative test. They also introduced the use of resampled metrics (R-MRR) alongside standard metrics to get a more accurate assessment of model performance, especially for large KGs where uniform sampling of negatives can be misleading.

Performance was analyzed across different types of KGs to understand which learning mechanisms are most important for different graph structures and densities. These methodological improvements aimed to provide a more robust and reliable assessment of model performance, reducing the risk of overestimating performance due to unrepresentative negative examples.

By utilizing these various techniques across synthetic and real-world KGs, the authors were able to disentangle the contributions of unstructured statistical learning, network learning, and motif learning in KG embedding models. This understanding helps in interpreting results more accurately and guides the development of more effective KG embedding models that can leverage the most relevant information for a given knowledge graph structure.

**Conclusion**

This paper makes significant contributions to the understanding of KG embedding models by identifying three key learning mechanisms: unstructured statistical learning, network learning, and motif learning. Through experiments on synthetic and real-world KGs, the authors demonstrate the ability of popular models like TransE and PairRE to learn patterns and show the impact of noise on this learning. They also propose tests to distinguish the contributions of each mechanism and highlight methodological issues in evaluating large KGs.
