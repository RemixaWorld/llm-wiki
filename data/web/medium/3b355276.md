## Background

### MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) in LLM

In the context of LLMs, MoE usually involves substituting the**FFN layer** in Transformer architectures with an MoE layer, as illustrated in the figure below.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 1. Illustration of MoE layer. Image from GShard paper [5].

More specifically, the left side illustrates a stack of N Transformer layers, each containing a single MHA sublayer followed by an FFN sublayer. On the right, we see a stack of N/2 Transformer layers, where the FFN sublayer in alternating layers is replaced with an MoE layer. In other words, MoE layers substitute FFNs in every other Transformer layer. In practice, MoE can be implemented to replace FFNs at configurable intervals within the Transformer architecture.

Looking deeper into the MoE layer, it consists of a Gating operation followed by multiple FFNs, each mirroring the structure of a standard FFN sublayer. These FFNs are known as “experts” in the MoE framework, and the Gating mechanism is trained to determine which expert should be activated for a given input.

In other words, only K out of N experts are activated for the t-th token, resulting in sparse g_{i, t} values since K is typically very small. This design increases the total number of trainable parameters due to the added FFNs, but only a small portion is utilized during each forward pass.

### Benefits and Challenges of MoE

The beauty of MoE is that it mirrors many real-world systems that follow similar principles, making it easier to grasp through relatable examples.

Now imagine we’re hiring chefs for a restaurant that offers both Chinese and Italian cuisines, and we’re considering two options:

* Option 1: hire a single chef who excels at both Chinese and Italian dishes, capable of preparing every dish alone. This mirrors a standard Transformer model, where a single FFN sublayer processes all input tokens.
* Option 2: hire multiple chefs, each specializing in either Chinese or Italian cuisine, along with a head chef who assigns orders based on their expertise. This resembles the MoE approach, where each chef is an expert and the head chef functions as the Gating mechanism to select the appropriate experts.

With the above analogy, it’s clear that Option 2 not only simplifies the hiring process but also ensures higher-quality preparation for both cuisines. On the other hand, finding a single chef who masters multiple cuisines is far more difficult — if not impossible — and may require compromising on the quality of the dishes.

Returning to the LLM context, the motivation behind MoE is partly driven by the scaling hypothesis, which suggests that emergent abilities tend to arise as LLMs are scaled up on massive datasets. This explains the trend of increasingly larger models, such as the GPT series growing from 117M to 175B parameters.

However, not everyone has the resources to train LLMs at such scale, and MoE offers a practical compromise: it enables scaling up model size to boost capacity while keeping training and inference costs manageable by activating only a small portion of the total parameters for each input token.

As demonstrated in [4], it’s possible to train a 2B model with just 0.3B parameters activated, a 16B model with 2.8B activated, or even a 145B model with only 22.2B parameters in use. In all cases, roughly 1/7 of the total parameters are activated at any given time, greatly enhancing training and inference efficiency.

However, every design has its limitations and introduces new challenges. For MoE, its performance depends heavily on the gating mechanism, which may not always route each input token to the most suitable expert. As a result, a few experts might be overused while others remain underutilized, receiving limited exposure to training data — a problem commonly known as “expert collapse.”

This can also cause additional issues such as load imbalance — since most input tokens are routed to a limited set of experts — and instability, where performance degrades if a token is assigned to experts that haven’t been adequately trained for the task.

That’s why load balancing is a frequent topic of discussion in MoE architectures.

DeepSeekMoE also introduces strategies to address load balancing, but in this article, we’ll concentrate on its core innovations and explore load balancing techniques — specifically auxiliary-loss-free load balancing [8] — in the next installment.

### Knowledge Specialization vs. Knowledge Sharing

When hiring in the restaurant example above, we’re also navigating the trade-off between knowledge specialization and knowledge sharing: Option 1 favors generalists but may compromise on depth, while Option 2 emphasizes specialization. This kind of trade-off is common in many real-world settings, such as companies, teams, and other organizations.

This trade-off also exists in MoE, though in a more implicit manner. In theory, each expert should specialize in specific aspects, as only a subset of input tokens is routed to each one, while some level of shared knowledge remains due to shared parameters. Unlike in real-world organizations, however, it’s challenging to precisely measure how specialized each expert is or how much knowledge is being shared among them.

Balancing specialization and knowledge sharing is a critical design consideration in MoE architectures, as neither over-specialization nor excessive redundancy leads to optimal performance.

In the case of over-specialization, training and inference may become unstable, and suboptimal routing can significantly degrade performance. Additionally, this often results in underutilized capacity, as highly specialized experts are capable of handling only a narrow subset of input tokens.

In the latter case, if experts become too similar, the extra parameters introduced by MoE won’t yield proportional gains in capacity, leading to an inefficient use of limited computational resources.

In the next section, we’ll explore how DeepSeekMoE is designed to strike a more effective balance between specialization and knowledge sharing.

## DeepSeekMoE Architecture

DeepSeekMoE introduces two key innovations to balance knowledge specialization and knowledge sharing in MoE: fine-grained expert segmentation and shared expert isolation.

### Fine-Grained Expert Segmentation

Fine-grained expert segmentation is introduced in DeepSeekMoE to encourage expert specialization. The underlying intuition is straightforward: activating more experts for a given input token increases the likelihood that the required knowledge can be decomposed and distributed across multiple experts.

In our earlier restaurant example, this resembles dividing each chef into specialized skills, as illustrated in the figure below. Initially, we have one chef for all Chinese dishes and another for all Italian dishes. After applying fine-grained expert segmentation, the skills required for each cuisine are distributed across multiple experts. This results in a group of chefs for Chinese dishes and another for Italian dishes, with each chef specializing in a particular skill within their respective cuisine.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Figure 4. Illustration of (a) before and (b) after applying fine-grained expert segmentation using the restaurant example. Image by author.

This is also illustrated in Figure 3, where subfigure (a) shows each input token being routed to 2 out of N experts, while in subfigure (b), each token is routed to 4 out of 2N experts. In more general cases, we can increase the number of experts from N to mN while reducing the intermediate hidden dimension of each expert FFN by a factor of 1/m, and activating m times more experts for each input token. This approach ensures that the overall computation cost of (a) and (b) remains roughly the same.

Although the authors did not provide a theoretical proof for the effectiveness of this strategy, they conducted experiments to validate their approach, which we will discuss in the Evaluation section.

### Shared Expert Isolation

Another technique proposed by DeepSeekMoE is the isolation of shared experts to reduce redundancy. The intuition behind this is that by reserving some experts to learn common knowledge across various tasks, the remaining experts can focus more on specialized knowledge, reducing redundancy among the unshared experts.

In our restaurant example, this is akin to further dividing all the chefs into two groups, as shown in the figure below. The first group, shown in the upper part, focuses on general cooking skills like basic knife techniques, cooking methods, and seasoning principles, while the second group of chefs specializes in their own unique dishes.

For instance, the dumpling chef can focus solely on dumpling folding and steaming without needing to worry about plating techniques, while the pasta chef can concentrate on perfecting pasta without having to learn chopping skills. This way, knowledge redundancy between chefs is minimized.

## Evaluation

As mentioned earlier, although the intuitions behind both strategies seem reasonable, the authors did not provide any theoretical proof to justify them, leaving it uncertain whether they truly address the tension between specialization and knowledge sharing, or to what extent they can help.

Basically, we want to understand three core questions:

* Can DeepSeekMoE achieve better Results?
* Can and to what extent does fine-grained expert segmentation benefit specialization?
* Can and to what extent does shared expert isolation reduce redundancy?

To address these questions, the authors carefully designed a series of experiments, and it is important to highlight them here.

### Can DeepSeekMoE Achieve Better Results?

Firstly, the authors investigate whether their method improves overall performance. To verify this, they train a series of models with comparable total and activated parameters, then evaluate them on various tasks. The main results are summarized in the table below, with the best metrics highlighted in bold.

Several takeaways:

* The columns highlighted in blue compare the standard Transformer (Dense) with two MoE architectures (Hash Layer [6] and Switch Transformer [7]), demonstrating that, with comparable activated parameters, MoE architectures can achieve significantly better performance.
* The columns highlighted in green further compare DeepSeekMoE with another MoE method, GShard [5], showing that, with comparable activated parameters, DeepSeekMoE achieves significantly better performance.

However, achieving better performance doesn’t necessarily indicate a better trade-off between specialization and knowledge sharing, so additional experiments are needed to assess this aspect.

### Does DeepSeekMoE Benefit Specialization?

Directly measuring the specialization of experts is challenging. Instead, the authors design an interesting experiment from the opposite direction: they disable some of the top-routed experts and observe the results.

Intuitively, experts that are more specialized should be less replaceable. Therefore, disabling the top-routed experts should have a greater impact on performance.

More specifically, they conduct experiments by disabling the top-routed experts in both DeepSeekMoE and GShard x 1.5, with GShard x 1.5 serving as the baseline. These two methods have comparable Pile loss when no experts are disabled, as seen in the leftmost point corresponding to ratio 0 in the figure below.

Figure 8. Pile loss for DeepSeekMoE and GShard x 1.5 with top routed experts disabled. Image from [4].

As the ratio of disabled routed experts increases, DeepSeekMoE consistently results in higher Pile loss, indicating that the routed experts in DeepSeekMoE are more specialized and, therefore, harder to replace by others.

### Does DeepSeekMoE Reduce Knowledge Redundancy?

Following a similar approach, the authors also disable the shared expert and activate one additional routed expert to determine whether the shared expert can be replaced by adding an extra routed expert.

As a result, they observe “a significant increase in the Pile loss, rising from 1.808 to 2.414,” which confirms that the knowledge acquired by the shared expert is somewhat unique. The routed experts are not sufficiently trained to cover that part of the knowledge. In other words, the routed experts are more specialized and exhibit less redundancy.

### Summary

In this article, we explain DeepSeekMoE, a key architectural innovation used in DeepSeek models such as DeepSeek-V2 and DeepSeek-V3, using an analogy from a restaurant example.

More specifically, we introduced how MoE works in general, its benefits and challenges, and the trade-off between expert specialization and knowledge sharing. We then explained two key elements of DeepSeekMoE: fine-grained expert segmentation and shared expert isolation. Finally, we discussed its performance in the evaluation section.

As our main takeaway, DeepSeekMoE improves results by promoting expert specialization, while maintaining computational costs comparable to general MoE architectures, thus enhancing computational efficiency.
