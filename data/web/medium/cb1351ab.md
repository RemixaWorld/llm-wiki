## While building the RAG application:

### **1\. Optimise Prompting**

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) involves giving the model short pieces of relevant resources to boost the model’s knowledge, reduce hallucinations and build user trust.

But simply including resources isn’t enough for even decent RAG performance. You must instruct the model clearly.
So be explicit in your prompts:

_“Use only the resources provided.”,_

_“If nothing in these docs helps, say that.”,_

_“Do not make stuff up.”_

Tell it to prioritize using these resources and to refer directly to only them. Also, make sure it knows to admit clearly when none of the resources provided are sufficient. These steps help ground the model’s responses in reality.

### 2\. Crafting Context Tokens efficiently

Rethink your assumptions about how much context your RAG really needs.

With efficient retrieval techniques, RAG would get some relevant contexts. But how do you narrow it down to what’s truly important?

One simple method is to take the final prompt, complete with context, instructions, and all retrieved search results, and then read it on a blank page. Doing this helps you spot redundancy, contradictions, and messy formatting.

Also, try structuring your context clearly. If your set of documents isn’t easy for a human to understand, it likely won’t help your model either. Arrange your context logically, highlighting connections between sections. This makes it easier for the model to extract and use the information effectively.

### 3\. RAG depends on the quality of retrieved documents

The quality of your RAG system depends entirely on the documents you retrieve. Two factors determine document quality - relevance and information density.

First, relevance. Relevance metrics measure how closely the retrieved documents match the user’s query. Common metrics include Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG). MRR measures how well your system ranks the first relevant document. NDCG considers the relevance of all documents returned and their rank positions.

Second, information density matters. If two documents are equally relevant, choose the one that’s more concise and focused. For example, in a movie review example, both transcripts and user reviews might be relevant. But top-rated or editorial reviews typically provide information that’s more concise and useful.

### 4\. Don’t ignore keyword search — use it as a baseline or in a hybrid search

Embedding-based retrieval has become popular recently, but keyword-based search still remains valuable. Embeddings are great at capturing overall meaning but often struggle with exact queries, such as names (“Ilya”), acronyms (“RAG”), or products (“claude-3-sonnet”). Keyword searches, like BM25, excel at these specific searches.

> “Vector embeddings do not magically solve search. In fact, the heavy lifting is in the step before you re-rank with semantic similarity search. Making a genuine improvement over BM25 or full-text search is hard.”
>
> — Aravind Srinivas, CEO Perplexity.ai

Keyword searches are very easy to understand , it’s clear why a document was retrieved based on matching keywords. Embedding-based retrieval is harder to interpret. Additionally, keyword search engines like Lucene and OpenSearch have decades of optimization behind them, making them fast and efficient.

In practice, a hybrid approach usually works best. Use keyword matching for clear, direct matches and embeddings for synonyms, related concepts, spelling mistakes, and multi-modal searches (like images and text together). For example, Shortwave shared how they built their RAG pipeline by combining query rewriting, keyword search, embedding retrieval, and ranking.

### 5\. Prefer RAG Over Finetuning for New Knowledge

Both Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and finetuning can help integrate external and proprietary information into language models (LLMs) and improve their performance on specific tasks. But in a project we struggle to decide which approach to prioritize.

Recent studies suggest RAG might have the upper hand. One experiment compared RAG to unsupervised finetuning on a subset of the MMLU benchmark and recent events. The results showed that RAG consistently outperformed finetuning both for knowledge already encountered during training and for entirely new information.

The benefits of RAG go beyond just better performance. There are practical advantages too:

* **Easier Updates:** Keeping retrieval indices current is simpler and cheaper than continuous pretraining or finetuning.
* **Content Control:** If a retrieval index contains problematic documents, like those with toxic or biased content, you can easily remove or modify them.
* **Granular Access:** RAG offers finer control over document retrieval. For example, in multi-organization setups, you can partition retrieval indices. This ensures that each organization can only access its own documents, preventing accidental data leaks.

### 6\. Long-Context Models Won’t Make RAG Obsolete

With models like Gemini 1.5 Pro boasting massive context windows of up to 2 million tokens, some are questioning whether RAG is still necessary.

Long context performance of state of the art models on 3 curated RAG datasets (Databricks DocsQA, FinanceBench, and Natural Questions). Context length is measured in thousands of tokens from 2k to 2 million- [[source](<https://www.databricks.com/blog/long-context-rag-performance-llms>)]

At first glance, a 10-million-token context size sounds like it could replace RAG entirely. Why bother with retrieval when you can just dump all your data into the context and let the model handle it? Right?

But the reality is more nuanced. While long-context models are great for use cases like analyzing multiple documents or interacting with large PDFs, RAG isn’t going anywhere. Here’s why:

1. **Relevance Still Matters:** Even with huge context windows, we still need a way to select the most relevant information. Without good retrieval, we risk overloading the model with distractors — or worse, irrelevant content.
2. **Reasoning Over Large Contexts Is Hard:** Beyond simple “needle-in-a-haystack” tests, we don’t yet have strong evidence that models can reason effectively over massive context sizes.
3. **Cost Concerns:** Larger contexts come with higher computational costs. Transformer models scale linearly with context length during inference. Just because a model _can_ read your entire Google Drive before answering a question doesn’t mean it’s cost-effective — or even practical.

So, don’t throw out your RAG workflows just yet. While long-context models will expand what LLMs can do, RAG will remain a valuable tool. It’s efficient, effective, and helps manage complexity in ways largest context windows can’t.

### 7\. Choose the Smallest Model That Gets the Job Done

When starting to build an LLM application, the instinct is often to go straight for the biggest, most powerful model available. But once you’ve confirmed that the task is technically doable, it’s worth exploring whether a smaller model can deliver similar results.

Smaller models come with clear advantages like lower latency and reduced costs. While they may not be as strong as their larger counterparts, techniques like chain-of-thought prompting, n-shot examples, and in-context learning can help smaller models perform surprisingly well.

In fact, a well-designed workflow using a smaller model can sometimes match or even exceed the output quality of a single large model. All while being faster and cheaper.

Take classification tasks as another example. Lightweight models like DistilBERT (67 million parameters) often deliver impressive results. The 400-million-parameter DistilBART is another strong contender. When finetuned on open-source data, it achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.84 for identifying hallucinations, outperforming many larger LLMs at a fraction of the latency and cost.

You could also use Small language models to supplement the LLM in RAG framework by [detecting hallucination](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.809.pdf>) and [deciding whether to pull data from VectorDB or not](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.242.pdf>).

Don’t underestimate smaller models. It’s easy to throw a massive model at every problem, but with a bit of creativity and testing, you can often find a solution that’s both efficient and effective. ### **8\. Don’t Treat Security as an Afterthought**

If your RAG system is going into production, security has to be built in from the start and not slapped on later. Two risks stand out in production RAG application — **prompt hijacking** (where someone crafts a clever input to manipulate the model’s behavior) and **hallucinations** ( we covered this earlier).

Apart from this, there’s a long list of overlooked risks that can quietly introduce major vulnerabilities. Let’s walk through a few that’s worth tightening up.

**A. Lock It Down with Access Controls**
Just because your knowledge base holds useful information doesn’t mean it should be visible to everyone. Without proper access boundaries, customer data or internal notes can bleed across teams or worse, into the wild. Role-based access control (RBAC) is your first line of defense here. It keeps things compartmentalized, and just as importantly, gives you audit trails to track who accessed what and when.

**B. Expect Abuse — Plan for It**
The moment your RAG endpoint is exposed to the public, it’s going to attract attention , not always the good kind. We’ve seen examples where unprotected APIs got hammered with automated requests, sometimes at rates that skyrocket costs or exfiltrate private data. At minimum, you’ll want rate limiting, bot detection (like reCAPTCHA), and validation checks on incoming traffic.

**C. Watch for Leaky PII Inputs**
People often paste sensitive stuff into prompts without realizing it, like API keys in logs, personal emails in screenshots, or full customer conversations in support tickets. Once that data hits your system, it’s tricky to clean it up completely. So your best bet would be to build in PII (Personally Identifiable Information) detection and masking before anything gets indexed or returned in a response. You can’t prevent every mistake, but you can catch a lot before it causes damage.

## 2\. During the Evaluation and Monitoring Phase:

Evaluating language models (LLMs) is complex. Even the most advanced research labs struggle with it.

LLMs produce open-ended outputs, and the tasks we ask them to perform vary widely. Despite the challenges, thoughtful and rigorous evaluations are critical for the RAG-based application.

### 2.1. Create Assertion-Based Unit Tests from Real Samples

Start by creating unit tests using input-output samples with the help of Subject Matter Experts. These tests should define expected outputs based on our criteria.

Assertions should be triggered whenever the pipeline changes. For instance, modifying a prompt, adding new context with RAG, or making other updates should prompt these tests. Because sometimes the LLM API Providers like OpenAI or ChatGPT could have released minor changes to the model without any notice, and your LLM App would be affected by it.

Here are some ideas for assertions:

* **Include/Exclude Phrases:** Specify phrases that should or shouldn’t appear in the responses.
* **Range Checks:** Ensure word counts, item counts, or sentence counts fall within acceptable ranges.
* **Execution-Based Tests for Code:** For code generation tasks, run the generated code and verify that it meets the user’s request. For example, if the user asks for a function named `foo`, the generated code should allow you to call `foo` after execution.

One challenge with execution-based evaluations is that the runtime state often doesn’t perfectly match the target code. In these cases, consider relaxing your assertions to focus only on the minimum requirements for a viable answer.

### 2.2. LLM-as-Judge is not perfect

The idea of using an LLM to evaluate other LLMs — “LLM-as-Judge” has its skeptics. Initially, I was among them. But when implemented correctly, LLM-as-Judge can achieve decent alignment with human judgments. In fact, GPT-4 has shown high accuracy, consistency, and stability compared to professional human evaluators.

For pairwise comparisons (e.g., comparing a control output to a new treatment), LLM-as-Judge often gets the direction right, even if the magnitude of the difference is noisy.

Here are some tips to make the most of LLM-as-Judge:

* **Pairwise Comparisons:** Instead of asking the LLM to score a single output, present it with two options and ask which is better. This approach typically yields more stable results.
* **Control for Response Length:** LLMs often favor longer responses. To reduce this bias, ensure that the pairs being compared are similar in length.
* **Use Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** Asking the LLM to explain its reasoning before making a final choice can improve reliability. As a bonus, this allows you to use a smaller, faster LLM while still achieving solid results. Since this evaluation step is typically batch-processed, the extra latency from CoT is rarely an issue.

### 2.3. Set up Guardrails

Setting up Evaluation pipelines alone is not enough. It is highly essential to setup Guardrails.

So, What are Guardrails?

_LLM guardrails are pre-defined rules and filters designed to protect LLM applications from vulnerabilities like data leakage, bias, and hallucination. They also shield against malicious inputs, such as prompt injections and jailbreaking attempts. Guardrails are made up of either input or output safety guards, each representing a unique safety criteria to safeguard your LLM against_.

Guardrails catch harmful or inappropriate content, while evaluations measure the quality and accuracy of outputs.

**LLM Guardrails** are a set of safety measures, guidelines, and frameworks designed to ensure that large language models operate responsibly and within defined boundaries. These guardrails serve multiple purposes, including:

* **Mitigating Risks** : Reducing biases, preventing privacy violations, and avoiding harmful outputs.
* **Ensuring Compliance** : Aligning AI systems with regulatory and ethical standards.
* **Improving Reliability** : Guaranteeing logical outputs and accurate content generation.

The difficulty in constructing guardrails often lies in establishing their requirements. ### 2.4. Handling Outputs — When LLMs Shouldn’t Generate

A major challenge with LLMs is their tendency to generate outputs even when they shouldn’t. This can lead to harmless but nonsensical responses or more problematic outcomes like toxicity or misinformation.

For example:

* In a legal RAG app, when a user asks, “How to bake a cake?, LLM model would answer it using its pre-trained data, while it should not
* If the input context contains non-English documents, the model might respond in a different language unexpectedly.

We can try instructing the LLM to respond with “not applicable” or “unknown” when appropriate, but this approach isn’t foolproof. Even with log probabilities available, they’re a poor indicator of output quality. Log probabilities measure the likelihood of a token appearing in the output but don’t guarantee correctness. Instruction-tuned models, designed to generate coherent responses, may produce high-probability outputs that are fluent but inaccurate.

Careful prompt engineering helps to some degree, but it’s not enough on its own. Complement it with **robust guardrails** to detect and manage problematic responses. For instance:

* **Content Moderation in AWS Guardrails:** Tools like AWS Bedrock has with denied topics feature, where you can mention the topics you don’t want the LLM to respond to. For example, healthcare advice, recommendation on sensitive topics and political issues.
* **Train BERT as custom guardrails:** You could build a classification model based on the out-of-context queries you get from the user using BERT based models

Additionally, precise retrieval techniques can enhance accuracy. If no relevant documents are found during retrieval, the system can deterministically respond with “I don’t know.”

### 2.5. Hallucination is a Persistent Problem

Unlike issues like content safety or PII violations which are quite rare thanks to the availability of tools to detect it , **hallucinations** are stubbornly persistent. Factual inaccuracies remain a common problem, with a baseline occurrence rate of 5–10%. Even for simple tasks like summarization, reducing this rate below 2% can be extremely difficult

Addressing hallucinations requires a two-pronged approach:

1. **Prompt Engineering (Upstream):**

Techniques like **Chain-of-Thought (CoT)** prompting can reduce hallucinations. By encouraging the model to explain its reasoning before generating an answer, you can improve its accuracy.

**2\. Factual Consistency Guardrails (Downstream):**

Once the output is generated, guardrails can evaluate the factuality of responses. They can flag or filter hallucinated content and, if necessary, regenerate outputs.

In cases where retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is used, hallucinations can sometimes be detected deterministically. For instance, if the model identifies its sources, you can manually verify whether the output is grounded in the input context.

If you are working in critical domains where there should be no room for hallucination, like healthcare, legal, you might need to take extra efforts to handle them. ### 2.6. Check for Development-Production Skew

In traditional machine learning pipelines, a common issue is **train-serve skew ,** when the data used for training differs from the data the model encounters in production. While LLMs often don’t require training or fine-tuning, a similar challenge arises. This happens when the data we test our systems on during development doesn’t accurately reflect the data they’ll face in production. If the two aren’t aligned, production accuracy can suffer.

### Types of Development-Production Skew

LLM data skew generally falls into two categories:

1. **Structural Skew:**

Structural Skew is caused by formatting issues, such as differences between a JSON dictionary with list-type values and a plain JSON list, errors like typos or sentence fragments.

These structural issues can affect model performance, as prompts are often sensitive to even minor formatting changes.

**2\. Content-Based Skew:**

Differences in the meaning or context of the data, also known as **semantic skew**.

To prevent skew from causing problems:

* Periodically measure the skew between development and production input/output pairs. Simple metrics like input/output lengths or formatting (e.g., JSON or XML compliance) can help track changes.
* For more advanced detection, cluster embeddings of input/output pairs can be used to identify **semantic drift**. For instance, if users start discussing new topics, this could signal areas the model hasn’t been exposed to.

When testing changes, such as prompt engineering, ensure that hold-out datasets are **current** and reflect recent user interactions. For example, if typos are common in production inputs, include typos in your hold-out dataset.

Beyond numerical metrics, qualitative assessments like regularly reviewing outputs are invaluable. These reviews ensure the model’s outputs align with expectations and user needs.

Finally, consider incorporating **nondeterminism** into skew checks. By running the pipeline multiple times for each input in your testing dataset and analyzing all outputs, you’re more likely to catch rare anomalies that might otherwise go unnoticed.

### 2.7. Review LLM Input-Output Samples Regularly

LLMs are dynamic systems. Despite their zero-shot capabilities and high-quality outputs, they can fail unpredictably. Regularly reviewing input-output pairs from production is essential for understanding how your LLM performs in real-world scenarios.

Production data is the real thing, a real place of LLM applications. It provides insights that can’t be substituted. Research suggests developers’ perceptions of “good” and “bad” outputs evolve as they interact with more data, a phenomenon known as **criteria drift**.

While it’s possible to define evaluation criteria upfront, these criteria are often incomplete. For instance, during development, you might refine prompts to improve response quality. Iterative testing and evaluation are necessary to align LLM behavior with human preferences.

### **2.8. Migrating Prompts Across Models is not easy as you think**

There’s nothing more frustrating than crafting a prompt that nails your use case only to have it break when you switch models. Whether you’re moving between providers or just upgrading to a newer version of the same model, things don’t always translate cleanly.

Swapping endpoints isn’t a magic fix. Even minor version bumps can shift model behavior in unexpected ways. If you’re migrating prompts, budget time for testing and tuning, not just for the switch, but to make sure your app still performs well after it. Having automated evals in place makes a huge difference. They give you a baseline, flag regressions, and save you from having to eyeball results one by one.

## 3\. Building better RAG Products

Even though we’re solving fresh challenges with LLMs and retrieval, the best results come when we stay grounded in tried-and-true product principles. Building something useful means focusing on what people actually need , not just what the model can do.

### 3.1 Involve Design Early and Often

When I started building RAG apps, I underestimated how valuable it was to have design in the room from the start. It’s not just about how the interface looks, it’s about how the _experience_ feels when a user is trying to get something done with an AI-backed system.

Designers are particularly good at helping us frame the user’s need in a way that makes it easier to solve. A good designer will help you spot mismatches between what users _think_ they’re asking and what your system _actually_ returns.

Their perspective forces better conversations early.

* When is RAG a good fit vs. a plain search or structured query?
* Does the UX help set the right expectations about what the model _can_ and _can’t_ do?

### 3.2 Design Your UX for Human-in-the-Loop

RAG systems benefit enormously from good human-in-the-loop UX. Whether it’s surfacing better grounding documents or letting users correct bad responses, the way we design feedback into the flow makes or breaks the long-term performance of the system.

Let’s say you’re building an internal RAG tool for answering policy questions from a knowledge base. Here are a few UX patterns you might consider:

1. **No feedback loop** — Model generates answers from retrieved docs, users can’t correct or flag anything.
2. **Manual override** — Users can flag incorrect responses or submit corrections, but it’s clunky.
3. **Structured feedback loop** — Users can review the sources used, tweak the retrieved results, and re-run the query.

Pattern #3 might take more effort, but it sets you up for continuous improvement. It builds user trust (“I can see _why_ this answer showed up”), gives them control (“I can adjust the source material”), and helps you collect implicit and explicit feedback to fine-tune both retrieval and generation.

This kind of loop is especially useful when:

* Users can upvote or downvote responses (explicit feedback)
* They adjust queries or re-rank source docs (implicit feedback)
* You log which sources were most often cited or ignored

The key is to treat feedback not as a burden but as a feature. RAG systems age well when their UX is designed to learn from interaction.

### 3.3 Prioritize Your Hierarchy of Needs Ruthlessly

When working on a RAG product, you have to balance a lot of things.

First up is reliability; can you count on the retrieval to give you the right context?

Then there’s groundedness; does the reply match what’s in the documents?

Usefulness is key too; does the answer really help the user?

Latency matters; can we get quick results even with lengthy contexts? ‘

Don’t forget about cost; are we wasting tokens on extra context or retries?

Lastly, safety and privacy are crucial; are we putting sensitive documents at risk in the answers?

If you try to optimize all of these at once, you’ll never ship. What’s worked for me is getting very clear on what’s non-negotiable for the use case.

For instance, in internal-facing tools, I might let latency take a back seat in the early version, so I can focus on grounding quality and retrieval relevance. In a customer-facing tool, safety and hallucination risk move to the top.

### 3.4 Calibrate Risk Tolerance to the Use Case

Not every RAG application needs the same level of scrutiny.

If I’m creating an assistant for customers to help them understand legal documents or health policies, then making sure the information is accurate and sources are clear is really important. A wrong answer isn’t just frustrating; it could lead to serious misunderstandings.

But for an internal RAG tool that helps engineers summarize architecture docs or generate test plans, I’ll take a bit more noise if it means moving faster and learning what users actually need.

The key is to align your bar for safety, factuality, and UX fidelity with the _impact_ of being wrong. Internal tools often give us more room to experiment and improve in a safer environment.

## Closing thoughts:

From my experience, building RAG apps is as much art as it is science. As the AI landscape evolves, stay flexible, what works today may need adjustment tomorrow.

Stay curious, iterate often, and keep user needs front and center. Doing so won’t just help you to create better products, it will make your product valuable.
