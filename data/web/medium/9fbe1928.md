## Embracing What Works, Improving What Doesn’t

Over the past few months, I’ve seen multiple _‘ChatGPT killers’_ , and the journey has been met with a great lesson: **those promises are never true**.

And quite frankly, the problem was pretty evident; they always insisted on touching what simply… worked.

### A Blessing and a Curse

In case you aren’t aware, we can create the powerful models we see today thanks to the mixing operator we describe as the attention mechanism.

[While I explain all there is to know about attention in this blog post](<https://thewhitebox.ai/the-attention-mechanism/>), the idea is that****[**Transformers**](<https://thewhitebox.ai/transformers-the-great-paradigm-shift/>)**process language by updating the meaning of each word in the sequence with its surrounding context.**

That way, words like ‘bat’ or ‘band,’ which have different meanings depending on the context, update their meaning by paying attention to the other words in the sequence (like ‘bat’ paying attention to ‘baseball’ to know it refers to a baseball bat and not an animal).

This way, **each word achieves a context-aware meaning** , and thus, the model builds an understanding over the entire sentence, allowing it to predict the next word.

_But why does attention work so well?_

Although this can be modified, as we’ll see in a minute, the principle __ is that every word in a sequence will pay attention to every other previous word (every single one). This concept, **known as global attention** , underpins the entire process.

This is done to ensure that, even though most relevant data will be close by (related words or topics are usually closer together in the text sequence), **if there’s an important relationship between words that are far apart, this relationship will be uncovered.**

For example, if the model is reading Chapter 9 in a book, but the character refers to an event that took place in Chapter 3, which is way back in the sequence, global attention ensures that the model will at least have the opportunity to identify this pattern and ‘recall’ what took place in Chapter 3, if necessary.

> Although our language has a high recency bias, in that related events to the one you are reading at any point are usually closer in time, some other modalities, like DNA, have extremely long-range dependencies in their data, with related genes sometimes being millions of bases apart.

But this globalized capacity is a blessing, and it’s also a curse.

Even though, to this day, no other AI architecture comes even close to the results and performance achieved by Transformer-based models with global attention, this capacity to look back indefinitely, which may sound like having infinite memory, is, in fact, the opposite: **Transformers don’t have memory**.

Or, at least, not a compressed one, which is a huge problem. _But what do I mean by that?_

### Caching Our Way to Success

The best way to understand how Transformers process data is through an example:

> Let’s say you are reading a book. If you’re on page 100 and want to read the first word in page 101 you… just read it. As you have built a compressed summary of what went on in the first 100 pages, you don’t need to reread them once again in order to read page 101.
>
> Transformers, on the contrary, to read page 101 have to re-read the first 100 pages every time it wants to read a new word. **It re-reads everything, every single time, for every single new word prediction.**

Luckily, we have a way of preventing the model from re-reading everything again. Described as the [KV Cache](<https://thewhitebox.ai/kv-cache-chatgpts-memory/>), it stores the attention results, the context-aware updated meaning, of previous words, meaning once we find the meaning of a word based on surrounding context once, we don’t have to recalculate it.

In summary, what the KV Cache allows is that this ‘word updating’ for every word with its surrounding context only has to be done for the newest word, **and the results of these exercises for previous words are cached.**

Now I know what you’re thinking: _“if a new word is added, wouldn’t that alter the meaning of previous words as they gain new additional surrounding context?”_

However, the answer is no, as these models are autoregressive, aka they only look back. The reason for this might not be very intuitive, so let’s see an example:

> Let’s say we have the sentence **‘The capitals of Finland and Sweden are…’** and the model predicts that the next word is, naturally, ‘Helsinki’. Therefore, to calculate the next word after Helsinki, the model will have to compute the attention of ‘Helsinki’ with all previous words.
>
> Luckily, we acknowledge that LLMs are causal models (usually referred to as autoregressive), meaning that words can only look back, not forward. Therefore, while we may generate a new set of words to continue the sequence, the attention values of previous words remain unchanged**because they are based on previous words, not future ones.**

Simply put, the attention that words like ‘Finland’ or ‘Sweden’ pay to previous words was computed earlier, and as the previous words in the sequence don’t change for new words coming afterward,**those results will always be the same.**

Therefore, we can cache — store — them so that we just need to compute attention for the newly generated word. But this, although it makes the process economically viable, **has some very important considerations.**

### From compute-bound to memory-bound

With the KV Cache, the computation costs of generation drop significantly. But there’s a catch.

While computation requirements fall, memory requirements skyrocket, as the longer the sequence, the more attention data must be cached.

> In the world of LLMs, something particularly important occurs when a model moves from the training phase to the inference phase: **the GPU usage bottleneck switches.**
>
> GPUs excel at matrix-matrix multiplication, which is the exact thing that takes place during training. We represent the neural network in matrix form,**which essentially means that computations between layers are a multiplication of matrices.**
>
> For that reason, during training, the process is compute-bound, meaning the model’s processing power is limited by the amount of compute the GPU processors can handle.
>
> However, in inference, as we have cached the previous attention scores, the problem becomes a **matrix-vector computation** between the newest predicted word and the rest of the sequence. Here, **GPUs here are heavily underutilized (with numbers usually well below 55% utilization).**
>
> In the contrary, the cache skyrockets memory demands, which, in simple terms, means that **you require many GPUs to run a model while, ironically, having around half of their processing power idle.**

In fact, the impact of the KV Cache is so large that, eventually,**the memory allocation for the cache grows larger than the actual model** , becoming the main memory bottleneck, as proven by [Hooper et al](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.18079>), reaching Terabytes of data for extremely long sequences:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*_dS89qmVSEqrorm9S4SMWg.png)

But that’s not all; we have yet another drawback.

### The latency problem in the prefilling stage

Generation (inference) is divided into two phases: **prefill** and **decode**.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:600/1*Xw0G43dl0hstMXF61Lo-3g.gif)

* The **prefill phase** occurs when the model receives the sequence for the first time, thereby having to compute attention for all words to generate the first new word. This is sometimes known as the** __ time-to-first token**, **where the KV Cache is filled at scale.**
* The **decode phase** occurs for all other generations. The word computes attention to all previous words using the KV Cache for each newly generated word. Although we need to account for the communication overhead between the memory and the GPU processors, **this is much faster, on a per-generated-token level, than the prefill phase** , as the latter has to compute attention for all words to create the cache.

Unsurprisingly, the prefill phase for long sequences takes a very long time, even minutes, for million-token-length sequences, a highly limiting factor for LLM deployment.

_But what if there was a way to find a compromise to solve these issues while still achieving great performance?_

Enter YOCO.

## You Only Cache Once

If you’ve reached here, understanding [YOCO](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.05254>) will be a walk in the park. As the proper acronym implies, this new architecture, an alternative to the Transformer, **only caches once.**

And that changes everything.

### Understanding how Transformers handle memory

Today, the standardized implementation of Transformers to model language, aka LLMs, is decoder-only Transformers.

Without going into much detail, it’s an architecture primed to generate new tokens based on the input sequence, like ChatGPT. In these architectures, **we cache every intermediate representation of words** , meaning the KV Cache is applied at every attention layer in layman's terms.

_But what does that mean?_

If we recall the attention mechanism, the standard procedure is to stack several attention mechanisms, known as Transformer blocks, recurrently updating the meaning of words concerning their preceding context. In other words, we don’t compute attention one single time; we do so dozens of times.

Therefore, the meaning of each word in relation to its surrounding context has been updated multiple times throughout the depth of the model.

> We also perform attention in parallel, known as multi-head attention, but that’s besides the point of this article so I will not get into it as it would complicate things too much.
>
> If required, [I cover it in my blog post](<https://thewhitebox.ai/the-attention-mechanism/>).

At earlier stages, the representations (the meaning of each word) may be simpler and individualized. As we go deeper into the network, the model develops a more globalized understanding of the sequence.

The point I’m trying to make is that, in standard architectures, we not only cache the last updated values of words,**but also their meaning in all intermediate representations.**

On the flip side, however, this causes the KV Cache to grow proportionally to the number of attention layers the model has. Therefore, **the deeper the model, the larger the KV Cache.**

So, _what does YOCO propose?_

### Why Don’t Just… Cache Only Once?

YOCO proposes a novel architecture that divides the standard decoder architecture into two: A self-decoder and a cross-decoder, as shown below:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*CbXWeidOVAcxFbVXIw5WUQ.png)

The questions this architecture aims to respond are the following:

* _Do we need to store the meaning of words across the entire network or simply store their meaning at a point in the network where we are confident the model understands each word well?_
* _Do we need to apply global attention at every point in the process, or can we use more efficient attention mechanisms without a too large decrease in performance?_

That probably sounded like hieroglyphics at first, but trust me, it isn’t that complicated.

### Self-Decoder

At first, the words in the input sequence are sent to the self-decoder. As mentioned, this decoder uses an efficient alternative to global attention, such as [sliding-window attention](<https://paperswithcode.com/method/sliding-window-attention>).

In this type of attention, the number of words one word can attend to is fixed. In other words, if our window is 1.000 words, and we are at word number 2.500, **instead of attending to all 2.499 previous words, this word can only attend to words between 1.500 and 2.499.**

Simply put, words only update their meaning with information provided by the K previous tokens, not the entire prior word sequence.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:580/1*uNuKckpIiXJm02CuBwWmqg.png)

As we can see above, the attention window for each word narrows, and thus, we not only reduce the KV Cache requirements but fix its size, too.

But I know what you’re thinking._Wouldn’t this induce the model to forget the past catastrophically?_ Using the earlier book analogy _, wouldn’t the model forget everything that took place in the earlier Chapters?_

> And the answer is, fascinatingly, no.

If we look at [Mistral’s 7B model](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06825>), we have a clear view of what’s really going on. In this toy example, they use a three-word window, meaning words can only pay attention to the previous two.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*V5t68C6zjlORNBkhpmdy1Q.png)

Therefore, while the second **_‘the’_** can only look back at **_‘sat’_** and **_‘on’_** , the latter two will look back into the first **_‘The’_** and **_‘cat’_** as they are in their respective attention windows.

Consequently, even though the second **_‘the’_** can’t see **_‘cat’_** , the information that **_‘cat’_** offers has been embedded into the meaning of **_‘sat’_** and **_‘on’._** In other words, the window ‘slides’ and information is still carried forward.

I know that was a mouthful, but that’s why we call it ‘sliding attention’: The window of attention slides through the sequence,**and newer tokens progressively carry information from past tokens into new ones.**

Circling back to YOCO, what YOCO does is the following:

1. It takes the input sequence through the self-decoder, which uses some sort of efficient attention (let’s assume it’s the sliding window method for the sake of simplicity)
2. Once it arrives at the last layer of the self-decoder, **it caches the attention results of all words and sends them to memory.**

And here’s the key element in the entire architecture. YOCO makes the assumption that by simply caching the attention scores of words in the last layer of the self-decoder is enough, as the model has built enough understanding at this point in the network and thus does not require any additional caching.

Hence, **this is the only time the model caches** , hence the architecture’s name. Now, we move into the cross-decoder.

### Cross-decoder

At this point, although the model already has a decent understanding of the sequence, we have to acknowledge that we have been using efficient attention, **which unequivocally affects the model's performance in lieu of a fixed, small KV Cache.**

Therefore, here, the model uses full global attention, but instead of recomputing the entire attention process, it uses the cache. In other words, in the cross-decoder phase, the new word will update its meaning only by using the KV Cache’s values, saving both computational and memory resources.

> I recommend looking at the earlier diagram to see how the cache participates in the entire process.

This has numerous implications:

* As mentioned, the KV Cache size is now fixed, determined by the efficient attention section (self-decoder), which has fixed-size attention windows. Thus, **the KV Cache doesn’t grow in size no matter how large the sequence is.**
* The KV Cache is smaller. As we are only caching the values of one layer instead of the dozens in Transformer architectures, the KV Cache is very small compared.
* The prefill stage can be computed at the early exit. As soon as we finish the self-decoder phase, we can kick-start the KV Cache storage. **This way, the time-to-first-word drops dramatically.**

In fact, here are some numbers.

1. **KV Cache reduction:** YOCO reduces the GPU memory consumption of KV caches by about **80 times** for 65B models compared to traditional Transformer models. For a 3B model, the memory consumption is reduced by more than **nine times for 1M tokens** and by two times for 32K tokens​.

> For example, at 65B model size, YOCO can serve 128K tokens with 1GB GPU memory, whereas a traditional Transformer can only support 1.6K tokens with the same memory, more than three orders of magnitude more efficient.

**2\. Prefilling Latency Reduction:** YOCO significantly speeds up the prefilling stage, **reducing the latency from 180 seconds to under six seconds for a 512K context length** , approximately**30.3 times faster**. For a 32K input, YOCO achieves about 2.87 times speedup in prefilling time.

**3\. Throughput Improvement:** The throughput, indicating how many tokens the model can process per second, shows substantial improvement. For instance, YOCO achieves a throughput of **43.1 tokens per second** for a 512K context length, about 9.6 times higher than the 4.5 tokens per second achieved by traditional Transformers.

In terms of performance, **YOCO still maintains competitive results across different model sizes, from 160M to 13B parameters.**

## Microsoft Does it Again.

If there’s one research lab pushing the efficiency revolution forward, it's Microsoft (and Mistral). From 1-bit LLMs to YOCO, they are trying to make these models run efficiently.

Of course, considering Microsoft’s role in the AI industry, OpenAI aside, is to serve models, it’s of great interest for them to make them easier to serve and cheaper to run. Nevertheless, [according to Azure’s CEO](<https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/microsoft-deploying-equivalent-of-five-561-petaflops-supercomputers-every-month/?utm_source=thetechoasis.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=microsoft-spyware-google-s-massive-bet-kan-networks>), they are adding $3 billion a month in NVIDIA H100 GPUs, so everything adds up.

While we still have to see YOCO ‘in the wild,’ and while I expect some performance degradation as it compromises both the attention mechanism and the model’s cache (lunch is never free), the insane memory benefits, which are crucial considering LLM inference is a memory-bound activity, make YOCO a very, very interesting architecture that could soon become widely used.

So while we can’t claim ‘Transformers are dead,’ the mantra ‘Transformers is all you need’ might soon become obsolete.
