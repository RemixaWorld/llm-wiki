Patients who visit hospitals to consult with doctors and medical practitioners about their health concerns have their diagnosis, treatment course and other medical details documented as Electronic Health Records (EHRs). In the US, for example, EHR use among office-based doctors has skyrocketed from 42% in 2008 to over 88% in 2021 [1], leading to a large portion of medical information now being stored in this format. These EHRs are used for various downstream activities such as ICD coding [2] which involves linking medical diagnoses and conditions to a standardized, unique alphanumeric code.

Given that EHRs are digitally organized as text, we can use automated methods to identify medical information and entities within the text and attempt to map them to corresponding ICD codes. However, ICD coding is a complex process — it requires accurately identifying diagnoses in the EHR, linking them with other relevant medical information and entities, and then mapping them to the correct ICD codes. This task requires strong Natural Language Understanding (NLU) skills as well as detailed knowledge of the ICD taxonomy. Generally, these tasks are performed by medical practitioners and clinical coders who have the specialized knowledge needed to accurately assign ICD codes. In this blog post, we explore how ICD codes can be represented as a Knowledge Graph (KG) and how to go about constructing this KG.

## Can we apply LLMs directly for ICD coding?

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for various clinical and medical applications, such as medical report summarization and entity extraction. This comes on the back of recent research showing that LLMs perform competitively on medical qualifying exams and on par with, or even better than, medical practitioners and doctors on medical question answering tasks [3, 4].

However, ICD coding differs significantly from tasks like summarization or entity extraction. As mentioned earlier, ICD coding demands strong Natural Language Understanding (NLU) abilities, along with an in-depth understanding of what each code represents. In the context of ICD coding, NLU involves tasks such as entity extraction and relation extraction to identify how medical entities in the text relate to each other.

LLMs are known to perform well on entity extraction and relation extraction in a zero-shot setting [5, 6, 7, 8]. However, they cannot be entirely relied upon to select the correct ICD code based just on their internalized, parametric knowledge. The ICD taxonomy includes over 70,000 hierarchically structured codes, meaning LLMs must precisely map clinical diagnoses within this vast and organized output space — a significantly more challenging task.

In practice, given the vast number of ICD codes, LLMs are likely to hallucinate when asked to predict an ICD code solely given its parameteric knowledge. We demonstrate this by prompting GPT-4o with ICD code descriptions and asking it to predict the corresponding ICD code and observe instances of hallucinations. In the leftmost example, we prompt the LLM with the description for ICD code **S46.301** which is**** “**Unspecified injury of muscle, fascia, and tendon of triceps, right arm** ”. In the example on the right, we use the description for ICD code **S48.11** which is **“Complete traumatic amputation at the level between right shoulder and elbow”**.

Relying on LLMs to perform ICD coding in a fully automated, end-to-end manner can lead to challenges*.

> *However, this does not mean these are unaddressable issues. LLMs can be used as dense retrievers by encoding the knowledge base into their parameters through fine-tuning. We can improve the retrieval workflow by prompting the LLM to suggest multiple relevant ICD codes and then applying post-processing steps to prune the predcitions. In this context, we focus on how we can utilize LLMs for ICD coding, given that we don’t fine-tune them and use them in a zero-shot fashion.

We approach ICD coding by breaking it down into language parsing and external knowledge querying. Particularly, we explore how ICD codes can be represented as a Knowledge Graph that can later be integrated with LLMs as external knowledge for querying ICD codes in a RAG setting. Additionally, we demonstrate how the zero-shot capabilities of LLMs in tasks like NER and relation extraction can be leveraged to efficiently construct this KG.

## What is a Knowledge Graph?

What exactly is a graph? In simple terms, a graph is a data structure that helps us map out connections between different entities — whether that’s people, devices, or even medical codes. Structurally, graphs are made up of nodes and edges. The nodes stand for the entities themselves, while the edges represent the relationships or interactions between them.

An example of a graph (Image by Authors)

Graphs make it easy to visualize and analyze complex systems, like social networks, where each node might represent a person and each edge a friendship, or computer networks, where nodes are devices and edges are the connections linking them. All in all, they are powerful methods for representing connected data and the relationships between different entities.

However, a simple graph does not provide any information about the specific type and nature of relationships that may exist between entities. For instance, given two entities representing persons A and B, a basic graph might simply indicate that these two individuals are connected, without revealing the nature of their connection. Is it a familial bond, a professional relationship, or a friendship?

This is where Knowledge Graphs come into play. A KG enhances a basic graph structure by embedding semantic information — additional details that define the meaning of the relationships between nodes. Unlike a simple graph, a KG can capture hierarchies, attributes, and more complex, nuanced connections between entities.

## Why KGs for ICD coding?

**Why use KGs for ICD coding?** ICD codes provide a detailed framework for categorizing diseases, symptoms, conditions, and medical procedures in a hierarchical structure, where broad categories branch out into increasingly specific subtypes. KGs are a perfect fit for such structures because they can model these complex relationships. KGs can naturally capture parent-child structures and reflect how different codes are related. This makes them ideal for representing the ICD taxonomy.

Beyond simply capturing these hierarchical relationships, KGs provide another benefit by linking the interconnected entities represented by ICD codes. For instance, a KG can depict not just the structure of ICD codes but also the relationships between various diseases, symptoms, and procedures. This enables us to better understand how a particular disease may relate to its associated symptoms, treatments, or risk factors.

KGs can be integrated with LLMs, particularly in the context of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and accessing external knowledge sources [9, 10]. RAG combines LLMs with external knowledge bases to improve the accuracy and relevance of responses. In this setup, KGs serve as a structured knowledge base that LLMs can query during generation, providing more contextually aware and factually grounded outputs.

## Creating the KG

How do we approach creating the KG for ICD codes? Each ICD code is typically represented by a plain-text description [2] that captures the essence of the code. Using these descriptions, we build a graph for each ICD code to capture its key relationships and attributes. Once all individual ICD code graphs are created, we merge them into a single, unified KG representing the entire ICD coding system.

### Graph Creation Process

The construction of the KG involves three main steps:

1. **Named Entity Recognition (NER)**
2. **Relation Extraction (RE)**
3. **Entity Linking (EL)**

Representation of the graph creation process (Image by Authors). Representation inspired from “Information Extraction Pipeline” figure in article [11].

### Named Entity Recognition

**Named Entity Recognition** (NER) is the process of identifying and extracting entities from unstructured text. In the context of ICD coding, NER helps us locate and classify key medical terms and entities, such as diseases, symptoms, and treatments, within clinical documents. For example, NER can identify “diabetes” as a**** disease and “chest pain” as a symptom.

NER is an important step in the KG construction process, as it helps us identify the nodes in our eventual graph. It is crucial that the tool we use for NER is highly accurate as faulty detections and errors end up adding noise to our KG.

### Relation Extraction

After identifying the entities, the next step is **Relation Extraction** — determining how**** different entities are related to one another. In the context of ICD coding, this could include links between medical entities such symptoms and condition or disease and treatment. For example, a relation could exist between the condition “chest pain**”** to the possible diagnosis “myocardial infarction”. This step is crucial for constructing the edges **(** Relation**)** between our nodes **(** Entities**)** in the graph.

### Entity Linking

**Entity Linking** ensures that identified entities and relationships are clear and consistent, by connecting them to a specific concept. In the medical context, many terms have synonyms or variations, such as “heart attack” and “myocardial infarction”. Entity Linking aligns these terms to a single, unified concept. This step helps maintain consistency in the graph and allows for more accurate querying.

### Storing and Querying the graph

Once the graph is created, it needs to be stored in a database that can efficiently handle graph data and allow for easy querying. We can use a **graph database** , which is designed to store nodes and edges — representing entities and their relationships — to store our graphs. This allows us to query and retrieve information from the graph.

### How can we utilize LLMs for KG creation?

We use LLMs in the **initial stages of graph creation** to identify named entities and the relationships that link them. For entity detection, we observe that models from libraries like Scispacy [12] do not fully cover the entity types we need to extract for building our graphs. Scispacy’s default entity extraction model identifies all medical entities from the text but does not provide the type of entity. We use LLMs for these tasks, as they allow us to flexibly describe the specific entities we want to extract and link, without requiring specialized training.

The first step is to determine which specific types of entities need to be extracted. Categories such as medical conditions, body parts, and symptoms are obvious candidates, but it is also important to consider additional entity types.

12. **other_info**

These entity types represent the spectrum of medical entities that can be found in ICD code descriptions. An explanation of what each entity type represents can be found in the prompt we use for performing NER and RE provided below.

### Relation Extraction

After identifying entities from an ICD description, we proceed to identify and link related entities. For this purpose, we consider all possible relationships between medical entities. However, in most cases, the primary anchor is the diagnosis corresponding to the ICD code, which serves as the central entity around which other entities are linked.

### Entity Linking

After extracting entities and relationships, we normalize all entities by linking them to the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) [13]. Developed by the U.S. National Library of Medicine, UMLS integrates various health and biomedical vocabularies and standards, such as SNOMED-CT and MeSH. Each medical entity is linked to a unique identifier called a Concept Unique Identifier (CUI).

Once linked to UMLS, we use the canonical names of the entities extracted by the LLM. For linking, we use Scispacy’s entity linker, which matches entities to UMLS concepts based on 3-gram character similarities. Since we have already extracted the entities, we bypass Scispacy’s default entity extraction pipeline and directly pass our extracted entities to the CandidateGenerator object. This process provides likely UMLS concept IDs, from which we select the concept ID with the highest similarity score to our input.

```python candidate_generator = CandidateGenerator(name="umls")entity_linker = EntityLinker(resolve_abbreviations=True, name="umls", candidate_generator=candidate_generator)def get_max_similarity_concept_id(candidates): """ Determines the concept ID with the maximum similarity score from a list of candidate concepts. Args: candidates (list): A list of MentionCandidate objects, each containing a 'similarities' list and a 'concept_id'. Returns: any: The concept ID associated with the highest similarity score in the given candidates list, or None if the list is empty. """ max_similarity = float('-inf') best_concept_id = None for candidate in candidates: candidate_max_similarity = max(candidate.similarities) if candidate_max_similarity > max_similarity: max_similarity = candidate_max_similarity best_concept_id = candidate.concept_id return best_concept_iddef normalize_entities(entities): """ Extracts the top linked concept for the first recognized entity in a given text using scispaCy. Parameters: - text (str): The input text to extract entities from. Returns: - str: The normalized entity if identified, or the original entity text. ### Building the KG

After extracting entities and relations from each ICD description, we proceed to construct the KG. We start by parsing all entities and relations from the LLM predictions. Next, we standardize the entities by mapping them to their UMLS canonical names and add them as nodes in the graph. We create edges between nodes that share a relationship based on the LLM predictions.

Additionally, a node representing the ICD code is added to the graph, and edges are created between this node and each extracted entity, facilitating future graph queries where we need to retrieve the ICD code. The graph is constructed using the NetworkX library.

A graph constructed for a single ICD code after running our pipeline (Image by Authors) ```python def parse_entities(lines, normalized_entity_map): """ Parses entity information from the provided lines. Args: lines (list): List of strings containing entity data in the format "EntityType: EntityName1 || EntityName2". normalized_entity_map (dict): Dictionary mapping original entity names to their normalized forms. Returns: tuple: A tuple containing: - entities (dict): A dictionary where keys are normalized entity names and values are entity types. - overall_entities (set): A set of all entity names encountered. """ entities = {} overall_entities = set() for line in lines: if ':' in line: entity_type, entity_names = line.split(':', 1) entity_type = entity_type.strip() entity_names = [name.strip() for name in entity_names.split("||")] for entity_name in entity_names: overall_entities.add(entity_name) entities[normalized_entity_map[entity_name]] = entity_type return entities, overall_entitiesdef parse_relations(lines): """ Parses relation information from the provided lines. Args: lines (list): List of strings containing relation data in the format "(EntityName1 || EntityName2)". Returns: list: A list of tuples representing relations, where each tuple is a pair of related entity names. """ relations = [] for line in lines: if line.startswith('(') and line.endswith(')'): entity_names = [e.strip() for e in line[1:-1].split('||')] if len(entity_names) == 2 and all(entity_names): relations.append((entity_names[0], entity_names[1])) return relationsdef build_graph(input_text, icd_code, icd_description, normalized_entity_map): """ Builds a graph based on entity and relation information extracted from input text. Args: input_text (str): Text containing entity and relation information. icd_code (str): ICD code for the graph root node. icd_description (str): Description of the ICD code. normalized_entity_map (dict): Dictionary mapping original entity names to their normalized forms. Returns: networkx.Graph: A NetworkX graph representing the entities, relations, and the ICD root node. """ lines = input_text.strip().split('
') entities_section, relations_section = split_sections(lines) entities, overall_entities = parse_entities(entities_section, normalized_entity_map) relations = parse_relations(relations_section) G = create_graph(icd_code, icd_description, entities, overall_entities, relations, normalized_entity_map) return Gdef split_sections(lines): """ Splits the input lines into separate sections for entities and relations. Args: lines (list): List of strings containing the lines of the input text. Returns: tuple: A tuple containing: - entities_section (list): List of strings corresponding to the "Entities:" section. - relations_section (list): List of strings corresponding to the "Relations:" section. """ entities_section = [] relations_section = [] current_section = None for line in lines: line = line.strip() if line == 'Entities:': current_section = entities_section elif line == 'Relations:': current_section = relations_section elif line and current_section is not None: current_section.append(line) return entities_section, relations_sectiondef create_graph(icd_code, description, entities, overall_entities, relations, normalized_entity_map): """ Creates a NetworkX graph using the provided ICD code, entities, and relations. Args: icd_code (str): ICD code for the root node of the graph. description (str): Description of the ICD code. entities (dict): Dictionary of entities with their types. overall_entities (set): Set of all entity names encountered. relations (list): List of tuples representing relations between entities. normalized_entity_map (dict): Dictionary mapping original entity names to their normalized forms. Returns: networkx.Graph: A NetworkX graph representing the entities, relations, and the ICD root node. """ G = nx.Graph() G.add_node(icd_code, type="ICD", description = description) for entity_name, entity_type in entities.items(): G.add_node(entity_name, type=entity_type) G.add_edge(entity_name, icd_code) for entity1, entity2 in relations: if entity1 in overall_entities and entity2 in overall_entities: entity1_normalized = normalized_entity_map[entity1] entity2_normalized = normalized_entity_map[entity2] G.add_edge(entity1_normalized, entity2_normalized) return G ``` ``` graphs_list = []for key, value in tqdm(extracted_graphs.items()): icd_description = icd_code_description[key] graph = build_graph(value, key, icd_description, normalized_entity_map) graphs_list.append(graph)kg = nx.compose_all(graphs_list) ```

### Index to GraphDB

We use Neo4j [14], a popular graph database, for indexing our graph. For our use case, we utilize a free instance provided through Neo4j Aura online. We first connect to our instance and then index our nodes and relationships in a batched process using the **neo4j** package in Python.

```python from neo4j import GraphDatabaseimport networkx as nxBATCH_SIZE = 1000uri = ""username = ""password = ""def create_nodes(tx, nodes): """ Creates or updates nodes in the Neo4j database. This function takes a list of nodes, and for each node, it either creates a new node or updates an existing one based on the node's `id`. The attributes of each node are set or updated as specified in the `attributes` field. Args: tx (neo4j.Transaction): The active Neo4j transaction. nodes (list): A list of dictionaries, where each dictionary represents a node with the following structure: - 'id': The unique identifier for the node. - 'attributes': A dictionary of key-value pairs representing node attributes. """ tx.run( """ UNWIND $nodes AS node MERGE (n:Node {id: node.id}) SET n += node.attributes """, nodes=nodes )def create_relationships(tx, relationships): """ Creates or updates relationships between nodes in the Neo4j database. This function takes a list of relationships and for each relationship, it either creates a new relationship or updates an existing one based on the source and target node identifiers (`source_id` and `target_id`). The relationship's attributes are set or updated as specified in the `attributes` field. Args: tx (neo4j.Transaction): The active Neo4j transaction. relationships (list): A list of dictionaries, where each dictionary represents a relationship with the following structure: - 'source_id': The unique identifier of the source node. - 'target_id': The unique identifier of the target node. - 'attributes': A dictionary of key-value pairs representing relationship attributes. """ tx.run( """ UNWIND $relationships AS rel MATCH (a:Node {id: rel.source_id}) MATCH (b:Node {id: rel.target_id}) MERGE (a)-[r:RELATES_TO]-(b) SET r += rel.attributes """, relationships=relationships )def create_index(tx): """ Creates a unique constraint on the `id` property of the `Node` label in the Neo4j database. This function ensures that the `id` property of each `Node` is unique, preventing the creation of nodes with duplicate `id` values. It is typically used for enforcing data integrity and speeding up lookup operations. Args: tx (neo4j.Transaction): The active Neo4j transaction. """ tx.run("CREATE CONSTRAINT FOR (n:Node) REQUIRE n.id IS UNIQUE") ``` ``` driver = GraphDatabase.driver(uri, auth=(username, password))with driver.session() as session: session.write_transaction(create_index) nodes = [ {'id': node_id, 'attributes': attributes} for node_id, attributes in kg.nodes(data=True) ] relationships = [ { 'source_id': source_id, 'target_id': target_id, 'attributes': attributes } for source_id, target_id, attributes in kg.edges(data=True) ] for i in range(0, len(nodes), BATCH_SIZE): batch = nodes[i:i+BATCH_SIZE] session.write_transaction(create_nodes, batch) for i in range(0, len(relationships), BATCH_SIZE): batch = relationships[i:i+BATCH_SIZE] session.write_transaction(create_relationships, batch) driver.close() ```

### Querying the Graph

After indexing our graph in Neo4j, we can begin querying it using Cypher [15], Neo4j’s declarative query language for graph data interaction. Cypher provides a powerful and flexible approach for querying our graphs.

In our initial example, we query for all ICD codes related to lacerations that can occur on the right eyelid. Here, “laceration” represents the condition, “eyelid” represents the body part, and “right” indicates the laterality. Relationships link the condition to the body part and the laterality to the body part. We encode these constraints in a Cypher query, allowing us to retrieve the relevant results.

## Conclusion and Future Work

In this blog post, we explore how to represent ICD codes as a structured Knowledge Graph (KG), as well as how to index and query them. Using a KG in this way allows for deeper integration with Large Language Models (LLMs) in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) setups, helping us generate more factually grounded outputs. We plan to explore such integrations in our upcoming work.

However, our approach has certain limitations.

1. For entity and relationship extraction, we use GPT-4o mini, a smaller LLM that comes with performance limitations and errors that can propagate into the final KG. Addressing these challenges would require more powerful or specialized models.
2. Although the Scispacy entity linker is generally effective, it can introduce errors in entity linking and standardization during KG creation. We also plan to explore if other medical knowledge bases and repositories such as PubMed articles could be integrated into the KG creation process to incorporate more domain knowledge.
