## The Ultimate Black Box

Although we have great intuition about how neural networks learn, [a topic I recently wrote about in my blog](<https://thewhitebox.ai/neural-networks-why-do-they-work/>), models growing into architectures with billions of parameters are still a complete black box, to the point that the name of my company, [**TheWhiteBox**](<https://thewhitebox.ai/>), is inspired by this problem.

So, firstly, _what is_** _mechanistic interpretability_** _, the field that aims to correct this problem?_

In simple terms, this field aims to identify the key patterns of knowledge a network possesses and how they are related to its parameters to predict its behavior.

As you probably know by now, neural networks like ChatGPT are made out of neurons (although the appropriate name is ‘hidden units’) located in the ‘hidden layers’ below:

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*6Q5GoMFn4wrQVEU8)

When prompted, these neurons fire (or not), and combining these elements helps the model generate data, such as a sewing learning guide or a narration of the battle of Gettysburg.

Simply put, **the model’s output depends on how these neurons combine.** However, demystifying these combinations, a seemingly harmless activity,**is one of the great unsolved problems in AI today.**

_But why?_

### The Beautiful Act of Compression

At their core, Generative AI models are data compressors. In other words, they are trained to compress data.

However, it is known that neural networks apply superposition to capture more facts than neurons they possess. The reason for such behavior is that they are fed training data three or four orders of magnitude (at least x100) larger than the model and tasked with learning and regenerating it.

Thus, they must learn a compression of that data that allows them to, when prompted, **‘recover’ the original data despite being much smaller**.

As the model is much smaller, rote memorization is not an option, as data simply wouldn’t fit in the model.

> In fact, that would turn the model into a database of the same size as the original training data, which is a pointless exercise; what we want is a small generative model that still represents the entire data and can be queried about it.

Thus, this smaller model is forced to absorb only the key patterns in the training data. However, compression is not only a matter of efficiency but also of developing intelligence.

### Compression is Intelligence

As I’ve mentioned many times, this is why researchers worldwide think LLMs are the — current — holy grail of AI; the impressive act of compression (just keeping the essential and discarding the irrelevant)**these models achieve is a clear sign of their intelligence** (even if they are pretty dumb if compared to humans).

_But what do I mean by learning the essentials?_ To understand why compression is an act of intelligence, we can take humans as a reference.

> For example, instead of learning every sentence by heart, humans learn about grammar and syntax, aka how words are written and how they commonly follow each other, to extrapolate that knowledge into new sentences without requiring rote memorization.
>
> Humans know ‘I banana eat’ is not a correct sentence without having to memorize every single way those three words follow each other.

This is precisely what LLMs do also, but we can’t really explain why or how, which is the point of mechanistic interpretability.

_But why am I telling you all this?_

Simple. Compression is a desired feature of these models, but it also implies a hard and unavoidable truth:

> Their smallest information compression and storage units, the neurons, are polysemantic.

### The hard truth

As mentioned earlier, neural networks apply superposition to amass more knowledge data points than neurons they have.

Consequently, for this to be feasible, **each neuron becomes knowledgeable in a wide range of semantically unrelated topics**. For instance, the same neuron may fire whenever the model generates Shakespeare’s poetry and when writing about tropical frogs.

And this, my dear reader, is a problem.

If neurons were monosemantic, uncovering the LLM’s knowledge and how it elicits that knowledge would be a walk on the park.

However, the fact that they are polysemantic makes individualizing each neuron to uncover ‘what they know’ and, thus, mapping the entire network’s knowledge, outright impossible.

Luckily, in October 2023, Anthropic made a huge discovery: while neurons are unequivocally polysemantic, **certain combinations of them are monosemantic.**

> In layman’s terms, although the outcome of a model was unpredictable based solely on one neuron’s behavior, whenever a set of neurons fired together, the outcome was always the same.

In other words, **the same neurons fired together when the model generated text from a particular topic.** This can be seen in the toy diagram below, where a specific combination of two hidden neurons (both neurons fire) forces the model to output Shakespeare verses.

![Created by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*EXqSae3IKQ2w26x7gbtFgw.png)

However, the issue was that the discovery was limited, as the neural network they managed to study was very, very small, meaning that monosemantic neuron combinations were a field of promise but not reality.

However, after Anthropic’s bright new research, it’s a reality now.

## A Sonnet of Hope

Recently, the same team that discovered monosemantic neuron combinations [released a paper](<https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/index.html#appendix-methods-steering>) in which they applied the same principle to **Claude 3 Sonnet** , their midrange production frontier model, which is currently the ninth-best LLM in the world.

_But what did they do specifically?_

### Interpreting frontier AI models

They analyzed the model’s activations and trained a parallel model that transformed them into interpretable features.

In layman’s terms, they trained a model that looked at how neurons in Sonnet fired, and predicted what key abstract features the combination of these neurons represented, creating a ‘feature map’ that researchers could interpret. In other words, **they found a ‘map’ between world concepts and certain neuron combinations.**

For instance, they discovered a feature specifically for San Francisco’s “Golden Gate Bridge”:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*z8NkQJWH-2rS04qA)

Importantly, these features were multimodal (reacting to both words and images related to that monument) and multilingual (reacting to the same concepts in other languages).

Additionally, they found features about famous people, monuments, arts, etc.

_But how did they do this?_

### Reconstructing induces learning

To achieve this, they trained a **sparse autoencoder (SAE)** , a model that took the activations and turned them into real-life features, and then tried to reconstruct the activations back.

Two components form a SAE:

* An **encoder** , which takes the activations from the model and applies a linear transformation into a higher dimensional space to transform them into features. In layman’s terms, the model finds patterns such as ‘if neurons fire in a certain way, they usually refer to Lebron James’.
* A **decoder** takes the features and applies another linear transformation to return the features back to the original activations.

In other words, they trained a model that could' dissect’ neuron activations into more fine-grained data — the interpretable features — but also reconstruct the original data — going back to the original activations.

> The reconstruction part is not done ‘just because’.
>
> By forcing models to reconstruct the original data, they must learn how that data is distributed originally, **which is equivalent to truly understanding it.**

As shown below, for words like ‘Golden’, ‘Gate’, or ‘Bridge’, the neurons of the model activate in the direction of the ‘Golden Gate’ feature. This, in turn, means that these features are added as relevant information to perform the next-word prediction, which induces the model to output ‘San Francisco’ as the American city where this monument is located.

![Created by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Zpf_xOmFn0S5VfYHcqcerg.png)

Long story short, the process is as follows:

1. They trained an SAE that took the activations of the LLM and transformed them into real-life features.
2. They analyzed how neurons activated for every word in the sequence. For example, they found that whenever a set of neurons fired, the word was always related to the Golden Gate Bridge, being able to identify that feature as _“Golden Gate Bridge”_.
3. Therefore, they found the set of features that influenced the generation of every word in the sequence, making the totally opaque world of neurons interpretable.

However, there could be thousands of these features, _right?_

The word ‘husky’ can be directly related to many different features: <dog>, <mammal>, <hairy>, <animal>, <domestic>, and so on, which would make the process of finding the key features that explain each word a nightmare.

Hence, **they forced the SAE to be sparse** , meaning that a handful of features should only represent every word in the sequence. That way, **they could pinpoint the exact real-life concept these neurons were ‘referring’ to.**

> **Technical insight:** To enforce sparsity, they added a penalty term to the objective loss function of the SAE. This penalty term calculated the determinant of the decoder matrix, meaning that the larger its value, the larger the penalty. This naturally induced the model to assign a valid yet sparse matrix.
>
> By doing this, they also gained another benefit: the decoder’s matrix, the matrix that takes the features and transforms them back into activations, represents the model's feature space. In layman’s terms, the decoder matrix’s columns are the different possible features that could be ‘explaining’ any given word.
>
> Therefore, it acts as a “feature look-up”, as the model just needs to see what columns participate in the reconstruction to know what features explain those particular activations. For instance, if the words are ‘Golden Gate’ one of the columns in the decoder activated would be the column referring to the ‘Golden Gate’ feature.

_But why does all this matter?_

> Simple, because by knowing how neurons combine to generate data related to a specific topic, we can predict its behavior… Or, crucially, steer it.

To prove this, using the Golden Gate Bridge example again, they clamped that specific combination (forcing these specific neurons to activate more intensely), **which forced the model to actually think it was the Golden Gate Bridge** :

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*EM6RL51j0m359_i5oEyE0w.png)

Of course, that is simply an interesting yet irrelevant response, but the key is that they proved that we could actually “clamp up” or “clamp down” specific topics at our pleasing.

Interesting, _but what does all this mean to the industry, good and bad?_

## Predictable yet Censorable?

Unsurprisingly, researchers found numerous undesirable features (lying, deception, power-seeking behavior, or even violent responses).

### Dialing down violence

Using this discovery, we could eventually ‘dial down’ or even prohibit such neuron combinations so that the model never generates that sort of data, no matter how insisting the user is.

This is crucial considering how easily jailbreakable models today are.

> For instance, while the model may reject a harmful request in English, it may comply in languages that have not been ‘aligned’. In layman’s terms, the training phase where we explain the model things like ‘whenever they ask you to build a bomb, don’t answer’ is done primarily in English, and not in much lesser known languages.
>
> However, as **features are multilinguistic** , prohibiting certain neuron combinations will cause the model to refuse the harmful prompt independently of the language.

That said, these preventive measures would only work as long as the attacker doesn’t have access to the actual model. A simple fine-tuning of the model could modify it completely and easily break the alignment guardrail.

> And as proven by recent [research by Nanda et al](<https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/jGuXSZgv6qfdhMCuJ/refusal-in-llms-is-mediated-by-a-single-direction>), we even don’t have to fine-tune the model, and we can simply eliminate the ‘safety feature’ for the model to go rogue automatically, making matters worse.

Nonetheless, this breakthrough is extremely important, as in a more futuristic event we have to deal with superhuman models, models that are superior to us in every single way, knowing how they process data could not only help us discover achieve new breakthroughs,**but could also help us learn how to control and prevent them from going rogue.**

However, as with any discovery in AI these days, there is also the flip side.

### Toward a lobotomized society

Picture a world where everyone uses the same LLMs assembled by for-profit corporations.

In that scenario, _what prevents these companies from using these capabilities to censor certain ways of thinking, manipulate, or gaslight society to see the world in a way that benefits the interests of such corporations or the governments behind them?_

We must acknowledge that even though LLMs will create immense value for society, using them as the only gateway for knowledge — as they are clearly positioning themselves to be — could be extremely detrimental.

> As everyone is funneled into the same ways of thinking, we lose the capacity to have an open mind.

This seems exaggerated, but AI algorithms are already manipulating society in every way. If a certain social network only pushes harmful content into the feed of your kids about a certain race or religion, _how will our kids grow up but despise anything our AI-governed feeds don’t embrace?_

I do not condemn corporations' vital role in pushing the industry forward; I’m sure their products will generate immense value for society.

But remember, their shareholders come first, which is why, now more than ever, **open-source needs to survive.**
