## **Instruction Tuning for LLMs: What and Why?**

Instruction tuning is the process of further training LLMs on a dataset of `(INSTRUCTION, OUTPUT)` pairs in a supervised manner, where `INSTRUCTION` represents the human instruction for the model and `OUTPUT` represents the desired output that follows the `INSTRUCTION`. This process aligns the next-word prediction objective of LLMs with the goal of making them follow human instructions.

A key challenge for LLMs is the gap between their training goals and user expectations. LLMs are typically trained to predict words, but users want helpful, safe, instruction-following responses.

Instruction Tuning is an effective approach to bridge this gap by:

1. Aligning the model’s focus from word prediction to instruction following.
2. Making the model’s behavior more controllable and predictable.
3. Enabling quick adaptation to specific domains without extensive retraining or architectural modifications.

## Instruction-Tuning Data: Quality or Quantity?

[LIMA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11206v1>), [LLaMa3](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783v2>), and [Yi](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.04652v1>) all discuss the importance of high-quality fine-tuning data, asserting that “quality is all you need.” They emphasize focusing on improving sample quality rather than simply increasing the quantity.

In instruction tuning, quality outweighs quantity — high-quality samples are much more effective than a large number of low- to medium-quality ones. Typically, a few thousand samples can yield strong results, and in some business scenarios, even 80 to a few hundred high-quality samples may be sufficient.

## How to Ensure High-Quality Data?

Given the importance of quality, a crucial question arises: How can we assess the quality of instruction-tuning samples?

This topic deserves its own discussion because it involves intricate and painstaking work.

To assess data quality, there are many methods that combine traditional NLP algorithms with LLMs. Some effective approaches include [DEITA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.15685v2>), [CaR](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18191v2>), [LESS](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04333v3>), [IFD](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12032v5>), and [MoDS](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.15653v1>).

* [DEITA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.15685v2>) emphasizes efficiency, using minimal data to achieve alignment through complexity, quality, and diversity metrics.
* [CaR](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18191v2>) combines expert-aligned scoring with clustering to maintain both quality and diversity in instruction data.
* [LESS](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.04333v3>) uses gradient similarity to target specific capabilities with a small but effective subset of data.
* [IFD](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12032v5>) introduces an autonomous approach, selecting impactful “cherry-picked” data based on difficulty.
* [MoDS](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.15653v1>) optimizes data selection by balancing quality, coverage, and necessity to address model-specific gaps.

Overall, these methods illustrate a spectrum of strategies, from reducing data volume to ensuring diverse and targeted selection for optimal instruction tuning.

## Data Diversity vs. Quality: Which is More Important?

“[Random Selection is Almost All You Need](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09335v1>)” reveals that random data selection proves to be an effective strategy. Notably, when handling large-scale datasets, most existing data selection methods struggle to significantly outperform this random approach.

## How to Ensure Data Diversity?

This question could also be a topic for a separate article. **Here are some practical experiences to ensure diversity**.

* Enrich instruction expressions by avoiding repetitive structures and incorporating varied contexts. For instance, transform “write code” instructions into real-world scenarios like job interviews or teachers instructing students in program design.
* Balance instruction and reference answer lengths, mixing short and long examples. This prevents information loss in lengthy content processing while training the model to generate diverse, comprehensive responses.
* To train the model to understand multi-turn dialogue contextual relevance, some queries in data should be related to the current session, while others should be unrelated.
* Obtain instruction datasets with real-world scenarios from diverse sources: for example, [LIMA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11206v1>) is manually curated from sources with helpful responses (such as Reddit), while another approach is to curate real-world user interactions from the internet using state-of-the-art LLMs, such as [LMSYS-CHAT-1M](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11998v4>) and [WILDCHAT](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.01470v1>).
* When designing instructions, it’s crucial to consider real-world scenarios. Don’t expect the model to accomplish tasks that even humans can’t do in one attempt, such as writing a complex backend service. Instead, we should break down more challenging tasks into manageable steps.

In addition to the practical experiences, previous quality algorithms have made some efforts to enhance diversity in data selection. For example, [MoDS](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.15653v1>) uses BERT to create sentence embeddings, calculating distances between data points to form a seed instruction dataset with extensive diversity and broad coverage. [IFD](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12032v5>) applies K-means clustering, then samples from each cluster.

In essence, my understanding is that data diversity should ensure: **The data structure should not reveal predictable patterns, and key information within samples must be distributed randomly**. This aims to prevent the model from focusing only on specific tokens or positions during training, encouraging it to process the entire sample instead.

## How to Prevent Task-Specific Fine-Tuning from Compromising General Instruction-Following?

Due to distributional differences between task-specific datasets and LLMs, supervised fine-tuning often enhances the model’s performance on specific tasks while compromising its general instruction-following abilities.

**What methods can be used to alleviate this problem? The following idea can serve as a reference.**

As illustrated in Figure 4, the [self-distillation fine-tuning (SDFT)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.13669v2>) method employs the LLM to rewrite the task dataset prior to fine-tuning.

Figure 4: Unlike vanilla fine-tuning, which may compromise seed LMs, the proposed self-distillation finetuning (SDFT) approach enhances seed LMs with improved downstream task performance while largely maintaining broad capabilities already learned. Source: [SDFT](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.13669v2>).

How to rewrite data? As illustrated in Figure 5, input both the instruction and the original response into the LLM, then let it generate a rewritten response.

Figure 5: The distillation template used in most of the experiments. It designates the original response as “reference answer” and prompts the model to generate a response using the reference answer as a guide. Source: [SDFT](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.13669v2>).

As Figure 6 illustrates, while vanilla fine-tuning enhances performance on the target task, it often comes at the cost of diminished performance on previously well-performing tasks. In contrast, SDFT mitigates this forgetting effect, achieving comparable or even superior performance across a wide range of tasks.

This approach narrows the distributional gap between the rewritten task dataset and the LLM. Consequently, fine-tuning on such a dataset induces minimal distributional changes to the LLM, thereby mitigating the potential damage to its general capabilities.

**Overall, in practice, whether to rewrite depends on the specific scenario. If the rewriting process yields a style similar to the original, it’s unnecessary to rewrite.**

## Which Instruction-Tuning Method Should We Choose for a Specific Task?

Instruction tuning methods primarily include:

* **Full-Model Tuning (FMT)** : The most basic fine-tuning approach, which optimizes all parameters of the LLM.
* [**Prompt tuning**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08691v2>): This method optimizes only the prompt parameters during fine-tuning. It adjusts only the prompt-related parameters in the model (such as newly added learnable prompt embeddings), rather than directly modifying the weights of the model’s core architecture.
* [**Low-Rank Adaptation** (LoRA)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09685v2>): Instead of altering the LLM’s input, LoRA updates pre-trained model weights using a trainable pair of low-rank decomposition matrices. This approach enables the model to learn task-specific patterns while preserving most of its pre-trained knowledge, thus minimizing overfitting risk and enhancing training efficiency.

[Prompt tuning](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08691v2>) and [LoRA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09685v2>) are parameter-efficient tuning methods.

As indicated in a [recent study](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.17193v1>), the choice of fine-tuning method should be based on the number of available fine-tuning examples:

* For datasets with only a few thousand examples, Parameter-Efficient Tuning (PET) methods like Prompt tuning or LoRA should be considered first.
* As the dataset size increases slightly, LoRA becomes preferable due to its stability and better scalability with finetuning data.
* For million-scale datasets, Full-Model Tuning (FMT) would be the optimal choice.

**Overall, based on my experience, if computational resources are sufficient, I recommend using Full-Model Tuning (FMT). Otherwise, consider optimization approaches like LoRA.**

## What Details Should be Noted Regarding Fine-Tuning?

Here are some specific practical experiences:

* If configuration and resources are sufficient, try to choose larger models for fine-tuning. The reason is that larger models are more stable.
* For smaller models, set a higher learning rate; for larger models, set a lower learning rate. Additionally, when dealing with a small amount of data, it is recommended to set a lower learning rate.
* The number of epochs is typically 1 to 3. If there are few fine-tuning samples, we can set a larger number of epochs.
* For initial fine-tuning, incorporate a warmup period and experiment with several learning rate schedulers. It is advisable to use a smaller `warmup_ratio` than what’s typically used in pre-training.
* Loss is not necessarily better the lower it gets; the final loss may be around 0.5. If the loss is too small, it’s necessary to consider whether overfitting has occurred. If the loss continues to increase during the training process, there might be an issue with the training logic, and we should check the code.
* Regarding training frameworks, both [DeepSpeed](<https://github.com/microsoft/DeepSpeed>) and [Megatron](<https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM>) are suitable options. I personally use DeepSpeed.

## Conclusion

Compared to pre-training, which requires substantial computational resources, and reinforcement learning, which is more difficult to debug, instruction tuning typically yields more immediate results in practical applications.
