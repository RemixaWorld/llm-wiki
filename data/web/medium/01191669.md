## What’s an Embedding

An embedding is just a fancy way of saying

>  _Representation as a Vector_

Yeah, that’s it.

Think of it like this: _you got something_ — could be a word, a picture, a sound, whatever — _and you want to turn it into a bunch of numbers that a computer can understand_. That’s an embedding. It’s like a secret code that helps machines make sense of the world.

Now, what you’re doing is encoding a sentence into a vector. This is an upstream task required in many NLP tasks and that has a lot of importance in our RAG pipeline for example.

> _Good embeddings — meaning good representations of your sentences — mean good matches for what you’re looking for._

**Why we need to represent sentences as vectors?**

> _The goal is to represent a variable length sentence into a fixed length vector, e.g. hello world to [0.1, 0.3, 0.9]._
>
> Each element of the vector should “encode” some semantics of the original sentence.

And these two things are important. Now I’ve got a vector. And if I have two vectors — that represent different sentences — I can do things like calculate:

* **Dot Product** : This gives a scalar value that represents the amount of “similarity” between the two vectors.
* **Cosine Distance** : This tells us the angle between two vectors.

## Why choosing the correct model matters

Which one should I use? And why this matters. Before talking about this, let’s give a bit of context first.

Probably this is pretty obvious; the best embedding model should be, generally speaking, a transformer-based model. I know, I told you it was obvious, but is it?

First of all, not every embedding model is transformer-based. This should be obvious too, but algorithms existed before like **Word2Vec** , **GloVe** and **GTE-Base**. Algorithms that were already representing this _bag-of-words_ in vectors and were used in search engines or similarity search, etc …

But then, transformers came onto the scene. Let’s clarify, Embeddings are a layer in the Transformer architecture. Something that the model uses. This layer is trained, same as the rest of the layers.

**And what does that mean?**

> _📢 The representations — vector embeddings — that the model creates improve with training_

Again, this might seem obvious, but it makes sense that I’m making a strong point on this.

The thing is that the model with attention mechanisms became popular and BERT was one of the first models that came to life. So let’s focus on this model for a moment. When BERT came out, its embedding layer was used to encode sentences into vectors. Now, since BERT was trained on a big amount of data it was supposed to perform at least as well as it was doing in other NLP tasks.

Turned out that it wasn’t. The performance of that embedding layer was good but not as good as it was supposed to be.

**But, why?**

Because BERT is a foundation model. It is trained to be a generalist. BERT was trained with the masking technique. It was great, Google gave us a model that performed well after fine-tuning on tasks like classification.

Anyway, the thing is that Embeddings started performing better once you fine-tune the model on a specific task. Of course, your representations are going to be better if your model is trained on more data, but it also has to be trained on a specific task.

This is why, even though **GPT** from OpenAI is one of the best models there’s around, its embedding representations aren’t the best — and this is something that you can look for yourself by checking [MTEB](<https://huggingface.co/spaces/mteb/leaderboard>). This happens because GPT isn’t in _similiarity search_ or in _re-ranking_ or _clustering_ tasks.

So, how about taking a look at those tasks?

## Embedding Tasks (based on MTEB)

The moment has come. All this **Blah, Blah** was worth it. Now we know:

* Generally speaking, transformer-based embeddings will perform the best.
* They perform better if they have been fine-tuned on a specific task.
* Just because the instruction model performs great on dialogue or instruction doesn’t mean it will have the best embedding representations.
* There are several embedding tasks for which different models perform the best.
* There’s no such thing as a go-to model that performs the best on every task.
* MTEB gives us a good benchmark for choosing the right model based on our problem.

![MTEB Embedding Task representation from original MTEB paper](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*yHJ4dmIMtA-aQZsxkA5hnA.png)

So, let’s take a look to this MTEB tasks.

### 1\. Bitext Mining

* **Definition** : Given two sets of sentences in different languages, find the best match for each sentence in the first set from the second set.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : F1, Accuracy, Precision, Recall
* **Example** : Find the English translation of a French sentence from a list of English sentences.

### 2\. Classification

* **Definition** : Train a logistic regression classifier on a train set and evaluate its performance on a test set.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : Accuracy, Average Precision, F1
* **Example** : Classify a piece of text as either positive or negative sentiment.

### 3\. Clustering

* **Definition** : Group a set of sentences or paragraphs into meaningful clusters.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : V-measure
* **Example** : Group a set of news articles into clusters based on their topics.

### 4\. Pair Classification

* **Definition** : Assign a label to a pair of text inputs, typically denoting duplicate or paraphrase pairs.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : Accuracy, Average Precision, F1, Precision, Recall
* **Example** : Determine if two sentences are paraphrases of each other.

### 5\. Reranking

* **Definition** : Rank a list of reference texts according to their relevance to a query.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : Mean MRR@k, MAP
* **Example** : Re-rank a list of search results based on their relevance to a search query.

### 6\. Retrieval

* **Definition** : Find relevant documents from a corpus for a given query.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : nDCG@k, MRR@k, MAP@k, Precision@k, Recall@k
* **Example** : Retrieve a list of relevant documents from a database for a search query.

### 7\. Semantic Textual Similarity (STS)

* **Definition** : Determine the similarity between two sentences.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : Pearson and Spearman correlations
* **Example** : Determine the similarity between two sentences, such as “I love playing football” and “I enjoy playing soccer”.

### 8\. Summarization

* **Definition** : Score machine-generated summaries based on their similarity to human-written summaries.
* **Evaluation Metrics** : Pearson and Spearman correlations
* **Example** : Evaluate the quality of a machine-generated summary of a news article based on its similarity to a human-written summary.

> _🚨Disclaimer: Again, you should always look for the model that fits the best your problem. This means that you really have to take a look to the model size and the compute you are going to need_

**So these are the TASKS you should look when choosing your Model and building your PIPELINE**

## Talking is Cheap, Show me the Code

Alright, you probably already know this, but if you’re new to this, you might be wondering how you can use these layers in a fast and simple way.

Thanks goodness the guys at _Hugging Face_ have our backs well covered and give us not only the incredibly reliable _transformers_ library but also _sentence_transformers_. And since _Hugging Face_ are the ones maintaining the library, it’s obvious that the models we’re using will be the ones on the **Hub**.

> _😉 And yes, MTEB is also on the Hugging Face Cloud, so the models listed on MTEB are accessible through the Hub._

In order to call a model from the Hub, the code will generally be the same. Of course, I will recommend that you take a closer look [here](<https://sbert.net/docs/sentence_transformer/pretrained_models.html>) for more information.

```python from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformermodel = SentenceTransformer("multi-qa-mpnet-base-cos-v1")query_embedding = model.encode("How big is London")passage_embeddings = model.encode([ "London is known for its finacial district", "London has 9,787,426 inhabitants at the 2011 census", "The United Kingdom is the fourth largest exporter of goods in the world",])similarity = model.similarity(query_embedding, passage_embeddings) ```

But there are also these _SUPER-DUPER_ cool models that you can give a prompt to.

These are **INSTRUCTOR Models** and not all of the models available on the Hub support this feature. But hey, it might be pretty useful for tasks like retrieval. Take a look here:

```python from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformerfrom sentence_transformers.util import cos_simmodel = SentenceTransformer("hkunlp/instructor-large")query = "where is the food stored in a yam plant"query_instruction = ( "Represent the Wikipedia question for retrieving supporting documents: ")corpus = [ 'Yams are perennial herbaceous vines native to Africa, Asia, and the Americas and cultivated for the consumption of their starchy tubers in many temperate and tropical regions. The tubers themselves, also called "yams", come in a variety of forms owing to numerous cultivars and related species.', "The disparate impact theory is especially controversial under the Fair Housing Act because the Act regulates many activities relating to housing, insurance, and mortgage loansâ€”and some scholars have argued that the theory's use under the Fair Housing Act, combined with extensions of the Community Reinvestment Act, contributed to rise of sub-prime lending and the crash of the U.S. housing market and ensuing global economic recession", "Disparate impact in United States labor law refers to practices in employment, housing, and other areas that adversely affect one group of people of a protected characteristic more than another, even though rules applied by employers or landlords are formally neutral.
