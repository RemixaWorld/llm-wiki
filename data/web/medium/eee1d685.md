## The State Problem

All sequence models, be that a Transformer, like ChatGPT, a traditional RNN, Mamba, or today’s topic, Test Time Training layers (or TTT), have one thing in common:

They all carry a hidden state, but do so differently.

### The importance of state

What I mean by the hidden state is that, as all these models are sequence-to-sequence models (you input a sequence, and they give you a sequence back), the next word (for simplicity, let’s assume they are modeling text) is a function of the last input and this hidden state, with the latter carrying the history of previous parts of the sequence.

_And what makes the Transformer, the architecture everyone uses, different?_ Simple. Most of the aforementioned architectures have a compressed state.

In layman’s terms, they don’t store the information of every single previous word in the sequence, but they have a ‘gestalt.’

Just like you and I don’t have an exact memory of all past experiences, as our mind has a limited allocation capacity, we only store key things in our life… and forget the rest. However, our memory is highly efficient, storing only the essential stuff.

These architectures work in a similar way, as their state (their memory) is fixed in size. Thus, **they have to define mechanisms to decide whether the new word they see is worth remembering**.

But as I said, Transformers like ChatGPT don’t work that way. When processing a sequence of text, they store the information of every single past word in the sequence.

Every single one.

This is like a cheat code; they can always look back to pick up on any detail they need to predict the next word.

This is the equivalent to humans storing every single event, fact, and experience in their lives in their memory, allowing them to remember everything.

But as you may imagine, lunch is never free.

### A Crude Trade-off

This infinite attention, this capacity to remember everything and pick up or retrieve any key fact in a sequence they need to predict the next word, means that the state, by not being compressed, grows proportionally to the sequence length.

This is obvious, as the more words the sequence has, the more information the model needs to store. Of course, this increases the memory requirements, which reduces the efficiency of running these models.

Specifically,**the memory cost is quadratic** , meaning that doubling a sequence's size quadruples the memory, and a threefold increase has a ninefold impact.

> This is because the model has to store each previous word's Key and Value activations. [This article](<https://thewhitebox.ai/the-attention-mechanism/>) provides a more detailed explanation of the attention mechanism Transformers use.

This inability to compress state becomes particularly problematic when running inferences on long sequences. As explained by [Hooper et al](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.18079>), this cache that stores the state of previous words, [known as the KV Cache](<https://thewhitebox.ai/kv-cache-chatgpts-memory/>), becomes the limiting factor in terms of memory.

This makes running these models for long sequences an absolute nightmare (not only in terms of costs but also the engineering complexity of the cluster).

Thus, for quite some time, researchers have tried to mitigate this issue while still being able to compete with a model that, memory constraints aside, is almost like a cheat code for language modeling.

And the newest of these proposals is **Test-Time Training layers** , which also come with a pleasant and fascinating surprise.

## A Model Inside a Model

Unlike all previous architectures, including the Transformer, TTT layers significantly change the overall process.

With TTT, The state, instead of being a cache backlog that the model can query to know what was previously represented in the sequence, **is now another model**.

And that has a huge impact.

### An Expressive Memory

The idea is not that hard. Whenever we talk about RNNs, two things occur during inference:

* **The update rule:** For every new input to the model, it decides how much this new input will change the hidden state (the memory). In layman’s terms, **it’s a process where the model decides whether a new input is worth remembering** , as the memory has a fixed size.
* **The output rule:** Based on the recently updated — or not — memory and the most recent previous input, the model predicts the next word.

As you may imagine, the quality of the input is largely influenced by the expressiveness of the state. In other words, **it’s all about the quality of the memory**.

Sadly, this is a trade-off because this memory is fixed in size.

While this is great from an efficiency perspective, as the memory complexity is independent of sequence length (the next prediction always depends on a fixed-sized memory and the latest input, so the length of the sequence doesn’t impact its cost), **for very large sequences there’s a non-zero chance that the model has forgotten critical information required for this next prediction.**

> For instance, if the model is predicting the outcome of a novel, the name of a key character might have been mentioned in the past, but at the time, the model considered it irrelevant and forgot it. If that name is not mentioned again, there’s no way the model can recover that information once it has been eliminated from the hidden state (from the memory).

For this reason, RNNs and every other model that compresses state have paled in comparison with the Transformer when it comes to working with very long sequences, as the Transformer has this hack that allows it to retrieve every single detail from the past.

> Formally, we say that Transformers have a global attention inductive bias. By the structure of the architecture, it assumes that any past bit of information might be important in the future.
>
> On the other hand, we say RNNs have a recency inductive bias, in which the next prediction is largely dependent on the most recent input (the previous) and its current state (its memory at the time).

So, _what do researchers propose this time?_ Well, making memory a model, too.

### A Cycle of Improvement

In summary, researchers have turned the model's memory into a smaller model, mainly a linear network (although any potential neural network could be used).

_But what does this entail?_

Well, it significantly changes how we update the model's memory. As you can see above, to obtain the new memory state (Wt), we need to calculate the gradient of the function of the previous state (Wt-1) and the current input (xt).

It’s a mouthful, but it’s not that hard. In a traditional RNN, we also use the previous state (the previous memory value) and the current input to get the model's new state.

> For instance, let’s say we have a sequence, and the next word is ‘um,’ which is a pause the speaker is doing to think while he/she speaks. As you may imagine, this word doesn’t provide much value, so the RNN downplays its contribution. Simply put, the memory is largely untouched as this new word is very forgettable.

However, this update was done using fixed weights learned during the training phase, which then determine the relevance of the previous memory and the new input.

In this situation, the memory is not adaptable to the current situation, it’s expressiveness will completely depend on how well the training was done, leaving no room for error.

Adding insult to injury, _what if new critical pieces of information appear irrelevant during training but are relevant now?_

For example, if the model is specialized in dogs, _what happens if a new breed appears?_ In that scenario, the model will reject storing information about this new breed as it does not align with the knowledge gathered during training.

Therefore, we need a model capable of adapting its mechanism of memory update, which can be done by making the memory a function.

And as you’ll see in a second, the procedure is genius.

### Learn What You Don’t Know

For every new word the model sees, instead of performing a naive update of the memory, the model first performs an input reconstruction.

In other words, it measures how well the memory function can reconstruct the input word, where _f(xt;W)_ represents the memory function and _xt_ the new input.

_But why do that?_ Simple, **we are measuring how well our memory function knows the new input.**

* If the model cannot reconstruct the input word back, the error above will be large, meaning that the model is unfamiliar with this new word. This will imply a large gradient and a large update to the memory weights, **as this new input is worth remembering or learnable**.
* If the model reconstructs the input word back perfectly, it already knows it well, and thus, the gradient update over the memory function will be very small. In layperson’s terms, there’s no new information here to update the memory.

> For the sake of length, I have not gone into the specific details of how this is done. Essentially, they utilize three projections (just like self-attention), to corrupt the input, measure how much of the input is worth learning, and how much of the input should be pushed into the output, a very similar approach to how self-attention treats data.

## Our First Active Learner?

There’s a non-zero chance that AI, at least in the form of LLMs, might never be able to perform creative processes beyond their data.

In other words, while it seems clear that techniques like [grokking for implicit reasoning](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/selfcorrecting-humanoid-grokking-tool-works>), and active search for System 2 thinking, probably two of the hottest avenues of research for the upcoming generation, will undoubtedly improve how current models reason (or ‘think’), **there’s absolutely no indication that any of these techniques might allow models to go ‘beyond their training data’.**

Simply put, it could be that these models' capacity to reason or discover new things about the world will be intrinsically limited by their knowledge, aka what they learned during training.

For that reason, it’s crucial that these models develop an active learning approach. In other words, to avoid being constrained by a fixed amount of knowledge, **they engage in a life-long learning experience in which they can absorb new knowledge continuously.**

> I find this crucial, and TTT layers are the first time I’ve seen an AI architecture actively developed in this direction, which could mean that RNNs could be brought from the dead to become a leading architecture again.

While never-ending fine-tuning sprees over Transformers might do the trick, as we’ve seen with some researchers trying to beat the ARC-AGI challenge, this idea that AIs have decoupled training and inference stages just feels wrong to me, to the point that I firmly believe that our current Transformer models are nowhere closer to AGI than previous AI iterations were, no matter how compelling their outputs can be.

_Will TTT layers change this once and for all?_

> Who knows, but one thing’s clear: TTTs feel like a huge step in the right direction.
