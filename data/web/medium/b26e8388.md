## Current Solutions (And Why They’re Not Perfect)

There are a few ways people try to solve this issue with RAG:

* **Prompting** : You can add instructions like “Always stick to the provided context” or “Only use the information given above.” However, this doesn’t always work. Additionally, prompting is often non-interpretable, and it may lead to unintended side effects.
* **Finetuning** : You could finetune the model to learn to follow context. But finetuning is expensive, time-consuming, and requires a lot of data.

The point is, we need something more targeted, more interpretable, and more efficient.

That’s where activation steering comes in.

## What is Activation Steering?

Before I explain activation steering, let’s quickly recap how transformer architectures work.

At each layer of a transformer, there’s something called the **residual stream**. This is basically the information flowing through the model.

(Image by author)

Here’s how that would be represented mathematically:

(Image by author)

The residual stream at layer _l_ , which we call **x_i^(l)** , contains the activations (the internal representations) that the model is using to process information.

Now here’s the key idea behind activation steering:

> We can extract these activations, manipulate them, and steer the model’s behaviour in specific directions.

Here’s a simple example I usually like to use.

### Making an LLM “Happier”

Let’s say I want to make my LLM generate more positive, cheerful outputs.

Here’s what I’d do:

First, I’d pass a “happy” prompt into the LLM, and I’d extract the residual stream activations at a particular layer for this prompt.

The vector representing the activation for the “happy” prompt comes from the residual stream.

So now we’re left with a vector representing that activation:

Then, I’d do the same thing with a “sad” prompt:

Now here’s the magic: I subtract the “sad” vector from the “happy” vector.

(Image by author)

This gives me a **steering vector** that represents the direction in “activation space” that corresponds to “happiness.”

A better way to do this is to take several positive samples and several negative samples, extract their vectors, and compute the mean difference between the two distributions.

(Image by author)

Now, whenever the LLM generates a token, I add this steering vector to the residual stream at that specific layer.

The result? The LLM’s outputs become noticeably “happier.”

## How we apply this to context-following

Okay, great.

Now I’ll show you how I apply the same idea to our problem: making LLMs stick to the context we provide.

The approach is surprisingly similar.

Instead of “happy” vs “sad” prompts, we use:

* **Positive samples** : Prompts that include system instructions, context, and a question → `<SYS> + <CTX> + <Q>`
* **Negative samples** : Prompts that only include the question → `<Q>`

Press enter or click to view image in full size

(Image by author)

We extract the residual stream activations for both, and compute:

> **context_following_vector = positive_activations — negative_activations**

Let me show you a concrete example.

**Positive prompt:**

``` <SYS>: You are a helpful assistant. Always use the provided context to answer questions.<CTX>: Donald Trump is the current president of the United States as of 2025.<Q>: Who is the president of the United States? ```

**Negative prompt:**

``` <Q>: Who is the president of the United States? ```

We pass both through the LLM, extract activations at a specific layer, and subtract to get our steering vector.

Then, for every output token the model generates, we add this steering vector to the residual stream.

The result? The model now sticks to the context we provided.

(Image by author)

## Does This Actually Work?

We tested this on a dataset across multiple models: Llama-3.1–8b, Llama-3.1–70b, and Mistral-7B.

The results were consistent: steering improved context-following performance significantly.

## Why Is This Better Than Other Methods?

I’d argue that activation steering has several advantages over traditional approaches

Firstly, **it’s more interpretable than prompting.** Prompt changes often have unintended consequences. On the other hand, I believe steering can be a more targeted approach with fewer unintended consequences.

While it’s still not “perfect” in terms of interpretability, it’s still methods like these that are moving in the right direction if we eventually want “perfect” interpretability and “targeted” fixes.

Secondly, **it’s far more efficient than finetuning.** Finetuning requires large datasets, gradient updates, and substantial compute. Steering only requires computing a vector once. No backpropagation or retraining needed.

The way I see it, steering is like giving the model a gentle nudge in the right direction, whereas finetuning is like retraining the model’s entire personality just to fix one specific thing.

## What’s Next

What’s exciting about this work is that activation steering opens up a whole new way of thinking about LLM control.

I’d argue that methods like activation steering (those that are highly targeted and operate at specific layers ) are fundamentally more **interpretable and therefore more** **efficient** than heavyweight post-training approaches like finetuning.

You get competitive performance without paying the usual costs: no large datasets, no gradient updates, and no repeated training runs.

Just a lightweight intervention that you can compute once and reuse.
