The field of [Large Language Models (LLMs)](<https://github.com/SalvatoreRa/tutorial/blob/main/artificial%20intelligence/FAQ.md#:~:text=Large%20Language%20Models>) has seen exponential growth in the past 2 years. It has gone from a research product to a product on the market. Today these models are used by millions of people, several companies are developing new models, and more and more applications will be based on [LLMs](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model>). LLMs in recent years are not only becoming more and more present but bigger and bigger. They have gone from hundreds of millions of parameters to billions of parameters. This means more and more data and facilities to train them.

On the one hand, it might seem that the cost of inference is very small in comparison with training. Pero’ considering models like stable diffusion or [ChatGPT](<https://openai.com/blog/chatgpt>) these are used by millions and millions of users with a significant increase in inference costs (both of power consumption, hardware needed, and thus also money).

## [Generative AI Fuels Climate ChangeWhat is the associated carbon dioxide of your favorite model?levelup.gitconnected.com](</generative-ai-fuels-climate-change-588f4be2a7c9?source=post_page-----948827aa926e--------------------------------------->)

> current LLM research has tended to increase model parameters consistently to boost performance. However, the resulting massive models containing billions or even trillions of parameters pose stringent hardware requirements, hindering their practical deployment and use. ([source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>))

There are several methods to be able to reduce the cost of inference of a model. Not all parameters are needed in the model, so after training one could thin out the weights that are no longer useful. In short, the most commonly used techniques to reduce the memory footprint and computational cost of an LLM are:

* [**Quantization**](<https://huggingface.co/docs/optimum/concept_guides/quantization>)**** transforms float32 weights into a lighter format such as integers. Fewer bits per weight means less computation and memory.
* [**Pruning**](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pruning_\(artificial_neural_network\)>)**** is the elimination of weights that are considered unimportant without affecting performance too much.
* [**Knowledge distillation**](<https://github.com/SalvatoreRa/tutorial/blob/main/artificial%20intelligence/FAQ.md#:~:text=What%20is%20knowledge%20distillation%3F>) leverages the knowledge of an LLM to train a much smaller and more specialized model.

In recent times these approaches have been applied to many models that are pretraining. These techniques are of interest to the community because they can also be applied before fine-tuning.

While these techniques are applied to models after training, there is research that focuses on changing the structure of the model to make it more efficient:

* **Compact architecture design** is a method to try to replace self-attention (the most expensive component of the model).
* **Dynamic networks** in which only certain substructures are active at a given time, such as a [Mixture of Experts](<https://huggingface.co/blog/moe>) (MoE)

## [Welcome Back 80s: Transformers Could Be Blown Away by ConvolutionThe Hyena model shows how convolution could be faster than self-attentionlevelup.gitconnected.com](</welcome-back-80s-transformers-could-be-blown-away-by-convolution-21ff15f6d1cc?source=post_page-----948827aa926e--------------------------------------->)

[DeepMind with Chinchilla](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556>) showed that practically the models today are underfitted, which should therefore be towed with many more tokens. Or alternatively, be smaller.

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15556.pdf>)

So to recap, we can say that there is a certain percentage of weights that could be eliminated, especially after the model is trained. According to DeepMind, we could directly eliminate several layers. So a question arises:

> If the models could be smaller and many weights could be eliminated, couldn’t we also directly eliminate layers?

[In a recent study](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887>), they showed that entire layers of an LLM can be removed without impacting accuracy. In other words, there are several layers that can be removed before performance suffers a drop. [In this study](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17887>), they suggest that as many as 50 percent of the layers of [LLaMA-2](<https://llama.meta.com/>) 70B can be removed before the model collapses. Of course, it is necessary to make some adjustments if you do such extensive pruning:

> In particular, we develop a method that uses the similarity between the representations at different layers to identify the optimal layers to prune for a given pruning fraction; then, after removing these layers we “heal” the pruning-induced mismatch with a small amount of fine tuning (using QLoRA). [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17887.pdf>)

Overview of our layer-pruning strategy and example results. (a) a flowchart describing the algorithm, (d) results of pruning Llama-2–70B with healing (dark blue) and without healing (light blue) as a function of the fraction of layers removed. image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17887.pdf>)

it is intriguing that this seems to be a common phenomenon for several model types (not only [LLaMA](<https://llama.meta.com/>)) but also [Qwen](<https://huggingface.co/Qwen>) and [Mistral](<https://mistral.ai/news/announcing-mistral-7b/>), which are among the most widely used open-source models. **Practically, up to almost half of the layers can be eliminated before the model reaches collapse (random guessing on the task).**

> Importantly, we see a characteristic flat region of robust performance followed by a sharp transition to random accuracy at a pruning fraction around 45%-55% for models in the Llama-2 family, 35% for Mistral 7B, 25% for Phi-2, and 20% for models from the Qwen family. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17887.pdf>)

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.17887.pdf>)

**This means that there is a threshold that is specific to each model family.** You can then remove layers until you pass this specific model threshold, until then the model retains enough knowledge to perform the task. The [fine-tuning](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuning_\(deep_learning\)>) performed by the authors helps the model maintain performance after pruning (although its impact is not huge on these tasks more on the generative ability of the model).

Another interesting finding is that larger models can undergo more robust pruning, which means they have many more redundant parameters and layers than smaller models.

The authors also note:

* The model’s memory footprint and inference time decrease linearly with the number of removed layers.
* All methods such as pruning, PEFT, and quantization can be combined together. So when a user has limited resources, he can combine various techniques to minimize the memory and computational cost of the model.
* It is better to prune deeper layers but not the last one.

In fact, it has already been shown that some layers can be eliminated in language models such as [BERT](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BERT_\(language_model\)>). There are layers that have a fundamental function and should not be touched.

> Dropping higher layers of the models maintains comparable performance to finetuning the full network, with linguistic information regressed to the lower layers. Conversely, pruning the lower layers (which hold the core linguistic information) leads to substantial degradation in performance. [source](<https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-acl.438.pdf>)

image source: [here](<https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-acl.438.pdf>)

Now these results are important because eliminating a number of layers allows for much more compact models that can then be exploited by a user even with a single commercially available GPU:

> For instance, when deleting the last 10 layers (25% of the total 40 layers) from the LLaMA 2–13B model, the results on the MMLU benchmark only drop from 55.0 to 52.2 (a 95% retention). Furthermore, by removing the last 22 layers (55% of the total 40 layers), resulting in a 5.6 billion parameter model, we can still obtain a score of 47.2 on MMLU without any fine-tuning, even outperforming the LLaMA 2–7B model. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>)

We can eliminate layers; in the previous method, these layers were eliminated with a rule that was more qualitative than quantitative. **But exactly what does it mean that a layer is redundant?**

A redundant layer is a layer that if removed does not impact the performance of the network, this is because its functionality is very similar to other layers in the network. **We now know that many of the deep layers are redundant.**

[Another study](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>) got to the bottom of the issue, creating a metric to be able to calculate the redundancy of a layer. To do this, they designed a metric called Block Influence (BI) that represents the importance of a layer. For the authors, a layer is more important the more it changes the representation of the input. The authors then rank the importance of the layers and eliminate the less important ones.

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>)

Intriguingly, this is not only a problem with the transformer but also with models that use another type of architecture. So other types of models also seem to suffer from high levels of redundancy and you can conduct pruning:

> We selected the RWKV-7B model proposed in, a formidable competitor to the Transformer architecture which indeed consists of identical layers to a certain extent. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>)

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03853.pdf>)

For the authors, therefore, layer redundancy is a generalized plague of LLMs. After all, both Transformer and [RWKV](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13048>) and Hyena and other architectures are still structures built from sequential layers. Also, as we said before efficiently training such large models requires more data probably than is used during training.

[Another study](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05406>) shows how any model can be pruned, including even 3B models always with efficient results. Bonsai’s approach focuses on identifying submodules (attention heads, rows in feedforward projection, etc.) that can be eliminated without harming the performance of model inference. The interest of users is obvious; the authors focused on finding a method compatible with the hardware found in users’ homes:

> Based on these strong results and its usability under real-world memory constraints, we view Bonsai as a significant contribution to unlocking the power of LLMs for a broader spectrum of practitioners facing diverse hardware constraints. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05406.pdf>)

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05406.pdf>)

Finally, [this article](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800>) focused on conducting pruning of mixture-of-experts LLMs (MoE LLMs). MoE LLMs are the trend of the moment, as they in themselves allow a reduction in on-the-fly (active) parameters by choosing only top-k experts for the inference of each token. This especially allows for greater speed in inference. This is not to say that these models are lightweight:

> For example, loading the Mixtral 8x7B model in bf16 format requires at least two A100–80G GPUs. Notably, in this MoE model, the eight experts constitute around 96% (45B out of 47B) of the total number of parameters. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14800.pdf>)

image source: [here](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=mWaYC6CZf5>)

Now not all experts are equally important, so this article proposes a mechanism for less significant experts:

> introduce hardware-friendly post-training methods for either permanently removing unimportant experts (expert pruning) or dynamically skipping experts during inference (dynamic expert skipping). Our proposed method significantly reduces memory usage for deploying MoE LLMs and enhances their inference speed. [source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14800.pdf>)

Press enter or click to view image in full size

image source: [here](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14800.pdf>)

Thus, eliminating experts makes it possible to reduce the number of GPUs and increase the inference of these models.

In conclusion, there are several methods and techniques that can be applied both to LLMs of any size and also to other architectures (RWKV). These techniques are used to eliminate entire layers, while other techniques eliminate experts within a MoE. Apart from the interesting technical result, this has real practical implications. **These models can be pruned until they become usable in inference with the hardware available to the practitioner.**

Until the release of ChatGPT, we saw a race to the parameter, where every company had virtual pride in creating bigger and bigger models. However, these models remained confined to research labs. ChatGPT served as a watershed in which LLMs became mainstream. On the one hand, the challenge shifted to other indicators: performance on benchmarks, context length, and so on.

On the other hand speed. These models began to consume more and more in inference, so the research community began to focus on how to reduce the weight of these models. Both because it means more carbon emissions and to increase speed in inference. Smaller models also will mean greater democratization of LLMs.
