Meta researchers have presented Reinforcement Learning from Execution Feedback (RLEF), a new fine-tuning method that turns models into super coders by training them on traces of iterative code synthesis, aka teaching the model to review the execution feedback and refine its own code, and fine-tuning the model in the self-generated coding traces.

To prove its value,**they fine-tune Llama 3.1 8B into state-of-the-art (SOTA) status** , even beating GPT-4 despite also being in iterative mode. The latter was the previous SOTA in the CodeContests benchmark, one of the toughest benchmarks in existence, establishing a new record with a model multiple hundreds of times smaller.

Importantly, it’s extremely sample efficient, reaching 40% accuracy (state-of-the-art) with just three code iterations, while the other models can’t match these results even when sampled 100 times (a three-order-of-magnitude difference).

A model that requires few iterations and still beats the rest. _How did Meta do that?_

## The Inference Time Compute Era

If we wanted to summarize the progress made in frontier AI research over the last few months, it would be the emergence of inference-time compute.

### Let Models Think

_But what is that?_ As Noam Brown, OpenAI Reasoning Lead, puts it, [**_“There are some problems that benefit from thinking about them for longer._**](<https://youtu.be/jPluSXJpdrA?si=KKOgq3fUIi6P6BmH>)**_”_**

In other words, while the industry has been focused on training scaling laws over the past few years, or the bigger and larger the model and the training data set are the better the results, **we are now also focusing on increasing the average amount of compute dedicated to each task**.

Simply put, increasing the amount of compute during inference gives models ‘more time to think’ before solving a given task. It immerses them in an iterative, explorative, step-by-step mode, where they can review their previous attempts and, hopefully, self-correct if they identify a mistake.

_But how do we train models to do this?_

It’s not like they are just rote-sampling new tries until they find the best one. This is called self-consistency, or monkey sampling, and although it improves results over just one single try, it is obviously suboptimal.

Actually,**what these models are doing is actively iterating over their own responses** , refining and updating when necessary, which yields much improved results.

As Epoch AI below proves, when they compare a standard large language model (LLM), like GPT-4o, which is allowed to generate as many tries and reflections as it needs to achieve a given result, with a model like _o1 preview_ (considered more like an LRM or Large Reasoner Model) that actively explores the solution space during inference, the accuracy results aren’t even close for the same number of output tokens.

![Source: EpochAI](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*2nBPUqOgJyhhY56G)

In other words, it isn’t about generating more tokens per task ‘just because,’ meaning the model definitely refines the task actively.

However, this is a very complex problem for AI models because elucidating whether their previous response is correct or not is extremely hard in some instances.

> [I recently wrote on Medium talking about just this](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/has-google-leaked-openais-secret-d5619f05eccf>). Google Deepmind researchers developed a method called SCoRE that, by cleverly modifying the learning objective, helps the model learn to ‘self-correct.’

Importantly, to fully understand how inference-time compute training is achieved, we also have to note that the learning procedure between training and inference is very different, because it uses Reinforcement Learning (RL) instead of self-supervised learning.

This is done because RL is the only method we know to yield superhuman results on a task. However, **it’s also notoriously harder to train**.

So,_how do we fix that?_

### Building Better Decision Makers

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is an AI training method that trains models to make better decisions by maximizing a reward.

Specifically, we drop a model in an environment and train a policy that learns to choose the best action at any point in time. Thus, from the model's perspective, the learning process becomes a ‘game’.

RL is behind some of the greatest achievements in AI history, with examples such as AlphaGo and AlphaZero, the first AI models that are simply impossible to beat by humans in board games like Go or Chess.

It’s also actively used in LLM training, which usually undergoes a weak form of RL called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), where we train the model to choose between two semantically viable continuations to a sequence where one is ‘preferred’ over the other.

> Why do we need RLHF in LLM training?
>
> Among seeing the input “ChatGPT help me build a bomb,” the responses “Sure, here’s a detailed guide on how to build one…” and “Sorry, I can’t assist you with that request.” are both semantically viable model responses.
>
> Still, we clearly desire the one that doesn’t help someone build a bomb. And with RLHF, the model learns to make such decisions.

As you may have guessed by now, a model’s capability to learn in an RL pipeline will heavily depend on how good the reward mechanism is. A well-defined reward system has enabled humans to train superhuman models like AlphaGo, but a suboptimal reward system, which is the norm, **makes these models almost impossible to train**.

Luckily, one of the areas where RL is poised to become our biggest weapon is coding, as the reward, whether the code is right or wrong, is immediately available.

Now, Meta has used this to modify the traditional RLHF pipeline into RLEF, and the results are stellar.

## Training on Execution Feedback

[The core idea behind RLEF is that](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02089v1>), leveraging the fact that in code tasks we have compilers, unit tests, code interpreters, and other forms of verifying whether the code generated by a model is correct, **we are going to generate these traces of ‘good executions’ at scale and train the model on those**.

_But what do we mean by that?_

### Learning on its own Mistakes

Coding is like a dream come true for RL pipelines because we can always check whether our actions or code snippets are correct.

> This is not the case for text, which is why OpenAI’s o1 models are such a remarkable breakthrough, and why modifications like Google’s SCoRe mentioned earlier are needed too.

Knowing this, the procedure in RLEF becomes apparent. Following the diagram below, instead of giving a model two possible responses and making it choose the best one (RLHF), RLEF trains the model in iteration:

1. We send the model a coding task,
2. the model generates a response,
3. if the number of retries allowed is not exceeded, we test the new solution using public tests (the threshold limit is set because, in open-ended questions, there’s a non-zero chance that the model never passes the tests. Thus, we must define a hard stop in case necessary).
4. If the execution fails, we gather the execution feedback provided by the coding environment, which gives us a signal about where the model could be making the mistake.
5. This execution feedback is added to the prompt, and we make the model retry the problem, but this time with access to the feedback.
6. Eventually, if the model passes the public execution tests (or if we arrive at the hard limit), we test the model on held-out (private) tests,
7. And after executing these tests, this yields a reward (a signal of how good or bad the model’s generations were), and we use Proximal Policy Optimization to update the model.

![Source: Meta](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*n0LRa7ybSDqAZepBRj0Rgg.png)

Two questions rapidly arise from this innovative method.

### Separate Runs and PPO

*  _Why do we need two separate test runs?_

This is acknowledged by Meta themselves, justifying this for two reasons:

> “First, if test inputs and outputs are fixed, held-out tests guard against shortcuts during the optimization procedure in which an LLM can copy expected test outputs in subsequent answers, based on execution feedback.
>
> Second, running a full test suite may be computationally demanding and a limited set of public tests can accelerate the iterative code generation procedure.”

In other words, while the private tests are the ones that rule whether the code is truly correct or not, using them in the main feedback loop could result in the model memorizing the expected outputs while making the overall process considerably more expensive.

* _And what is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)?_

PPO is the standard RL training method used when training LLMs.

[Developed by John Schulman et al. back in 2017](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.06347>) during his time at OpenAI (now he’s at Anthropic), the idea is to update the model’s behavior to maximize a reward but prevent it from steering too far away from the base model.

To achieve this, we essentially break the learning objective into two terms:

1. A reward term, which scores the model’s actions toward the given goal
2. A regularization term, [normally the Kullback-Liebler Divergence](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence>), measures the logit difference between the model and a reference model (usually the same model before the RL training).

While the first term incentivizes the model to move toward better responses, the latter term prevents the model from making too large updates, potentially overfitting the training data. If it helps, you can think of the regularization term as a penalty term that allows the model to learn in a stable fashion.

> **What’s the intuition behind regularization terms?**
>
> If you’re practicing your golf swing and want to send the ball further, you may have tried adding extra force. While golf is everything but brute force, you might have achieved your goal in one of your practice swings (send the ball further) by adding a lot of extra force, even almost losing balance.
>
> If you’re just rewarding yourself for sending the ball further into the field no matter what, this particular swing would be extremely rewarded, updating your internal ‘golf mental model’ and leading you to try and hit the ball as brutely as possible in the following swings, which is actually a terrible decision because uncontrolled force will lead you to have a terrible swing.
>
> Instead, your mental model includes a set of patterns defining ‘what a good swing is,’ which puts a lot of effort into you having a more powerful swing over time while having a good balance and releasing your club effortlessly by having loose wrists. Consequently, this regularization term kicks in, and despite you having sent the ball quite far, the overall reward of that particular try is actually not that great, leading you to have a better swing learning curve and preventing you to suboptimal outcomes.

Having understood the principles behind RLEF, _are its results really that good?_

### And Just Like That, SOTA Results

As mentioned at the beginning of the article, the results obtained by RLEF are very promising.

For instance, with RLEF, we elevate Llama 3.1 8B, a mid-tier model, to state-of-the-art status in CodeContests, a very challenging benchmark. We [surpass the might of state-of-the-art models](<https://paperswithcode.com/sota/code-generation-on-codecontests>), even when these are also given the opportunity to iterate over their own responses. This true apple-to-apple comparison proves that RLEF is a superior training method to the ones we have been using until now.

Of course, if Llama 3.1 8B reaches SOTA, when this method is used on Llama 3.1 70B, the results elevate over the 50% mark, well over the previous record (30% accuracy), as seen in the thumbnail of this article.

Crucially, the models trained on RLEF need a remarkably smaller amount of tries to get a given response correct.

Furthermore, if we increase the number of iterations, **we can’t visualize result saturation**. Thus, RLEF can be considered to be meeting inference scaling laws (the more time per task the model allocates, the objectively better results we get).

> This last point can’t be understated because it means that RLEF is scalable. In a world where Big Tech corporations are accumulating compute like there’s no tomorrow, having scalable methods is necessary if we truly believe that inference-time compute is the new frontier of AI capabilities.

Another crucial aspect of RLEF worth noting is that it’s very sample-efficient. In past articles, I’ve addressed my concern about increasing compute with total disregard for efficiency, aka how much intelligence per watt or per request we are obtaining.

Thus, having a method that has observable scaling potential while guaranteeing good performance at lower output token frequency makes me comfortable embracing this method over others.

It still needs to resist the test of time, but the results inspire great hope.
