## Protecting Data and Citing Sources

The need for RAG stems from the need for two things:

1. Being able to reference the data source
2. Protect private data from LLM providers

### Why Do We Need RAG?

LLMs take an input sequence and predict the distribution over the next token, followed by a stochastic sample from that distribution.

In layman’s terms, once they have processed the input sequence, they take their entire vocabulary of words they know, rank them according to their feasibility as a potential next word to the sequence, and then choose randomly from a subset of these words.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*AOekFkzYtb3o-N52)

Therefore, it’s important to acknowledge that they aren’t retrieving the next word from a database that you can instantly point toward; they have compressed the entire Internet into their weights, but how knowledge is elicited from them is a total mystery, and we are just beginning to comprehend how these models work internally.

Long story short, they aren’t retrieving their predictions from specific data sources; they are literally predicting every single word. So, just as you may be able to describe the story of World War II to your kids but you may not recall exactly where you obtained that information, LLMs suffer from the same ‘source mystery’ phenomenon… at all times.

To make matters worse, you may want to use your LLMs on data you vigorously protect. Data is a vital asset for companies these days, so any cybersecurity risk to that data is a deal-breaker and, as for [Retool’s latest Generative AI report](<https://retool.com/blog/state-of-ai-h1-2024>), **one of the main blockers to implementation in enterprises.**

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*-qwT8NNbiApPQMNF6U_uNA.png)

Therefore, if you’re working with proprietary models like OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude, the reasons not to fine-tune these models with your data are pretty convincing. In that scenario, you are left at a crossroads.

You want to use Generative AI models to chat with your data, but you can’t feed that data to the models because of security concerns. And even in the data you’re willing to share, standalone LLMs can’t cite sources, which, added to the usual great suspect in GenAI, hallucinations, makes the experience pretty much pointless.

And here is where RAG comes in.

### Quick RAG Basics

Retrieval Augmented Generation is a process by which you connect your LLM to a knowledge base, usually a vector database, but other sources like knowledge graphs are valid, too.

1. Upon receiving a request from a user, it is first sent to the knowledge base that stores your data to be used as a semantic extraction query. In simple terms, to extract chunks of your data that semantically relate to the query (i.e., if the user asks about a chocolate fudge recipe, the retrieved context chunks should related to chocolate fudge recipes).
2. Then, these context chunks should ideally be sent to a reranker to be reordered, but it isn’t a requirement (more on that later), and then inserted into the LLM’s prompt alongside the user prompt.
3. Thus, the LLM sees the user’s query and the retrieved context, which it can now use to answer and, importantly, cite sources.

![Source: Author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*LZvppU5I-jf2VaMraDWD0Q.png)

However, this method has a big problem: **context destruction**. As you may have realized, I talked about chunks of context. The model doesn’t retrieve the entire knowledge base for every query, but just the key pieces of text that matter.

This leads us to one of the most important yet brittle parts of the entire process: _how do you chunk your data?_ The big concern here is that the critical context required might be divided into two chunks… but only one gets retrieved. In that scenario, the LLM is missing context, which will most probably result in sub-par performance.

So,_what can we do to prevent this?_

## Context Retrieval, Making Sure Context is Good Context

[Anthropic’s research](<https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval>) aims to tackle this precise problem: ensuring full context every time. But before we clarify the method, we need to add a previous step that is becoming essential: BM25.

### BM25 + Semantic Retrieval = Global and Word Context

In basic RAG, chunks of context are retrieved using a, you guessed it, retriever model. These tiny models do one thing only: they take the user’s query in its embedding form (vector form) and measure its distance to other vectors in the vector database to retrieve the ones with the shortest distance.

_But what does that even mean?_ LLM’s main principle is similarity. To them, two pieces of context are semantically related if their embeddings vectors are close.

![Source: Google](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*sr-HkS-GP_chLqIXLBwEsg.png)

However, as these chunks of context may have hundreds of words,**we are comparing the user’s request to the global meaning of that chunk**. In other words, the chunk may be semantically related, as a whole, to the user’s request, but we don’t have enough granularity to know whether key pieces of context are actually in the text chunk.

Hence, while this ensures some semantic relationship, the chunk may not be the one the user wants.

``` User request = "What is the capital of Spain."Retrieved chunk = "Barcelona is a city in the northern-eastern part of Spain,famous for its food, its architectures..." ```

_See the issue with the retrieved chunk above?_ While the chunk is undeniably related to the user’s query on a global semantic basis (they both talk about a city in Spain), **it does not answer the question** , as Madrid is the capital of Spain.

For that reason, it’s highly recommended that this global retrieval be combined with word-level retrieval using BM25, an evolution of TF-IDF.

Without going into much detail (for that, [check this YouTube video](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ruBm9WywevM>)), **the idea is that we can also measure word importance**. TF-IDF methods measure two things:

1. **Word frequency** , how many times a given word appears in a sequence
2. **Word uniqueness** , how much that word comes out in all chunks (usually to prevent very common words like ‘a’ or ‘the’ having a high score

This gives a score for each word, which is used to make the match with the query.

> BM25 adds a twist, which is to consider the length of the chunk.
>
> While a word may appear two times in a sequence of 100 and just once in a sequence of just ten words, while the former appears more times, the latter has a ‘bigger proportional value’ as it appears once but in a sequence with few words, signaling that its importance might be greater in the latter case.

Therefore, BM25 allows you to identify chunks of data that share key critical words with the user query, which in the previous case would have guaranteed at least some chunk containing the word _‘capital.’_

_But if we have two retriever methods, what do we do then?_

We apply **rank fusion** , [which this Medium post explains pretty nicely](</@devalshah1619/mathematical-intuition-behind-reciprocal-rank-fusion-rrf-explained-in-2-mins-002df0cc5e2a>), that evaluates the score a document has had in every retrieved method and fuses the scores to get a final list of context chunks considering both semantic similarity and BM25, as shown below:

![Source: Anthropic](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Oje9bgERbeHG6Xfgk2jh0Q.png)

Consequently, through this RAG pipeline, we not only get relevant chunks on a global basis, but we also guarantee that chunks that share key words with the user query.

This alone is already newsworthy because most people use only the vector database part, so BM25 is already a performance booster.

But we are still not solving the big problem: _how do we ensure proper context?_ And here is where Anthropic’s research comes in.

### Contextual RAG

Finally, we get to the center point of this article (luckily, we’ve explained most of the theory already, so it’ll be quick).

![Source: Anthropic](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*-TmfzJoA5zFkFM1367sCMQ.png)

To provide an extra level of insight to it chunk, Anthropic proposes using a cheap LLM (like their Claude 3 Haiku) as a chunk enhancement. To do so, they feed the chunks alongside the original complete document, and ask Haiku to ‘situate the chunk within the document.’

![Prompt sent to Claude 3 Haiku. Source: Anthropic](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*XRGz3i3rDluShJyRFFYpKw.png)

This leads us to a much more contextualized chunk that will include context from other parts of the document by contextualizing that chunk about the complete document:

![Source: Anthropic](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*YyCezmR2gdIstdl7RdJkRg.png)

If we look at the example above, the first chunk might be including the key information the user might be looking for, but it missing key context. Thus, if the user refers to the company as ‘ACME Corp’ instead of ‘company,’ there's a chance that the original chunk gets overlooked.

However, with contextualized retrieval, the new chunk continues to include the key data and the key context that may be required to apply semantic similarity to the user’s query.

Having more context-rich chunks dramatically improves performance, and the costs of actively adapting each chunk turn out to be very economically accessible, **at just $1.02 per million tokens**(750,000 words, which is a lot of chunks, probably way more than what you need for your use case).

> Crucially, contextual retrieval reduces error rates drastically by 49%, which grows to 67% if you add reranking to the mix.

> **But what is reranking?**
>
> Retriever models (the models that extract the chunks) are small and fast. However, they only apply simple extraction heuristics, like Euclidean distance or cosine similarity between vectors. Sadly, this extraction may be suboptimal, as we are trading-off speed and large extractions over accuracy.
>
> Rerankers, on the other hand, are larger and slower, making them unfeasible for large extractions. However, we can use them to rerank a limited group of chunks extracted by the retriever.
>
> **The critical difference is that rerankers perform cross-attention between the user query and each chunk** , allowing it to identify key relationships between both pieces of text that a mere similarity method might have missed.
>
> [I wrote a more detailed review of reranking in my newsletter’s knowledge base](<https://www.notion.so/Reranking-Implementing-GenAI-with-One-Line-of-Code-a62dce9ab83c441d9f8d058bbb17c19e>).

## Being Smart on Deployments or Suffer the Consequences

Having smart RAG implementations isn’t only about reducing errors; **it could also be a matter of survival.**

With the new suite of ‘long-inference models’ like OpenAI’s o1 family, these models not only considerably increase the output token number, as the model generates reasoning tokens besides output tokens to self-reflect and perform an active search, but they are also much more sensitive to the context provided.

> Thus, with o1-type models, you must ensure that your RAG pipelines feed quality content to the model. Otherwise, you are overpaying for a model that will yield worse performance than usual, making Contextual Retrieval mandatory.
