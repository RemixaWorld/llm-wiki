Large Language Models (LLMs) are a big part of today’s AI systems. They help power tools like chatbots, virtual assistants, and text generators. But, like any complex technology, LLMs have their weaknesses. These models can be vulnerable to attacks, such as harmful inputs during their use or tampering with data during training.

Protecting LLMs from these kinds of risks requires thoughtful strategies. It’s important to put safeguards in place during both the training phase and when the models are being used in real-world applications.

## How do we defend LLM models?

We can implement defenses during training and inference to enhance a model’s resilience. During training, techniques like supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) help adjust model parameters to recognize and reject attacks. Conversely, at inference time, where model weights remain fixed, behavior is controlled through prompts or guardrails that filter unsafe content.

Each approach has its pros and cons. Inference-time defenses offer flexibility. You can modify prompts or add modular components without retraining the entire model. However, they may increase latency and struggle with nuanced or reasoning-heavy attacks. On the other hand, training-time defenses enable models to detect complex attacks more effectively but come with trade-offs such as alignment tax — where adapting for safety reduces general performance by shifting data distributions or causing catastrophic forgetting.

## 1\. Training-Time Defenses

One key area to focus on is **training-time defenses**. These are methods used to protect AI models during their training process. They include steps taken before training starts and after the initial training is complete. Let me explain the main approaches in each phase.

## 1.1 Pre-Training Defenses

The first opportunity to build safety into a model comes during pre-training. This is when we prepare the data and set up the training process. Two key strategies are commonly used:

### Content Filtering

The first step is to remove harmful or toxic content from the training data. Research teams like [Team et al. (2024) in Gemini 1.5](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.05530>) [1], [Dubey et al. (2024)in Llama 3 ](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783>)[2], have shown how following strict safety rules can reduce risks in a model’s behavior. Cleaning up the data at this stage avoids many problems later on.

### Conditional Pre-Training

Another approach is to label potentially harmful data with special markers, as described by [Anil et al. (2023) in PaLM 2](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.10403>) [3]. This helps the model learn to create safer outputs by understanding which content is risky. Starting with these safeguards early makes the later steps of tuning and refining the model much easier.

## 1.2 Post-Training Defenses

Once the model has been pre-trained, additional steps can be taken to make it safer. These steps include supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and adversarial training.

### 1.2.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

This process involves showing the model examples of unsafe inputs and teaching it to reject them. For example, [Bianchi et al. (2023)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.07875>)[4] found that adding a small amount of “rejection-specific” data, just 3% of the training set and found a noticeable difference. The key is to collect various unsafe scenarios so the model can better handle different situations.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Safety instructions make models safer, but too much safety can make models exaggerate safety [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.07875>)]

### 1.2.2 Adversarial Training

Adversarial training is a more advanced approach. It involves testing the model with challenging scenarios. [Ge et al. (2023)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07689>)[5] developed a system where one model creates unsafe inputs, and another is trained to reject them. This back-and-forth process helps the model become more resilient. Reward models are often used to guide this process. One model focuses on helpful responses, while another flags unsafe ones for further improvement.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

According to the feedback from the evaluator, MART first identifies successful attacks from generated prompts, and then leverages them to train the adversarial LLM [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07689>)]

### Layered Safety Measures:

Relying on a single defense isn’t enough. Combining multiple methods creates stronger and safer models. Here are a few advanced techniques:

**Two-Step Safety Fine-Tuning:**[Wang et al. (2023h)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.15851>)[6] described a two-step method. First, the model learns to identify whether a question is harmful or harmless. Then, it evaluates the safety of its answers. Adding safety checks during the model’s use (inference) can also help block harmful outputs.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The pipeline of SELF-GUARD. The left side illustrates the two-step training process, while the right side depicts the inference flow.[[source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.15851>)]

**Adversarial Optimization:** Another approach is adversarial training with optimization techniques, like the one introduced by Madry[7]. It uses specific algorithms to test and strengthen the model against difficult inputs. This approach works well but requires a lot of computing power. For teams with fewer resources, simpler options like soft-prompt tuning (He & Vechev, 2023) or LoRA (Varshney et al., 2024) can be good alternatives. These methods focus on improving the model without needing to retrain it completely.

### 1.2.3 Preference Alignment by RLHF

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a cornerstone for aligning LLMs with human values. In RLHF, we train a reward model to capture human preferences based on ranked responses to identical prompts. This reward model tunes the target language model through iterative optimization [(Ouyang et al., 2022)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02155>).

Press enter or click to view image in full size

A diagram illustrating the three steps of PPO method: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) reward model (RM) training, and (3) reinforcement learning via proximal policy optimization (PPO) on this reward model [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.02155>)]

RLHF stands out because it improves out-of-distribution generalization for handling complex or unexpected scenarios ([Kirk et al., 2023b](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06452>)). However, constructing an effective preference dataset remains a major challenge. Researchers like [Ji et al. (2023)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04657>) address this by creating annotated datasets that evaluate responses on helpfulness and harmlessness.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

The annotation process has two stages: first, QA pairs are assessed for safety across 14 harm categories to assign a meta-label. Then, responses to a prompt are ranked based on their helpfulness and safety.[[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04657>)\- [Ji et al. (2023)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.04657>)]

Meanwhile, scalable solutions like those from [Shi et al. (2023b)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08685>) automate this process using reverse instruction models that generate tasks from predefined content. For example, turning a sonnet about love into the prompt “Write a sonnet about human love.” This approach extends to generating harmful inputs for safety datasets.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

SAFER-INSTRUCT follows a structured approach to improve instruction-based learning. It starts with reversed instruction tuning, training the model to generate instructions from responses. Then, it efficiently creates topic-specific instructions, filters out low-quality ones, and uses an expert model to generate high-quality responses.[[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08685>)]

While RLHF relies heavily on human feedback, Reinforcement Learning with AI Feedback (RLAIF) reduces this dependency by substituting human annotations with AI-generated feedback guided by predefined safety rules, called “Constitutions” ([Bai et al., 2022b](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08073>)). RLAIF lowers the effort needed to create annotated datasets while aligning models with diverse user needs and organizational policies.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Constitutional AI (CAI) trains models in two stages: supervised learning (SL) and reinforcement learning (RL). Guided by a set of core principles, SL refines the model’s initial behavior, ensuring a strong foundation before RL takes over. The RL phase then further enhances performance and reliability.[[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08073>)]

RLAIF has proven effective in systems like Claude ([Anthropic, 2024](<https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/de8ba9b01c9ab7cbabf5c33b80b7bbc618857627/Model_Card_Claude_3.pdf>)) and Qwen2 ([Yang et al., 2024a](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.10671>)). Instead of manually scoring every response for harmlessness or helpfulness, the process relies on AI-driven evaluations tied to specific alignment objectives, making it both scalable and adaptable.

After collecting preference data, updating model parameters becomes the next challenge. Two primary algorithms drive this process:

1. **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** OpenAI’s InstructGPT uses PPO to dynamically optimize outputs based on scores assigned by the reward model during training iterations. Although highly customizable for individual needs, PPO is computationally intensive due to its reliance on dynamic data generation and gradient-based updates. Variants like RRHF introduce ranking losses for fine-tuning preferences more effectively ([Yuan et al., 2023a](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=EdIGMCHk4l>)). GRPO enhances PPO’s memory efficiency without sacrificing performance during training cycles (Shao et al., 2024).

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Workflow of RRHF compared with PPO[[Source](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=EdIGMCHk4l>)]

**2\. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** DPO simplifies preference optimization by treating it as fine-tuning, directly adjusting parameters using preference data without additional intermediate steps like reward modeling or iterative scoring loops. This simplicity leads to stable training processes adopted widely in systems such as Zephyr, Llama3 , and Yi. However, DPO struggles with generalizability since its feedback isn’t tailored for specific models.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

DPO fine-tunes language models to align with human preferences — without relying on reinforcement learning. Traditional methods first train a reward model on human-labeled response pairs and then use RL to maximize this reward. DPO takes a simpler approach, directly optimizing for responses that best match human preferences using a classification-based objective, eliminating the need for a separate reward function or complex RL training.[[source](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/a85b405ed65c6477a4fe8302b5e06ce7-Paper-Conference.pdf>)]

Balancing competing objectives such as helpfulness versus harmlessness is critical in preference optimization but challenging due to their inherent conflict. Encouraging helpful answers while rejecting harmful ones creates instability in multi-objective optimization processes.

### 1.2.4 Representation Engineering:

Beyond end-to-end gradient-based methods like RLHF or RLAIF, some researchers focus directly on manipulating internal model representations for enhanced control and reliability:

* **Knowledge Editing:** Techniques such as those proposed by[ Mitchell et al. (2022)](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=0DcZxeWfOPt>) modify specific layers of transformer architectures to inject new knowledge or remove undesirable information.
* **Steering Methods:** These approaches analyze internal representations mechanistically instead of relying solely on external tuning workflows.

Recent advances demonstrate how representation engineering can serve as a defense mechanism against unsafe outputs:

* [Zou et al. (2024a)](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04313>) redirected potentially harmful outputs toward orthogonal representations or end-of-sequence tokens, forcing models to stop generating problematic responses altogether.

Inspired by representation engineering , circuit breaking operate directly on internal representations, linking harmful states to circuit breakers. This impedes traversal through a sequence of harmful states[[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04313>)]

By combining interpretability-focused approaches with traditional alignment methods, we gain deeper control over how LLMs behave across various scenarios without adding excessive complexity.

## 2\. Inference-Time Defense

Keeping AI models safe while they generate responses is important. There are several ways to reduce risks, such as **prompting techniques, guardrail systems, ensembling,** and **adversarial suffix filters**.

### 2.1 Prompting

Language models can follow instructions well, so **prompting** is a useful way to improve safety without retraining the model.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Image by Author

**Prompt Rewriting**

One way to make AI responses safer is by **adjusting the input prompt**. Researchers found that adding phrases about being responsible or avoiding harm makes models more likely to reject unsafe requests.

Another method, **Reducing Potential Offense (RPO),** looks for certain words or suffixes that can reduce harmful outputs instead of amplifying them. This helps keep responses more appropriate.

A different approach, called **Self-Reflection Prompting** , asks the model to first generate a response and then review it to check if it follows ethical and safety guidelines.

**Few-Shot Prompting**
To handle tricky adversarial attacks, a strategy called **In-Context Defense** includes examples of safe behavior in the input prompt. These examples teach the model how to recognize and respond to harmful queries. This can be combined with retrieval techniques, where the system finds safety-related examples that match the user’s query and adds them to the prompt. Together, these methods help the model create safer and more context-aware responses.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

An illustration of few-shot attack and defense methods [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.00629>)]

**Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:**
The **Chain-of-Thought** method improves how models reason through complex problems. It does this by guiding the model to break down a query into steps and think more carefully about the intent behind it. This method also helps the model apply safety rules when responding.

Prompting is a practical and cost-effective way to improve safety since it doesn’t require retraining the model. However, it’s not perfect. Using many examples or multi-step prompts can slow down response times. There’s also a risk that unsafe content in the examples could influence the model in the wrong way. To address these challenges, prompting is often combined with other methods, like filtering unsafe keywords.

### 2.2 Guardrail Systems

Guardrail systems are tools that help keep language models’ responses safe and appropriate. These systems act like filters, blocking unsafe content from being generated. They often include several parts, like prompt filters, toxicity detectors, and tools that help generate ethical responses.

One example is the NeMo Guardrails system. It uses large language models (LLMs) and databases to detect unsafe content and incorrect information in conversations. It also uses a special language called Colang to define safety rules. Other researchers have created similar tools, such as SPML, which allows developers to design secure prompts.

In addition to filtering content, guardrail systems may use fine-tuned models to double-check the safety of prompts and responses. These systems are an important part of making sure language models behave responsibly.

### 2.3 Language Model Ensembling

**Language Model Ensembling** combines outputs from multiple models to create safer and more accurate responses. The idea is simple. By blending predictions from several models, you reduce the risk of errors from any single model. However, while this approach can improve safety, it has some practical challenges.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Defending against adversarial attack with selecting response that is both “helpful” and “harmless” [[Source](<https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3605760.3623764>)]

For example, [_Chen et al. (2023a)_](<https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3605760.3623764>) introduced a method called **Moving Target Defense (MTD)**. This strategy combines responses from eight large-scale commercial models. It then evaluates the outputs to select those that are deemed “harmless” and “helpful.” While this method works conceptually, it comes with significant downsides. The process is slow, and it requires a lot of computational resources, making it hard to use in real-world systems.

Building on this, [_Chern et al. (2024)_](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.05998>) proposed a debate-based approach. In this method, multiple models “discuss” sensitive or risky topics. First, each model provides an initial response. Then, over several rounds, they refine their answers based on feedback from other models. This process improves safety, especially when smaller models work alongside larger ones. However, the downside is the cost. Running multiple models in a debate is computationally expensive and time-consuming, which makes it less practical for everyday use.

Combating Adversarial Attacks with Multi-Agent Debate [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.05998>)]

In short, while ensembling methods can improve safety, they are best suited for specialized tasks where higher costs and slower response times are acceptable.

### 2.4 Defense Against Adversarial Suffixes

Adversarial suffixes are extra bits of text added to prompts to trick a model into **giving harmful or inappropriate responses**. Zou et al. (2023b) found that some suffixes can make a model **approve dangerous requests**. Traditional keyword filters often fail to catch these attacks, so better methods are needed.

Screenshots of harmful content generation from the examples shown in Figure 1: ChatGPT (top left), Claude 2 (top right), Bard (bottom left), LLaMA-2 (bottom right) [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15043v2>)]

### Perplexity Filtering

One way to detect adversarial suffixes is by measuring their **perplexity**. This tells us how confusing or unnatural a sequence of words is. **High perplexity** often means the text doesn’t make logical sense, which can help flag adversarial suffixes.

However, this method has flaws. [_Alon & Kamfonas (2023)_](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14132>) found that using perplexity alone leads to **false positives** , where safe prompts get incorrectly marked as harmful. To improve accuracy, they trained a classifier using the **Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) algorithm**.

### Perturbation-Based Methods

Adversarial suffixes are very sensitive to small changes. This makes **perturbation-based defenses** a useful way to protect models.

**Erase-and-Check Algorithm** : [_Kumar et al. (2023)_](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.02705v2>) developed this method to check prompts **one word at a time**. It works by removing a single word from the prompt and then testing if the remaining text is still harmful. If **any version** of the modified prompt is flagged as unsafe, the entire input is considered harmful. This technique helps detect tricky adversarial prompts.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Erase-and-check helps defend against adversarial suffix attacks by gradually removing words from the end of a prompt and running the shortened versions through a safety filter. If any of these variations are flagged as harmful, the entire prompt is classified as unsafe. [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.02705v2>)]

**Randomized Smoothing (SmoothLLM)** : [_Robey et al. (2023)_](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03684>) introduced **SmoothLLM** , which makes models more resistant to attacks by adding **random noise** to the input. It slightly changes the text by inserting, swapping, or modifying words. These small changes can break adversarial suffixes and make them less effective.SmoothLLM has been tested against **Generated Content Guidance (GCG) attacks** and reduced their success rate to **less than 1%**.

Here’s how it works:

* A normal LLM **processes a manipulated prompt** without extra safeguards.
* SmoothLLM **adds a protection layer** by generating multiple altered versions of the input before the model responds. This disrupts attacks before they reach the LLM.

## Closing Thoughts:

There’s no single way to fully protect language models. Instead, the best approach is to **layer multiple security measures**.

Defenses used during **training** can catch complex attacks early. But they might also change how the model behaves, sometimes affecting performance. **Inference-time techniques** (the ones used while the model is running) are more flexible but often struggle with advanced threats.

From my experience, **Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) and AI Feedback (RLAIF)** offer a good middle ground. They improve security without adding too much extra cost.

If you’re looking for practical steps, try mixing **simple methods** like careful prompt design with **structured safeguards** like guardrails. **Ensembling** (using multiple models together) is effective, but it takes a lot of resources. Save it for situations where high reliability is a must.

No defense is perfect. But by using a **balanced mix of strategies** , you can make your model much safer while keeping it useful. **Security threats will keep evolving, and our defenses need to evolve too.**
