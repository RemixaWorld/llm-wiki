## Why Machine Learning (ML) Model Degrades Performance

Have you ever wondered why a perfectly trained machine learning model starts making questionable decisions? This is where data drift enters the picture — a silent culprit that causes deployed ML models to decline in performance.

Data drift occurs when your production data deviates from your training data’s characteristics. It’s like both Mike and you tried to navigate modern-day traffic using a map from the 1990s — the bare roads might be the same, but the traffic patterns have fundamentally changed. You’re destined to get lost.

The drifts can come from various sources: selection bias, seasonality, policy updates, or simply evolving user behaviors. Let’s face it: people change, and the uncertainty is the only certain thing. This was a wake-up call, and Mike’s team needed to stay ahead of the game to maintain model performance.

Their solution? Implementing a drift detector that could flag when production data diverged significantly from the training set. They could choose their battle plan when such divergence occurs:

* Completely retrain the model with fresh data
* Fall back to a simpler but more robust model
* Or adjust the weights to compensate for the drift

But this raised a crucial question.

## How can Mike define the divergence or drift of two distributions?

Mike initially thought to track standard performance metrics like accuracy or F1 score …, but this requires real-time access to ground truth labels — luxurious assets unavailable in production environments. Imagine we couldn’t wait for fraudulent activities to occur to get the positive labels in fraud detection.

Mike can also suggest more pragmatic strategies. For example, they could monitor the input distribution **_P(X)_** itself rather than trying to track any target-related distribution the target distribution: **_P(Y)_** or conditional distributions like **_P(X|Y)_** or **_P(Y|X)_** , which would always require those elusive target variables.

This decision led Mike to explore various distribution distance metrics. He compared the training data distribution **_P(X_train)_** with the test data distribution **_P(X_test)_** and observed the evolution of those distances in the dashboard. His team could create an early warning system for data drift by tracking these distances over time.

When Mike began his investigation, he discovered several interesting metrics, each offering a unique perspective on observing data drift. Each metric compares two probability distributions, which he’ll call **_P(x)_** and **_Q(x)_** , with their respective cumulative distribution functions **_Fp(x)_** and **_Fq(x)_**. Let’s tackle this learning journey and explore these metrics and their practical applications together.

### **1\. Kullback-Leibler Divergence (KLD)**

The [KL divergence](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence>), sometimes called relative entropy, measures how one probability distribution differs from another. Consider measuring the information lost when using one distribution to approximate another.

KLD has become fundamental in pattern recognition and information theory, with its unique properties:

* Asymmetric: Comparing P to Q gives us a different result than comparing Q to P (KLD(P||Q) ≠ KLD(Q||P)). This asymmetry is why, despite its common nickname, “KL distance,” it’s not technically a distance metric.
* Non-negative: Thanks to [Gibbs inequality](<https://statproofbook.github.io/P/kl-nonneg.html>), KLD is always zero or positive. When two distributions match perfectly, KLD equals zero and grows more significant as the distributions diverge.

### **2\. Wasserstein Distance**

[Wasserstein distance](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasserstein_metric>), or “Earth Mover’s Distance,” takes an optimization-based approach. We can imagine each distribution as a pile of earth, and this metric measures the minimum amount of “work” needed to reshape one pile into the other.

This is the minimum “work” needed to transform distribution **_P(x)_** into **_Q(x)_**. It’s like finding the most efficient transformation method, so the metric is considered the solution to an optimization problem.

### **3\. Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) Test**

The [KS test](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov%E2%80%93Smirnov_test>) takes a visual approach to distribution comparison. Picture two cumulative distribution (CDF) curves plotted on the same graph. The KS test finds the most significant vertical gap between these curves — the more prominent this gap, the more likely these distributions are different.

Example of KS test (Image by Author)

What makes the KS test special is:

* Non-parametric: no assumptions about the underlying distribution
* Sensitivity to both location and shape changes (These changes often create noticeable gaps between the cumulative distribution curves)
* Statistical significance testing built-in: Statistically speaking, the KS approach tests the null hypothesis that two samples are drawn from the same distribution (or one is drawn from a particular distribution), (i.e. **_H₀_ ​: _Fp(x)_** = **_Fq(x), ∀ x_)**

Those metrics can be implemented from `**scipy.stats**` library: `[**entropy**](<https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.entropy.html>)` for KLD, `[**wasserstein_distance**](<https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance.html>)`, and `[**ks_2samp**](<https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ks_2samp.html>)`**** for KS test.

As those numbers turned into visuals, the story became clear for this specific context:

* Wasserstein distance is sensitive to gradual changes, capturing location and shape differences. Still, it demands high computing power and is less scalable in higher dimensions (as it’s the solution to a complex optimization problem).
* KL-divergence is better for detecting major shifts and is more efficient. However, it was heavily penalized when one distribution was very small or zero compared to another.
* KS test shows that small distribution changes can shift the maximum difference point, reducing stability. This is clear with standard deviation changes, which alter the cumulative distribution function (CDF) shape. Also, the KS test focuses only on the maximum distance, which can overlook minor systematic differences.

Another message popped up as Mike sent his pull requests in the codebase. This time, it was from his manager: “I’m aware we have a problem with our model. What’s the plan?”

Mike smiled. For once, he had good news. “We’ve identified the issue,” he replied. “More importantly, we’re implementing a system to catch these problems early.”

“Roger. But what’s causing the difference?” — the manager pressed.

“Outdated model. Training data was before 2021, but user behavior changed dramatically during COVID,” Mike explained.

“Thanks. So, what’s the next step to prevent this from happening again?”

“The general idea is to treat data drift monitoring as the data product itself with priority and a clear roadmap. Model deployment isn’t the finish line; it’s the starting point. It’s also about maintaining its health and reliability.” — Mike took a deep breath and clicked “send.”

The question isn’t if the data will drift, but when — and whether we’ll be ready when it does.
