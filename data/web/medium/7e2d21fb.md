Did you build your first RAG app and feel a bit disappointed?

That’s okay. If it’s working, that’s a good start. Now it’s time to make it better.

A RAG can go wrong in several ways.

Some relate to response quality, while others relate to the system’s latency. However, we should fix performance in every corner before the deployment. It’s not easy, but doable.

The best way to think of the fixes is to think along a typical RAG pipeline.

RAGs can be iteratively improved. But most RAG apps we develop start with _chunking_ , then _embedding_ , _storage_ , _retrieval_ , and finally _augmentation_ or responding. To fix the overall system, we need to fix these individual components.

So, fixing them individually is my seven-step strategy to improve an overall RAG.

## Find the perfect chunking strategy.

Chunking is the crucial part of an entire RAG pipeline.

LLMs perform better when the prompt has little noise. You will get a high-quality response if everything in the prompt is relevant to the query. When it’s not, expect trash.

AI engineers sometimes misuse the long context window of modern LLMs.

You can get a full-length physics textbook within the 1M token context window of Gemini 1.5 Pro. To make things interesting, Gemini 2.0 Pro has 2X the size of 1.5. You’d think you could have an entire organization’s knowledge base inside a prompt.

That’s one way to kill your RAG app.

To answer the question “how electrons move in alternate current,” you don’t need an entire book. You only need sections of electronics. Everything else is noise.

Instead, here’s what you can do.

Split the book into sections. Take a subset of splits that contains the word electron. Embed them to the prompt along with the question.

This time, you’re more likely to get the correct answer and get it quicker.

This is chunking. The better the chunks, the better your app. The question is, “How do we say the chunks are better?”

This is a topic for a full-day workshop.

You can chunk the document in several ways. The basic yet often sufficient technique is recursive character split. Agentic chunking, on the other hand, takes a human-like approach to splitting the documents.

Here’s a quick summary of the popular chunking techniques.

### Recursive character split

This method splits the text into equal lengths.

But we don’t chunk the document into distinct splits. Instead, we allow overlaps. This ensures at least one chunk entirely captures a core idea.

But this method has a serious drawback.

It assumes that every idea has been explained in an equal amount of text. That’s not true, and it’s obvious. You’ll have a mix of good and bad chunks using this method.

The chunk size and the overlap window size are the key modification parameters.

Large values for the window and overlap would capture enough context in a single chunk. However, they may also include more noise. Balancing the values for these parameters is challenging yet essential.

## [Why Does Position-Based Chunking Lead to Poor Performance in RAGs?How to implement semantic chunking and gain better results.medium.com](<https://medium.com/data-science/semantic-chunking-for-rag-35b7675ffafd?source=post_page-----e8cde832bb0a--------------------------------------->)

Here’s my usual approach.

I start with large values: about 1000 tokens window and 200 token overlap. Then, I read a sample of chunks to see if they talk about one-and-only-one idea. If most chunks don’t, I’ll adjust the values.

### Semantic chunking.

Semantic chunking splits the documents where the meaning significantly changes.

First, we convert every sentence (or paragraph) to its vector form. Then, we compute the distance between that and the next. When the distance is beyond the threshold, we declare a breaking point.

Chunks from this method generally speak about a single idea and can be of different lengths.

However, semantic chunking is both expensive and repetitive. For example, if the text discusses one topic, temporarily shifts to another, and then returns to the original subject, this method would generate three chunks. However, it needs to be one (or two if the temporary shift is treated as separate.)

### Agentic chunking

Agentic chunking generally creates better chunks than the other two.

## [How to Achieve Near Human-Level Performance in Chunking for RAGsThe costly yet powerful splitting technique for superior RAG retrievalmedium.com](<https://medium.com/data-science/agentic-chunking-for-rags-091beccd94b1?source=post_page-----e8cde832bb0a--------------------------------------->)

This method mimics a human. Initial chunks are created with recursive character split. Then, an LLM would assign the chunks to a previous chunk if it is relevant to that or create a new chunk if it’s a novel idea.

This method is also the most complex one to implement and the slowest to perform.

Every newly created chunk will have a chunk summary. The LLM would check a new sentence and pick the chunk whose summary is similar. At every step, the chunk summary is updated to reflect the change.

That’s a lot of LLM calls.

### Chunk clustering

This is a method I stumbled upon, although I’m not certain if it has been documented before.

If information is scattered throughout a document, we must bring it together. I did this by clustering the vector embeddings of every paragraph. Each resulting cluster is a chunk created using similar paragraphs from the document that have been selected.

Clustering creates quality chunks much faster than agentic chunking.

## [How I Used Clustering to Improve Chunking and Build Better RAGsIt’s both fast and cost-effectivemedium.com](<https://medium.com/data-science/improving-rag-chunking-with-clustering-03c1cf41f1cd?source=post_page-----e8cde832bb0a--------------------------------------->)

Each chunking method I outlined has its strengths. In most cases, our approach must balance cost, quality, and latency. Therefore, work on a sample, test your methods, and select one.

## Use a fine-tuned embedding model.

This is something we easily overlook.

We generally believe that larger models make fewer mistakes, so a large embedding model would be more effective. You can either use the largest Open AI embedding model or pick the largest open-source model without considering the impact on cost.

You can’t be more wrong than that.

Larger models are built to work in a wide variety of contexts. They embedded documents on gardening niches as well as astronomy. It is a safe bet.

But, large or small, they don’t know the codewords of your inner circle.

Every organization has abbreviations, naming conventions, or fancy names for internal projects. Beyond their walls, these words may have no meaning. The embedding model you’ve trusted hadn’t seen it during training either.

This is why a smaller mode is always my preference.

Smaller models can still capture the language structures and meanings for general terms. They can be fine-tuned for your private vocabulary. This fine-tuned smaller model often outperforms the larger ones.

Besides, you save a lot for their use, and they are generally very fast.

Pick a small embedding model like [BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5](<https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5>). Prepare a dataset with about 1,000 examples. Fine-tune the model and use it in your project.

The downside, however, is dataset preparation.

Creating a domain-specific dataset requires effort from subject matter experts. But their time is often limited and rarely available for R&D. Certainly, this isn’t a task an intern can perform either.

Specific instructions on fine-tuning embedding models vary. For instance, [this link is an example of fine-tuning bge-small-en-v1.5](<https://github.com/FlagOpen/FlagEmbedding/tree/master/examples/finetune/embedder>).

## Consider hybrid data sources.

If you’ve developed your first RAG by following a tutorial, chances are you’ve used a vector store.

A vector store is optimized for faster vector search. Vector search is a fantastic technique for retrieving information based on semantic similarity. It can retrieve documents even when they are differently worded.

But it doesn’t mean other databases don’t fit RAGs well.

## [I Couldn’t Build Robust RAG With Vector Search; Here’s My Alternative.Why your RAGs need more than just semantic search to succeed.pub.towardsai.net](<https://pub.towardsai.net/hybrid-vector-keyword-bm25-for-rags-96e73fd4b1cd?source=post_page-----e8cde832bb0a--------------------------------------->)

Many other databases support vector search. For instance, the Postgres PgVector extension adds vector search capabilities and databases like Neo4J support vector search out of the box.

The best way is to think of all these databases as complementary solutions.

I prefer hybrid solutions. Vector search is sufficient for pure text. However, to pull data about a customer, you need to get it from a relational database. Alternatively, you can use a graph RAG to reason answers better than a vector search.

## Switch to an advanced retrieval technique.

What could we possibly do to improve retrieval?

After all, retrieval is fetching the relevant document. The vector store and other databases we’re using take care of this. It seems there isn’t much work on this front.

You could experiment with a different distance matric and see which works best.

But trust me, retrieval can be more sophisticated. Rather than a single retrieval, you can do it in stages. If you orchestrate them smartly, you will get much more, yet relevant contextual information.

One such technique I find promising is the chain-of-thought retrieval.

In this method, we first retrieve documents as we usually do. However, an LLM would decide if a follow-up retrieval can improve the context fetched. This iteration can go on a few times, resulting in a deeper and complete context to answer a user query.

## A smaller LLM could do just as well as a large one.

I’ve already discussed how effective smaller LLMs can be when discussing embedding models—this also applies to text generation.

Think about it. Large LLMs can handle world knowledge and multilingualism. But do you need them?

In most cases (especially for RAGs), you don’t.

The LLM answers the question based on the given context. If the context is insufficient, we ask the LLM to say so. The world knowledge we have with larger models is only a distraction to the LLM.

You may use a model with better reasoning abilities for prompt routing and recursive retrieval tasks.

However, such models are often overkill for the augmentation phase.

You may fine-tune the model if you prefer a specific output format every time. But other than that, you don’t have to. You don’t have to fine-tune it to fit your organizational knowledge.

## Use prompt caching

When moving from prototype to production, you must balance cost, quality, and latency.

You can store prompts and responses for future use, preventing the need to generate new tokens. As a result, your app can be faster and cheaper.

On the quality front, it’s a mixed result.

You can retrieve responses from the cache based on the prompt’s semantic similarity, but this may not be accurate. You need to experiment again with the threshold to find the sweet spot.

While this isn’t acceptable in some applications, you may choose the caching approach in most less-critical circumstances.

## Rerank (Optional)

The last technique I rely on is reranking the retrieved context before augmenting.

Studies show that the position of the correct document in the overall context matters for response quality. Your response could be accurate if the relevant context is found at the beginning. If the relevant document is found at the end, it is as if it doesn’t exist at all in the context.

We have specialized LLMs that rank retrieved contexts based on their relevancy to the original query.

I prefer using Cohere’s reranking model. If you prefer open-source models, [bge-rerank-base](<https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-reranker-base>) is a good choice. But there are a few [other models](<https://huggingface.co/models?search=rerank>) you can try out on Huggingface.

But reranking hasn’t been successful in many instances.

Reranking has only marginally improved overall performance. It is not worth the extra effort, running cost, and additional latency. However, a minor tweak can change this.

When we combine reranking with a dartboard technique, I see better results than direct embedding inside the prompt.
