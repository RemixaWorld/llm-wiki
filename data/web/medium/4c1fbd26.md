## Confusing Memorization with Reasoning

There are four ways in which an AI can reason.

### Four Options, A Harsh Reality

1. **Implicit reasoning:** When the model has built internal reasoning circuits that allow it to perform ‘innate reasoning.’ It’s similar to driving your car; you perform intelligent actions unconsciously; they are natural to you.
2. **Verbalised reasoning:** When we induce the model to explicitly verbalize the reasoning, like asking it to ‘solve the problem step-by-step.’ Making the model ‘explain its thoughts’ leads to better reasoning.

> An interesting fact for you. Anthropic’s Claude models are prompted by the system to generate ‘thoughts.’ These are parts of the response to the user included in <thought> clauses, which are hidden from the user but help it respond better.

**3\. Few-shot reasoning:** When we provide the reasoning chains as context. For instance, when we give examples of ‘how to reason’ as part of the prompt. By providing examples to imitate, that’s precisely what the model will copy.

**4\. Active search reasoning** occurs when we allow models to generate and verify multiple possible solutions until they settle on the best one.

Today, most of our efforts revolve around the second and third options. However, in both cases, the results are pretty mediocre and, as we will see in a minute, **outright inferior to the method we are seeing today**.

Another promising path is search-enhanced reasoning, a way of ‘giving models time to think.’

Long considered the holy grail of LLMs, [I provide much more detail in my newsletter](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/ai-truly-intelligent>), but the idea is that by allowing models to explore the space of possible solutions, they are imitating the same procedure we humans do when thinking about a problem, a term psychologists refer to as ‘System 2 thinking’ or ‘conscious thinking’ (conscious as in ‘deliberate’ not in the sense that they are conscious beings).

However, the sheer scale of compute and memory requirements that this paradigm requires makes it today a wet dream far from reality.

> Additionally, **these models have a verification problem**. While they offer extremely promising results in areas where each thought can be automatically verified (for instance, if I’m trying to solve a code problem that has tests that can be run to validate the generated code snippet), they are largely unexplored in open-ended problems.
>
> Some people (OpenAI/Google) have tested using other LLMs to ‘validate thoughts,’ [but as Google Deepmind itself proved](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21787>), this method plateaus pretty quickly.
>
> For example, using search to create the best possible summary of a text. Such a subjective validation is non-trivial to solve)

So, with options two and three being very limited, and the fourth being too complex or too expensive, this leaves us with the first option: **implicit reasoning**.

### Can AI implicitly reason?

Based on their responses, you may be tempted to think that they are already great reasoners. But that couldn’t be further from the truth.

While approaching System 2 through search undoubtedly needs to happen eventually, **we still have yet to train good ‘intrinsic reasoners,’** models that are intrinsically smart or, more specifically, models that can effectively reason over their _“known knowns.”_

> One way to differentiate System 1 & 2 of thinking is that System 1 is performing intelligent, reasoned actions over past experience and knowledge, while System 2 requires active search reasoning to find an answer that is not natural or automatic to you.

And I have plenty of proof that current models can’t reason over their knowledge. In fact, you can check it for yourself.

For instance, one common reasoning type they always fail is **composition reasoning** , when they are provided with two related facts and the model has to infer a third one.

For instance:

1. Barack Obama’s wife is Michelle.
2. Michelle was born in 1964.

Seeing this, if we ask the model to infer when Barack’s wife was born (1964), only a few get it right. But if we turn it up a notch, things get pretty bad.

Here’s a prompt almost all LLMs today fail on the first try:

_“Alice has 10 brothers and 10 sisters. How many sisters does any of the brothers have?”_ Again, this is a composition exercise. The model has two facts, and a third fact must be inferred based on those two.

Here’s the answer GPT-4o gave me:

You see what’s going on, _right?_ The model can’t reach the conclusion that Alice must be counted as part of the sister group.

Next, let’s try to lure it into the answer by asking it to take a step-by-step approach, aka the “verbalizing reasoning” method (the classical chain-of-thought method):

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*-Artmv6zldyHanTn)

Notice the pattern, _right?_ The model has not figured out that Alice is also one of the sisters, despite being obvious based on the stated facts I gave it.

While Claude 3.5 Sonnet got it correct (below), it made waves a few days ago when the model could not decide whether “9.11” was larger or smaller than “9.9”.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*qGW5FZxkzXIBO7beP8fWvA.png)

But when tested on more complicated compositions, all LLMs fail, without exception. Therefore, if they aren’t capable of such simple reasoning, we quickly realize that **LLMs are just parroting things without actually understanding them**.

Therefore, _have we been continuously gaslighted by Tech tycoons into thinking LLMs are much smarter than they are?_

Long story short, yes, but that doesn’t mean we can’t solve it through a technique called **grokking**.

## Grokking, Becoming One with Data

Just like any other AI, LLMs are trained using a standard procedure.

### A Decades-long Method

1. We gather as much data as we can and divide it into two categories: training and testing.
2. We train the model on the training set and then test it on ‘unseen’ testing data. If the model performs well on the test data, it has learned useful stuff from training that allows it to perform well in data ‘it has not seen before.’ This idea is called ‘generalization.’

> There’s a crucial point to note here: generalization has different levels.
>
> Generally, the testing data is almost always from the same distribution as the training data (i.e. the training data was dog photos, the testing data are new dogs).
>
> Out-of-distribution generalization, when the model learns priors that transfer to not only unseen, but wildly different data (illustrative example, training on dogs, testing on cats), is a largely unsolved problem in AI today, even for LLMs.

Traditionally, in this procedure, the key thing to avoid is ‘overfitting,’ a phenomenon that occurs when **we extend the training so much that the model memorizes the training data** , making it incapable of generalizing to the test set.

> By training for too long on data, that will lure the model into absurdly-specific conclusions. For instance, if the model has just seen husky photos and overfits, it will only accept other huskies as ‘dogs’.

Thus, historically, researchers have aimed to find the sweet spot between under/overfitting. Using a dog example again, we aim to find the moment when the model learns general dog attributes (four-legged, hairy, paws…) as characteristics of all dogs instead of assuming that all dogs look like huskies.

> This problem is called the ‘variance/bias trade-off.’

![Generated by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*4msEQpI_amRodM6A)

But there’s a problem. If we only mildly expose the models to the data to prevent overfitting, the models only seem to be learning the data’s ‘gestalt.’

And here’s where grokking comes in. It is one of the most counterintuitive ideas in AI today, **but it is potentially ground-breaking.**

### The Art of Grokking

Grokking breaks completely with the previous method by **extending training well beyond the overfitting point.**

At first, this might seem like a crime, but it hides a magical secret: with enough grokking, the model will learn to generalize to depths far exceeding those of a non-grokked model.

As we can see in the image below, when we overfit a model (the moment when the red curve reaches 100% accuracy), the accuracy on the test set is very low.

However, if we continue training on the overfitted data, suddenly, the model starts performing well on the test set, and, in the case of reasoning comparison problems (right), the model even learns to perform out-of-distribution comparisons (more on this in a second).

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*oUmEZCQfmBDykKnu)

**And this overextension of the training is what we call grokking**. At first, this might not make sense. _How does a model that has memorized the training data perform well on different data?_

The shortest answer is that **the model has become ‘one with the data.’** _But what does that mean?_

### Giving Models Time to Learn

Just like allowing LLMs to search for different ways of solving a problem before answering is akin to giving models _‘time to think,’_ grokking can be seen as a way to give models _‘time to learn.’_

The issue with the traditional training method is that if we reach overfitting and stop training, the model has indeed memorized the training data. Thus, we simply assumed that overfitting was something to avoid.

But if we extend the training, akin to making a model _‘bang its head against data it already knows again and again,’_ something magical happens. Over time, **the model learns simpler ways to reach the same conclusions.**

On a more technical explanation, most AI training today revolves around regularization, akin to Occam’s Razor. In layman’s terms, the simpler the solution to a problem (the fewer assumptions you need), the better that solution is, **as the model learns only to keep the key elements that dictate a solution**.

As with all things, what is going on in grokking is better understood with an example. Let’s say you train a model to identify cats in photos and, to do so, you provide it:

* with 99 photos of dark-colour cats that are four-legged, hairy, have whiskers, slit-shaped eyes, and a tail,
* and 1 photo of a hairless cat (like a Sphynx).

If the model overfits the data, it will just assume that all cats are dark-colored, four-legged, hairy, and have whiskers, slit-shaped eyes, and tails.

_And the Sphynx?_ It has overfitted the data, so it will still argue that it’s also a cat, but without a better explanation than ‘just because.’

But if we continue to train the model on this data for longer, the model eventually finds a simpler answer to the question, ‘ _What is a cat?_ ’

For example, **it might conclude that having whiskers, slit-shaped eyes, and a tail are sufficient conditions for labeling the animal as a cat,** with being four-legged, hairy, and being a specific dark color a common trait but not generalizable, as not all cats dark-colored or hairy, for instance.

As this solution is simpler yet sufficient, it has a higher chance of generalizing well. Indeed, despite not being hairy or dark color, **the Sphynx is also a cat** under the simpler definition the model reached during grokking.

In a nutshell, by seeing the same data repeatedly, like a human reading this [research paper](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15071>) multiple times (this is me, lol), the model eventually develops a more holistic, sound, and simple understanding of the data.

Thus, with grokking, **models go from memorizing to understanding data.**

_But how do they do that?_

### A Mechanistic Interpretation

In Transformers, the input tokens (the words that are sent to the model) go through a series of updates:

* The attention mechanism updates each token (let’s assume a token is a word) with its surrounding context (the other words in the sequence)
* The MLP layers embed core knowledge from the model into the word (i.e. if the word is ‘Michael Jordan, ’ the model will provide basketball information even if the word ‘basketball’ isn’t present in the sequence).

This is done multiple times until, eventually, the model has harnessed a good understanding of the next word in the sequence.

For example, in the image below, we can see a ‘refusal circuit’ showing how LLMs realize that the input sequence is a dangerous request and should, thus, refuse to answer it.

![Generated by author](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*A9rk_QrGLgHIg7YbcfF_NQ.png)

In frontier AI models, this ‘update’ is done dozens of times, building more complex and powerful features like the ones in the image above.

> In today’s case, they used a GPT-2-level Transformer with just 8 layers.

Fascinatingly, you can ‘intervene’ these models and observe how the meaning of each word ‘changes’ with every new update or, speaking a more ‘AI-esque’ jargon, we can observe what other words or semantics each word is pointing to (or paying attention) at any given time.

Let’s see this with a composition example. For two given explicit facts, the model must be capable of inferring a third. For instance:

**Question:** Was Albert Einstein alive in 1904?

Knowing that:

1. Albert Einstein is the father of Hans Albert Einstein.
2. Hans Albert Einstein was born in 1904.

Response: Yes, Albert Einstein was alive in 1904.

Here, we have:

* two facts (listed above)
* a bridge between the facts (Hans Albert Einstein)
* The inferred fact is that, based on its relationship with his son, Albert was indeed alive in 1904.

Knowing this, the researchers observed the model’s inner circuits over the entire training. When the model was first overfitted, they quickly realized it was memorizing these sequences.

_But how did they know?_

Specifically,**the model directly avoided the bridge (Hans)** and automatically predicted the inferred fact whenever it saw the first two facts during training.

This is not a problem if the model has seen the composition (in that case, we can’t discern whether the model has memorized the composition or not), but when faced with similar yet new compositions, the model could not predict the new inferred fact because it completely ignored the bridge that connected both facts and enabled the third (the consequence of that relationship).

> In other words, it memorized the relationship between the three facts instead of understanding that the third is inferred based on the relationship between the first two facts.

But when they extended the training (grokking), things changed. Using the below image as a reference:

* At the earlier layers, the model retrieves the first fact (Albert is Hans’ father) and the bridge (Hans, connecting the two facts).
* At layer 5, the first fact (r1) value has been updated to point directly into the bridge.

In simple terms, the words from the first fact are directly pointing (or paying attention to) to Hans. Intuitively, the model, based on the provided information, is aware that ‘Hans’ is the bridge that connects both facts.

> Recall that previously to grokking, the bridge was completely avoided.

* Importantly, the updated value of fact 2 (r2 below, or the fact that Hans was born in 1904) continues to point to itself, ensuring that the model does not forget that second fact.
* Finally, in the later layers, the model retrieves the second fact and, using the bridge, points to the inferred fact (depicted as ‘t’). In other words, in the last layers, instead of memorizing the third fact the model actively infers it.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Fa_oGuV7bfyTCDVOhHWC9g.png)

_So what does all this complicated process mean?_

In a nutshell, **the model has:**

* built a compositional reasoning circuit that can take two facts,
* identify their relationship (the bridge),
* and use this connection to infer the third fact, **even in situations it has never seen before.**

> In other words, it’s not memorizing the relationship; it’s understanding it.

Therefore, it doesn’t jump steps (unlike when it memorized the data, as it did not require the bridge to find the inferred fact because it had memorized it).

Now, not only has it interiorized the reasoning process (it expects the bridge), **but it follows the same steps humans do** (locate the bridge and, that way, infer the third fact), imitating the reasoning process.

_But does grokking ‘walk the talk’?_ Boy it does.

### Impressive Results

Unlike frontier AI models, a simple grokked GPT-2 level model (a model from 2019, prehistoric by today’s standards) **completely obliterates GPT-4 Turbo and Gemini 1.5 Pro** , despite both using retrieval augmented generation to improve results.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*ZyKlmMPKs-lNcycjH-DNXw.png)

Although they did not publish the evaluation dataset, the tasks were similar to the one below, where the model had to connect several entities and identify a complex relationship, like _‘who’s older, Rick or John?’_

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*DO7HaED5nZYgsO5FARZKzQ.png)

> Interestingly, both GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet get this one correct, but suffer with more complex ones while the grokked transformer effortlessly nails the test.

The data is impossible to ignore, and I expect grokked models to become a common theme shortly, a key trend we will all soon hear about.

## Will it Become Common?

From a purely technological standpoint, this is a very relevant achievement— not in the architectures used, but in how we train models.

Seeing how a grokked GPT-2 level model (1.5 billion parameters) destroyed frontier models in reasoning problems, models up to a thousand times larger, **I wouldn’t be surprised if many labs soon release grokked models in the 2-to-10 billion parameter range.**
