## Background

### MoE (Mixture-of-Experts) in Transformers

MoE stands for Mixture-of-Experts, and in the context of Transformer models, this typically involves replacing the FFN in every few Transformer layers with multiple FFNs, each serving as an Expert. When an input token is processed, a Gating operation selects the top-K Experts and routes the token to the chosen FFNs, ensuring that only the selected Experts are activated.

### Load Balancing and Why It Matters

In our previous restaurant analogy, we explained the concept of MoE using a restaurant that serves multiple cuisines, where each chef acts as an expert, and the head chef functions as the Gating operation, assigning each dish to the chefs with the right skills.

To make sure such a system works effectively, we need:

* Each specialized chef must master the skills required for their specific cuisine (for example, the dumpling-making chef must know how to make dumplings), while collectively, they can handle all the dishes.
* The head chef possesses a good understanding of the expertise of all the specialized chefs and can assign orders efficiently based on their skills.

In MoE, the former corresponds to the trade-off between expert specialization and knowledge sharing, which we discussed in detail in [**DeepSeekMoE**](<https://medium.com/towards-artificial-intelligence/deepseek-v3-part-2-deepseekmoe-f3ae6ff9e296>)**.** The latter reflects the importance of load balancing, which is the main focus of this article.

**So what makes load-balancing so important?**

The reason for this is that MoE struggles when load imbalance occurs, with the most common issue being “route collapse,” where only a small subset of experts receive most of the input tokens, while the remaining experts remain underutilized.

As a result, most computations are handled by the over-loaded experts, leading to bottlenecks in hardware utilization, as experts are typically distributed across multiple GPU cores.

Route collapse can also cause training instability due to gradient conflict. As the over-loaded experts receive a larger number of input tokens, they accumulate bigger gradients and learn much faster than the under-loaded experts. Consequently, the gradients from the over-loaded and under-loaded experts may diverge in both magnitude and direction, making it more difficult for the training process to converge.

Finally, load imbalance in MoE can lead to poor performance and generalization, as the under-loaded experts don’t receive enough training tokens to acquire meaningful knowledge.

Since load balancing is crucial in MoE, various techniques have been proposed to address this issue. Among these prior efforts, the most commonly used strategies are adding auxiliary loss for load balancing and Expert Choice.

### Load-Balancing with Auxiliary Loss

A common strategy to improve load balancing is to introduce auxiliary loss functions on top of the original objective during model training.

Figure 2. An example of auxiliary loss used for enforcing load balancing. Image edited from [5].

The figure above shows an example of auxiliary loss functions, where

* N is the number of experts, T is the number of tokens, and K is the number of activated experts for each input token.
* s_{i, t} is the output from Gating, which has been normalized to [0, 1] via Softmax, indicating the probability of the t-th token choosing the i-th expert. Vector u_t is the input hidden state for the t-th token, while e_i is the centroid for the i-th expert, which can be seen as the average token embedding routed to the i-th expert in the past. Thus, s_{i, t} measures how close the current input is to the average tokens received by the i-th expert.
* Therefore, P_i can be seen as the average probability of choosing the i-th expert for the entire input sequence.
* f_i represents the fraction of tokens routed to the i-th expert.

Note that f_i is non-differentiable, so minimizing the above loss function effectively becomes minimizing s_{i, t}. Additionally, since f_i depends on s_{i, t}, any adjustment applied to s_{i, t} will also impact f_i, causing the loads assigned to each expert to be adjusted accordingly.

However, balancing load with such auxiliary losses comes at a cost, as their gradient may interfere with the gradient from the language modeling objective, leading to degraded model performance. This issue is particularly pronounced in the case of extreme imbalance, where both f_i and P_i for the overloaded expert become very large.

Therefore, balancing load with this approach requires careful weighting of the auxiliary loss. To illustrate this more clearly, the authors of [5] conducted an experiment by training models with different alpha values. The results are shown in the figure below, where the y-axis represents model performance in terms of perplexity, and the x-axis shows MaxVio, a metric indicating the degree of load imbalance. A higher MaxVio value indicates more severe load imbalance (i stands for the i-th expert).

### Expert Choice

Another prior work we want to mention here is Expert Choice [7], which proposes a simple yet effective load balancing method by shifting the routing strategy from “token choice” to “expert choice.”

More specifically, the gating scores in MoE routing are often calculated using Softmax on top of an affinity matrix, as shown in Figure 2. Traditional routing methods apply Softmax across the token dimension to select experts for each token, which is why these methods are referred to as “token choice.” The issue with this approach is that it doesn’t allow control over how many tokens each expert receives, ultimately leading to the load imbalance problem.

Expert Choice, on the other hand, applies Softmax across the expert dimension by selecting tokens routed to each expert. As a result, the tokens received by each expert are perfectly balanced by design, eliminating the need for an auxiliary loss to achieve load balancing. In the original paper [7], this method demonstrates both improved model performance and faster training speed.

However, one limitation of Expert Choice is the future token leakage issue. Since each expert decides which token to process after seeing all token routing scores, it violates causality, which can be a serious problem in auto-regressive tasks such as text generation and machine translation.

### DeepSeek’s Auxiliary-loss-free Load Balancing

To address the load balancing issue without introducing gradient interference, DeepSeek proposes a new technique called Loss-Free Balancing, which directly adjusts the gating scores s_{i, t}.

As we mentioned before, when we minimize the auxiliary loss illustrated in Figure 2, that finally becomes minimizing P_i by adjusting s_{i, t}.

Therefore, if we can directly adjust s_{i, t}, in theory, we should be able to achieve a similar effect as applying an auxiliary loss.

To achieve this, an expert-wise bias is added to the gating scores of each expert, as shown in the figure below. Note that b_i is NOT used in the final gating scores (as we will see later, this bias is also non-differentiable), but it is used in TopK to select experts.

Figure 4. Introducing bias b_i to gating scores. Image from [5].

The bias b_i is calculated in a very intuitive way, as shown in the figure below: we first compute the average number of tokens assigned to each expert and their average, then calculate the difference between each expert’s assigned tokens and the average. The bias is determined by the sign of this difference (or error), multiplied by a fixed update rate, which is a tunable hyper-parameter. In later sections, we will explore more experiments on the impact of this hyper-parameter.

## Evaluation

Basically, there are three important questions need to be answered:

* Can the proposed method achieve a better trade-off between performance and load balance?
* What is the impact of the update rate u in Figure 5?
* Can we further optimize the bias updating rule, considering it is so simple?

### Impact of Hyper-parameter (Update Rate)

As shown in Figure 5, the proposed method introduces a new hyper-parameter uu called the update rate. A natural question arises: how does this hyper-parameter impact the effectiveness of the loss-free approach? Specifically, we need to understand whether the loss-free approach is sensitive or robust to the value of uu, and how to select the optimal value to best leverage the proposed method.

As mentioned earlier, adding a bias term to the gating scores is conceptually similar to applying direct gradient updates to the gating scores without relying on backpropagation through a loss function. In this context, the update rate uu plays a similar role to the step size in gradient updates. From this perspective, we can expect a similar impact: a small update rate may lead to slow convergence, while an excessively large update rate could cause instability and fluctuations.

In the original paper, the authors conduct experiments with different update rates ranging from 1e-4 to 1e-2, and the results are shown in the figure below:

Figure 9. The impact of update rate on training load balance. Image from [5].

As we expected, MaxVio drops more slowly when uu is too small (1e-4 in this case). However, an overly large uu also negatively impacts the process, leading to higher MaxVio throughout training due to increased fluctuations.

### Alternative Bias Updating Rules

To answer the third question, the authors experiment with several alternative strategies and compare them with the proposed version:

* **Variant 1** : calculating bias using the value of e_i, instead of only its sign, i.e. from b_i = b_i +u∗**sign**(e_i) to b_i = b_i +u∗e_i.

## Summary

In this article, we explain the auxiliary-loss-free load balancing method used in DeepSeekMoE, one of the key architectural innovations employed in DeepSeek models.

More specifically, we first introduce the fundamentals of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), emphasizing the importance of load balancing and reviewing prior solutions, including auxiliary loss methods and Expert Choice. We then explain DeepSeek’s loss-free load balancing approach and discuss its performance.

Our key takeaway is that DeepSeek’s loss-free method avoids introducing gradient interference while preserving causality, with its effectiveness demonstrated through empirical results from the original paper.
