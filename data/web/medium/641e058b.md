_Building successful machine learning systems is not just about having the latest algorithms or the most powerful GPUs. The reality is that most ML projects fail not because of technical limitations, but because teams do not get the fundamentals right from the start. Think of it like building a house — we can have the most beautiful architectural design, but if our foundation is weak, everything will eventually crumble._

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*VYd4QPEqkT4t6ozOCcyHug.png)

In this post, we will explore three critical skills that separate successful ML projects from the ones that get abandoned after months of work: properly framing the ML problems, creating thoughtful design documents, and making sure the technical work actually serves real business needs. These might not be the most exciting topics compared to discussing the latest neural network architectures, but they are what make the difference between projects that deliver real value and those that end up as expensive learning experiences.

## The Challenge of Framing ML Problems

Picture this scenario: A company executive walks into a meeting and says, “Our customer support is too slow. Can we use AI to fix this?” It is a common request, and the natural response from most ML teams is to start brainstorming about chatbots, natural language processing models, automated response systems etc. But “slow customer support” is not actually an ML problem. It is a business problem that might benefit from an ML solution, but there is a crucial translation step that often gets skipped.

This translation step is where many projects go wrong. Remarkable amount of ML projects never make it to production, and a significant portion of these failures happen because teams solve the **_wrong problem_**. They build technically impressive solutions that do not address the real business need.

## Breaking Down Business Problems into ML Components

To transform a unclear business challenge into something machine learning can actually work, we need to clearly define three essential elements:

**Inputs** : What data will the model work with? This needs to be specific and available.

**Outputs** : What exactly should the model predict or classify?

**Objective Function** : How will we measure whether the model is doing a good job?

Let’s stick with our customer support example. After some investigation, we might discover that the real bottleneck isn’t response time at all — it’s misrouting, where customer requests are frequently sent to the wrong departments (for example, an IT-related issue might initially be directed to HR; the HR team then realizes the mistake, reports it, and only after some delay is the request forwarded to the IT department). This routing problem causes delays because requests have to be forwarded multiple times before reaching the right team.

Now we can reframe this as a concrete ML problem:

* **Input** : The text content of a customer support request
* **Output** : Which department should handle this request (IT, Billing, HR, Technical Support, etc.)
* **Objective** : Minimize the number of incorrectly routed requests

Suddenly, we **_have something actionable_**. This is now a **_multiclass classification problem_** , and we can start thinking about datasets, feature extraction, model architectures, and evaluation metrics. More importantly, we can estimate the business impact — if we reduce misrouted requests by 50%, how much time and money does that save?

## Understanding the Landscape of ML Task Types

The type of output that the model produces fundamentally determines what kind of ML problem we are dealing with. Understanding these categories helps us choose the right approach and set appropriate expectations.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*sNatK2iTkwi789veapHAyA.png)

### The Flexibility Factor

Here is something that surprises many people new to ML. We can often reframe the same business problem as **_either classification or regression_** , and the choice can significantly impact complexity of our project and success.

Take house price prediction:

* _Regression approach_ : Predict the exact price ($347,500)
* _Classification approach_ : Predict a price range (Under $300k, $300k-$500k, Over $500k)

The regression approach might seem more precise, but the classification approach could be more **_practical_** if our users (like real estate agents) primarily care about price ranges rather than exact values. It might also be more **_robust to outliers_** and **_easier_** to evaluate.

Similarly, consider Medium post send time optimization:

* _Classification approach_ : Predict the best time slot (Morning, Afternoon, Evening)
* _Regression approach_ : Predict the exact optimal time (10:23 AM)

The classification approach might be easier to implement and more **_actionable_** for marketers, even if it is technically less precise.

### Handling Complex Scenarios

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*-n_OugI6KvhTOJcu4NWPfw.png)

Some problems come with their own unique challenges that require creative problem-framing approaches.

**High Cardinality Classification**

What happens when we have thousands or even millions of possible categories? Imagine building a product recommendation system for Amazon, which has millions of products. Traditional classification approaches struggle here because:

* We need substantial training data for each category (E.g: at least 100 examples per class). Because rare categories become nearly impossible to predict accurately.
* The model architecture becomes computationally expensive and unwieldy.
* New products cannot be handled without retraining.

![Since the different types of labels is too much, labels with less sample tend to be misclassified.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*v99oOLYAc7yAZk5BEpE0iA.png)

One effective solution is hierarchical classification. Instead of trying to predict the exact product in one step, we break it down:

1. First, predict the general category (Electronics, Books, Clothing)
2. Then predict the subcategory (Laptops, Smartphones, Tablets)
3. Finally, predict the specific product within that subcategory

This approach is more manageable and often more accurate because it mirrors how humans think about categorization.

**The Strategic Reframing Approach**

Sometimes, completely changing how we frame a problem can make it dramatically more manageable. Consider the challenge of predicting which **product** a user will purchase next on an e-commerce site.

**Traditional Multiclass Approach**

You might create a model with **N outputs** — one for each product in the catalog. But catalogs often contain thousands of items and are constantly evolving. Every time a new product is added or removed, the entire model would need to be retrained, which is inefficient and hard to maintain in a real-time environment.

**Regression Reframing**

A better approach is to frame this as a **regression task**. The input consists of features from the **user** (e.g., browsing history, preferences), the **environment** (e.g., time of day, device), and the **product** (e.g., category, price, popularity). The output is a single value between 0 and 1 — the higher the value, the more likely the user is to buy that product in the current context.

Now, for each product, you can predict the **probability** that the user will purchase it next. When new products are added to the catalog, we just start making predictions for them — without needing to retrain the entire model. This approach is far more scalable, flexible, and production-ready.

This kind of strategic thinking about problem framing can save us months of work and make our system much more maintainable in the long run.

## Understanding ML’s Unique User Experience Challenges

Traditional software is deterministic — give it the same input, and you’ll get the same output every time. ML systems are fundamentally different. They’re probabilistic, which means they can give different results for the same input at different times. This creates unique challenges for user experience design.

**The Consistency Problem**

Users expect software to behave consistently. If they perform the same action twice, they expect the same result. But ML systems learn and adapt, which can lead to confusing user experiences.

In dynamic systems powered by machine learning, suggestions and results often change based on evolving user behavior. While this may be technically optimal, it can lead to confusion when users return expecting previous preferences or settings to remain consistent. If the system updates too aggressively, users may feel disoriented or frustrated when familiar options are no longer visible.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*XGfQaVO7_tuARCSTdRERsQ.png)

To prevent this, it’s important to consider a consistency-accuracy trade-off. This means defining clear rules for when the system should prioritize consistency — preserving familiar elements for the user — and when it can adapt to offer new or updated suggestions. The key lesson is that technical correctness should be balanced with user expectations. A smooth, predictable user experience sometimes requires deliberately limiting how much the system changes in response to data.

**The “Mostly Correct” Challenge**

Large Language Models like GPT have introduced a new category of challenge: the “mostly correct” prediction. These models can generate responses that are mostly correct but contain subtle errors that are difficult for users to catch.

This creates a paradox. The high accuracy makes the system seem reliable, but the occasional errors can be dangerous if users over-rely on the system. The impact depends heavily on the use case and the user’s expertise level.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*TOZx_TB1n8meJg5eWFO0ZQ.png)

For example, using an LLM (like GPT) to help draft customer support responses works well because:

* Human agents can easily spot errors or inappropriate responses
* The cost of an error is relatively low (maybe a slightly awkward email)
* The system provides value even when it’s not perfect

But using LLM to generate code for someone who doesn’t know the programming language is problematic because:

* Non-experts can’t easily identify errors in the generated code
* The cost of errors can be high (security vulnerabilities, system crashes)
* “Mostly correct” code can be worse than no code at all

The solution often involves a “** _human-in-the-loop_** ” approach. Instead of showing users a single “best” prediction, show them multiple options and let them choose. Design your interface so that even non-experts can evaluate the quality of different options. Provide confidence scores or uncertainty indicators when appropriate.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:499/1*kZVOWsuxf3h3BnlC-TQ6cA.png)

**Managing Latency and Performance Expectations**

ML models can be unpredictably slow. A model that usually responds in 100 milliseconds might occasionally take 10 seconds, especially when dealing with complex or unusual inputs. Users don’t care about the technical reasons for this delay — **_they just know your app feels broken_**.

The solution is building what’s called a “** _smooth failing_** ” system. Have backup approaches ready:

* Simpler models that are less accurate but faster
* Cached predictions for common queries
* Heuristic rules that can provide reasonable answers quickly
* Graceful degradation that maintains core functionality even when the ML system is slow

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*moDkGazll6TPcq_kjBx3zA.png)

For example, a search engine might use a complex neural ranking model for most queries, but fall back to simpler keyword matching if the neural model is taking too long. The results might be slightly less relevant, but users get fast responses.

## Design Documents: North Star of the Project

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*5siReE_kXdw0NRmNGko8sQ.png)

If you worked in high-velocity tech environments — especially startups — you might think, _“Design documents sound like unnecessary paperwork that’ll just slow us down.”_ But in reality, projects that skip thoughtful design documentation almost always take longer and deliver less value than those that invest time upfront to plan carefully.

A good design document is not unnecessary paperwork but a north star of our project. It forces us to think through challenges before we encounter them, helps us communicate our vision to stakeholders, and provides a reference point when (not if) requirements change.

### Why Design Documents Are Critical for ML Projects

**They Reveal Hidden Problems**

Writing forces us to think systematically about our approach. We might discover that our brilliant idea has a fundamental flaw before we spend three months implementing it. Thanks to the design document, we might see:

* Our planned solution would require data we do not have access to
* The system would be too slow for their real-time requirements
* There are legal or ethical issues they hadn’t considered
* The business value doesn’t justify the development cost

**Aligning Diverse Teams**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*KL-1jZAFvWWS8od7Y4NSlA.png)

ML projects typically involve data scientists, software engineers, product managers, business stakeholders, domain experts, etc. These groups often have different perspectives, vocabularies, and priorities. Without a shared understanding of what we are building and why, teams often end up working toward different goals.

A design document gets everyone literally on the same page. It forces us to translate between technical and business languages and ensures that the data scientist’s model accuracy goals align with the product manager’s user experience requirements.

**They Help You Say No**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*ouT2PpOimkDz7hvUxlsvWA.png)

Scope creep is one of the biggest threats to ML projects. Stakeholders love to suggest additions: “While we’re building the recommendation system, could it also predict inventory needs?” or “Can we add real-time personalization while we’re at it?”

A well-written design document with clear goals and anti-goals gives us a framework for evaluating these requests. We can point to our stated objectives and explain how the proposed addition fits (or doesn’t fit) with our current scope and timeline.

**They Save Time in the Long Run**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*A51cw9G66dYPHiouUiS8dA.png)

Yes, writing a design document takes time upfront. But that time investment pays dividends when:

* New team members need to understand the project
* You need to debug issues months later
* Requirements change and you need to assess the impact
* You’re planning related projects and want to learn from this one

## What Goes Into an Effective Design Document

A good ML design document should cover several key areas, though the exact structure can vary based on your project and organization.

### Problem Definition and Context

Start with the business context. **_Why does this problem matter? What’s the current state, and what would success look like?_** Don’t assume everyone reading your document understands the background.

For example, instead of writing “Build a recommendation system for products,” write something like: “Our e-commerce site currently shows the same featured products to all users on the homepage. This leads to low click-through rates (2.1%) and poor conversion (0.3%) because the products aren’t relevant to most visitors. We want to build a personalized recommendation system that shows each user products they’re likely to be interested in, with the goal of improving homepage click-through rates to 5% and conversion to 1.2%.”

### Clear Goals and Anti-Goals

This is one of the most powerful sections, and it’s often overlooked. Goals are obvious — what you want to achieve. But anti-goals are equally important. These are explicit statements about what you’re NOT trying to do.

Anti-goals help you avoid over-engineering and keep your scope focused. For a recommendation system at a small boutique, your anti-goals might include:

* We don’t need to handle millions of users (current traffic is 10,000 monthly visitors)
* Real-time updates aren’t critical (batch processing daily is sufficient)
* We don’t need to support multiple languages initially
* We don’t need to integrate with external data sources

These anti-goals help you choose simpler solutions and avoid getting bogged down in requirements that don’t matter for your specific use case.

### Technical Approach Overview

Describe your planned solution at a high level. **_What type of model will you use? What features will you extract? How will you train and evaluate the model?_** You don’t need to have every detail figured out, but you should have a reasonable plan.

Be honest about uncertainties. If you’re not sure whether a neural network or gradient boosting will work better, say so. Outline your plan for testing different approaches.

### Data Requirements and Availability

ML projects live or die by their data. Be explicit about:

* What data you need
* What data you currently have access to
* What data you’ll need to collect
* Data quality concerns
* Privacy and legal considerations

This section often reveals show-stopping issues early. Maybe the data you need exists but is owned by a different team that’s reluctant to share it. Maybe the data exists but has quality issues that will require significant cleaning. Better to discover these issues now than after you’ve started building.

### Success Metrics and Evaluation Plan

How will you know if your project succeeded? Be specific and measurable. “Improve customer satisfaction” is not a success metric. “Reduce average customer support resolution time from 24 hours to 12 hours” is.

Define both technical metrics (model accuracy, precision, recall) and business metrics (revenue impact, user engagement, operational efficiency). Explain how you’ll measure these metrics and what thresholds constitute success.

### Risks, Limitations, and Mitigation Strategies

This section is crucial but often rushed. Think systematically about what could go wrong:

* Technical risks (model performance, scalability, integration challenges)
* Business risks (changing requirements, resource constraints, competitive pressures)
* External risks (data availability, regulatory changes, vendor dependencies)

For each significant risk, outline your mitigation strate** _gy. You don’t need to solve every potential problem_** , but you should show that you’ve thought them through.

### Timeline and Resource Requirements

Provide realistic estimates for development phases, resource needs, and key milestones. Be honest about uncertainties — ML projects are inherently experimental, and timelines often slip.

Break the project into phases with clear deliverables. This makes it easier to track progress and gives you natural points to reassess and adjust the plan.

## Common Design Document Misbeliefs and Misconceptions

### 1: “Only big companies need design documents”

Startups may think they are too agile for design documents. In reality, startups need them more because they have less margin for error. A startup cannot afford to spend three months building the wrong thing or discover halfway through development that their approach won’t work.

**_The document doesn’t need to be hundreds of pages_**. A simple, focused document that covers the key points is **_better than no document at all_**.

### 2: “Design documents are only for complex projects”

Even simple projects benefit from the thinking process that design documents encourage. You might discover that your “simple” project has hidden complexities, or that there’s an even simpler approach you hadn’t considered.

### 3: “We need to follow a strict template”

Templates can be helpful starting points, but they shouldn’t be rigid requirements. Your design document should serve your project’s needs. A computer vision project might need different sections than a natural language processing project.

### 4: “Design documents must be perfect before we start coding”

Design documents are **_living_** documents that evolve as you learn more about your problem. Don’t let perfect be the enemy of good. It’s better to have an imperfect document that you iterate on than to spend weeks trying to perfect it before starting development.

## The Review and Iteration Process

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*tW0zoStchmw2mXZok-eZUw.png)

Once you’ve written your design document, the real value comes from getting **_feedback_**. Share it widely — not just with your immediate team, but with stakeholders across the organization.

### Encourage Critical Feedback

Make it clear that you want people to poke holes in your approach. Ask specific questions:

* “What am I missing about the business context?”
* “What technical risks haven’t I considered?”
* “Are there simpler approaches I should explore?”
* “What would make this project more valuable to your team?”

Generic responses like “looks good to me” aren’t helpful. Push for specific, actionable feedback.

### Understand the Reasoning Behind Suggestions

When reviewers suggest changes, understand their reasoning. Are they concerned about technical feasibility? Business value? Resource constraints? Timeline pressure? The underlying concern might be more important than the specific suggestion.

### Iterate Based on Feedback

Expect your design document to go through multiple revisions. This isn’t a sign of failure — it’s a sign that the process is working.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*GdATA4BSnH-3Q3otAnSDSA.png)

### Keep It Updated

Your design document shouldn’t be a write-once artifact that gets filed away. As you learn more about your problem, your data, and your users, update your document. When requirements change, update your document. When you discover new risks or opportunities, add them to your document.

## Real-World Constraints and Trade-offs

Academic ML focuses on maximizing accuracy on clean datasets. Real-world ML is messier and requires considering constraints that don’t appear in research papers.

**Correctness vs. Robustness**

This is a fundamental design choice that affects your entire system:

_Correctness-focused systems_ never return an inaccurate result. If the system isn’t confident in its prediction, it returns no result rather than a potentially wrong one. This approach is essential for safety-critical applications like medical diagnosis, autonomous vehicles, or financial trading systems.

_Robustness-focused systems_ always try to provide some response, even if it might be inaccurate sometimes. This approach makes sense for consumer applications where users would rather have an imperfect system than one that frequently says “I don’t know.”

The choice depends on the cost of different types of errors in your specific context.

**Resource and Performance Constraints**

Real-world systems have limits that academic research often ignores:

_Computational Budget_ : You might not be able to use the most accurate model if it’s too expensive to run at scale. Sometimes a simpler model that you can afford to run is better than a complex model you can only use occasionally.

_Latency Requirements_ : Users might prefer a fast, decent answer over a slow, perfect one. E-commerce sites have found that even 100ms of additional latency can hurt conversion rates.

_Data Availability_ : We might not have access to all the data that would theoretically improve your model. Privacy regulations, business partnerships, or technical limitations might restrict what data you can use.

## Measuring Success

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*6WGOyBGn-N0_9F5_6IK1sA.png)

Finally, remember that the success of an ML system isn’t measured by technical metrics alone. Yes, accuracy and F1 scores matter, but they’re not the end goal. The real measures of success are (in business):

* Does the system solve the original business problem?
* Do users find it helpful and easy to use?
* Does it integrate well with existing workflows?
* Can it be maintained and updated over time?
* Does it provide enough value to justify its costs?

Keep these broader success criteria in mind throughout your project. They should influence your problem framing, your design decisions, and your evaluation metrics.

## Putting It All Together: A Complete Example

Let’s walk through how these concepts work together in practice with a realistic example. Imagine you’re working for an e-commerce company that wants to improve their email marketing effectiveness.

### Step 1: Problem Framing

**Initial Business Problem** : “Our email marketing campaigns have low open rates and we’re losing subscribers.”

After investigation, you discover several specific issues:

* Generic timing: emails are sent at the same time for all users
* One-size-fits-all content: same emails go to all segments
* No personalization based on user behavior

You decide to focus first on the timing issue because it seems most tractable and has clear success metrics.

**ML Problem Framing** :

* **Input** : User behavior data (past email opens, website activity, purchase history), email content features (subject line, sender, category), temporal features (day of week, season)
* **Output** : Optimal send time for each user-email combination
* **Objective** : Maximize email open rates across the user base

We consider different ways to frame the output:

* Regression: Predict exact optimal time (10:23 AM)
* Classification: Predict optimal time bucket (Morning, Afternoon, Evening, Night)
* Ranking: Rank different time slots by predicted effectiveness

You choose the classification approach because:

* It’s simpler to implement and evaluate
* The business can easily act on time bucket recommendations
* It’s more robust to noise in the training data
* You can always refine to more specific times later

### Step 2: Design Document Creation

Your design document includes:

**Goals** :

* Increase average email open rates from 18% to 25%
* Personalize send times for individual users
* Handle seasonal patterns and special events
* Integrate with existing email marketing platform

**Anti-Goals** :

* We don’t need real-time predictions (batch processing is fine)
* We don’t need to optimize for click-through rates initially (just opens)
* We don’t need to handle multiple languages in the first version
* We don’t need to predict optimal content (just timing)

**Technical Approach** :

* Use historical engagement data to learn user-specific patterns
* Feature engineering around temporal patterns, user demographics, and email characteristics
* Start with gradient boosting models for interpretability
* Evaluation using A/B testing framework
* Fallback to population-level defaults for new users

**Risks and Mitigation** :

* _Risk_ : Training data might be biased toward current send times

_Mitigation_ : Include randomized experiments in training data collection

*  _Risk_ : User patterns might change over time

_Mitigation_ : Regular model retraining and monitoring for drift

*  _Risk_ : System needs to handle users with limited history

_Mitigation_ : Hierarchical approach with population and segment-level defaults

**Success Metrics** :

* Primary: Overall open rate improvement (target: 18% → 25%)
* Secondary: Reduction in unsubscribe rates
* Technical: Model accuracy on hold-out test set
* Business: Revenue attributed to email marketing

### Step 3: Business Alignment and Human Factors

**User Experience Considerations** :

* How will marketers interact with this system?
* What happens when the model is uncertain about the best time?
* How do you handle edge cases (new users, unusual behavior)?
* What level of control do marketers want over the system?

**Team Coordination** :

* Work closely with email marketing team to understand their workflow
* Involve customer success team to understand user preferences and complaints
* Coordinate with data engineering team for data pipeline requirements
* Regular check-ins with business stakeholders on progress and results

**Implementation Strategy** : You decide on a hybrid approach:

* Use ML predictions for users with sufficient data
* Fall back to segment-level heuristics for new users
* Always allow marketers to override the system’s recommendations
* Provide confidence scores so marketers know when to trust the predictions
* Start with a limited rollout to measure impact before full deployment

### Step 4: Addressing Real-World Complexities

**Data Challenges** :

* Some users have very sparse engagement history
* Email engagement might be influenced by external factors (holidays, news events, competitive campaigns)
* Data quality issues with timezone handling and duplicate records

**Technical Constraints** :

* Predictions need to be generated 24 hours in advance for campaign scheduling
* System needs to handle 50 million users with reasonable computational cost
* Integration with legacy email platform has API limitations

**Business Constraints** :

* Marketing team wants to maintain some campaigns at fixed times (newsletters, announcements)
* Legal requirements around email frequency and unsubscribe handling
* Budget constraints on computational resources

>  _We started with a clear business problem — low email open rates and subscriber loss — and transformed it into a targeted machine learning problem focused on optimizing send times for individual users. By choosing a classification approach, we ensured ease of implementation and alignment with business needs, while incorporating fallback mechanisms and confidence scores to handle real-world challenges like sparse data and edge cases. Our solution emphasizes seamless integration with existing systems, supports human oversight, and is guided by measurable success metrics, laying a strong foundation for more personalized and effective email marketing at scale._

## Conclusion

Building successful ML systems requires more than just technical expertise. It requires the ability to translate business problems into ML problems, the discipline to plan thoroughly before diving into implementation, and the wisdom to keep human needs at the center of your technical decisions.

These skills — problem framing, design documentation, and business alignment — might seem less exciting than the latest neural network architectures, but they’re what separate successful ML projects from the ones that get shelved after months of work.

Master these fundamentals, and you’ll find that your ML projects not only work technically but actually deliver value to real users and real businesses. And in the end, that’s what matters most.
