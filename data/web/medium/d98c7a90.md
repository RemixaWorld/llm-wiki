## Data-augmented LLM applications and Query types

When it comes to data-augmented LLM applications, there are various forms they can take, from question-answering bots to semantic processing operators within complex data pipelines. In general, these applications aim to establish a mapping from a user’s query to an answer based on given data. The challenge lies in incorporating external data to accurately address the user’s queries. This introduces different levels of complexity and engagement, requiring the LLM to perform cognitive processing tasks.

### Stratification of Queries:

In the landscape of data-augmented LLM applications, queries can be stratified based on their complexity and the depth of data interaction required. This stratification helps us understand the varying levels of cognitive processing that LLMs must perform to generate accurate responses. The levels include explicit facts, implicit facts, interpretable rationales, and hidden rationales.

**Level-1 Explicit Facts:** These queries directly ask for explicit facts present in the given data.

**Level-2 Implicit Facts:** These queries require some level of common sense reasoning or basic logical deductions to uncover implicit facts.

**Level-3: Interpretable Rationales:** These queries require more than just factual knowledge. They demand an understanding of domain-specific reasoning and the ability to apply it effectively within the given context.

Take pharmaceuticals, for example. An LLM must be able to interpret FDA guidance documents, which outline the regulatory requirements for drug applications. Similarly, in customer support, the model needs to follow structured workflows to process inquiries while adhering to predefined protocols. In healthcare, diagnostic manuals, such as those guiding the management of acute chest pain, provide clear procedural steps and decision trees for specific conditions. By leveraging these external rationales, specialized LLM systems can act as expert advisors, whether evaluating regulatory compliance, assisting customers, or managing medical conditions.

**Level-4: Hidden Rationales:** This level tackles a more complex challenge. It handles queries where the rationale is not explicitly documented but must be inferred from patterns and outcomes in external data. Hidden rationales involve implicit reasoning chains, logical relationships, and the non-trivial task of identifying and extracting the reasoning needed for each query.

For example, in IT operations, a cloud team might have handled numerous incidents, each with unique circumstances and resolutions. An LLM would need to mine this tacit knowledge, recognizing patterns in how past incidents were resolved. Similarly, in software development, debugging histories often contain implicit insights, even though the decision-making process isn’t systematically recorded.

## Explicit Fact Queries (L1)

Explicit fact queries are the simplest type of data-augmented queries. These queries can be answered by directly accessing specific documents or snippets within the dataset. The answers to these questions are usually found in plain text within the documents, requiring minimal reasoning or complex inference.

**Definition:**
Explicit fact queries involve retrieving answers directly from specific data segments within the dataset. These queries rely on the retrieval of relevant data segments and constructing the answer based on that information.

The dataset D can be divided into smaller units or segments, labeled as D1, D2, . . . , Dn, in various ways: D = {D1, D2, . . . , Dn} . Each unit Di is relatively compact and contains more focused and specific information.

For any given query q ∈ Q, not all segments within D are necessary to formulate a response. We define a function δ : Q × D → {0, 1} to indicate whether a data segment d ∈ D is essential for a particular query q. Here, δ(q, d) = 1 means that data segment d is crucial for answering query q, while δ(q, d) = 0 indicates it is not.

The information reliance of query q, represented by the subset of segments essential for addressing query q, is defined as: Dep(q) = {d | d ∈ D and δ(q, d) = 1}

It’s straightforward to understand that Dep(q) is an element of P(D), where P(D) represents the power set of D.

Q1 queries, also known as explicit fact queries, are characterized by their ability to be answered directly from specific portions of a dataset D

.For any given query q with a corresponding answer a, an explicit fact query must have:

1. **A data retrieval mechanism** rD : Q → P(D) that pinpoints the relevant parts of D needed to answer q. This mechanism ensures that rD(q) closely aligns with Dep(q), which is the smallest subset of D required to respond to q.
2. **An answer generation function** θ, typically an LLM prompted for inference, that creates the answer a using only the information retrieved by rD. The output θ(rD(q)) should be identical or very similar to a, showcasing the query’s dependence on readily available, explicit facts.

### Challenges and Solutions:

Queries at this level primarily require accurate data retrieval to enable LLMs to provide correct answers. RAG is the most widely used technical solution for handling these queries due to its effectiveness, adaptability, and cost-efficiency. However, even with RAG, building a robust and high-quality system presents significant hurdles, including:

• **Data Handling Complexities:** External data is often highly unstructured and includes various formats like tables, images, and videos. Moreover, the process of dividing or “chunking” this data poses challenges in preserving the original context and meaning.

• **Information Retrieval Hurdles:** Extracting relevant data segments from a large, unstructured dataset can be computationally demanding and error-prone. The challenge lies in developing efficient and precise retrieval methods.

• **Assessment Difficulties:** Measuring the performance of a RAG system, especially at the component level, is a complex task. It requires developing comprehensive metrics that can accurately gauge the quality of both data retrieval and response generation.

## Retrieval-augmented Generation (RAG) for Explicit Fact Queries:

### What is RAG?

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is all about enhancing a large language model (LLM) by adding extra information from an external source to improve its responses.

### How RAG works?

Here’s how it works in practice:

It all starts with a question from a user, like, “How do I do <something>?”

RAG mainly involved 2 steps — Retrieval followed by Augmented Question Answering.

The first step is _retrieval_. This means the system takes the user’s question and searches a knowledge base for the most relevant content that might answer it. This retrieval step is the heart of RAG and it’s also the most important and complex part. But for now, think of it as a system that knows how to find the best bits of information related to the question.

Once we’ve pulled the relevant info from the knowledge base, we send it, along with the user’s question, to the _large language model_ (like ChatGPT). The LLM then “reads” the information and generates an answer. This is the _augmented generation_ step.

### 1\. Data Processing Enhancement:

In data processing enhancements, we use techniques for extracting information from documents, including text, tables, and figures, in a way that ensures relevant snippets are accurately identified and retrieved.

**Parsing Multi-modal Documents**

One common challenge is handling multi-modal content in source documents, such as charts, tables, or even videos like meeting recordings. There are two main approaches to address this issue. The first approach involves converting the multi-modal content into textual form. For example, Table-to-Text methods [2] translate tables into text, while other techniques convert visual content into textual or attribute-based descriptions. These descriptions are then processed by large language models. The second approach[3] utilizes multi-modal embedding techniques, which use the retrieved embeddings from multi-modal data as soft prompts for input.

**Optimizing Chunking**

Segmenting long texts into smaller chunks is a necessary operation. Larger text chunks can preserve more of the semantic coherence of the context, but they may also contain more noise within each chunk. Common chunking strategies include fixed size chunking, recursive chunking, sliding window chunking, paragraph-based chunking, and semantic chunking [4],[5][6]. Some methods are designed to identify the level of detail required by a query and select text chunks of appropriate granularity for retrieval. Alternatively, other methods process and refine the text into smaller segments that still maintain a high degree of information completeness. Additionally, there are approaches that use vision models to segment text based on the original document structure.

### **2\. Data Retrieval Enhancement:**

The integration of Information Retrieval (IR) techniques into RAG applications is seamless. The main steps involved in this process include establishing data indexes, processing queries, retrieving and matching, re-ranking, and evaluation. Indexing plays a crucial role in determining how the retrieval system operates, as it establishes mappings from search terms to text segments. There are three broad types of indexing methods: sparse, dense, and hybrid retrieval.

**Sparse Retrieval:** This was the initial indexing method widely adopted due to its simplicity. Techniques like TF-IDF and BM25 are used to identify the most representative keywords of each text segment based on their relative frequency. While these methods are still prevalent in many RAG projects, they have limitations in recognizing synonyms. To address this issue, similarity-based matching methods like KNN can be used. Another approach is to transform indices like keywords into predictions of the probabilities of query tokens for the corresponding text segment.

**Dense Retrieval:** This approach involves using pre-trained or fine-tuned text encoders to map texts to a dense vector space that aligns with query requirements. BERT-based encoders are commonly fine-tuned as dense retrievers on unsupervised data using methods such as**DPR[7], ANCE[8], SimCSE[9], and TAS-B**. Unsupervised contrastive learning can also be employed for fine-tuning. Feedback from language models can guide the training objectives of retrievers, enhancing their suitability for RAG systems. LLM-based dense retrieval, which leverages the powerful capabilities of large language models, has gained attention and exploration. Methods like LLM2vec and Llama2Vec have been developed to train unsupervised dense retrieval encoders based on pre-trained language models, resulting in significant improvements in retrieval task performance.

**Other Approaches:** Combining sparse retrieval and dense retrieval is an effective method to focus on the central theme of text segments and capture global features simultaneously. Some studies propose initially determining the knowledge domain needed to answer a query and then using dense retrieval to recall supplementary information within that domain. Various methods [11] have been explored to blend dense vector indexing with sparse encoder indexing, aiming to capture the semantic information of text blocks and enhance the precision of targeted paragraph retrieval. Additionally, researchers have enhanced the capabilities of language models by fine-tuning them for indexing and retrieving, effectively integrating these abilities directly into the models. This enables the models to autonomously generate data indices and text segments for each query.

### 3\. Aligning Queries with Document Segments

The purpose of this step is to align the query with document segments in external data to find the most suitable segment for answering the query. There are three main approaches to this alignment: traditional alignment, document domain alignment, and query domain alignment.

Three Types of Query-Document Alignment (Image by Author)

**Traditional Alignment** : In traditional alignment, both the document segments and the query are mapped into the same encoding space. For example, many dense retrieval architectures use specialized query encoders to achieve this alignment.

**Sparse Retrieval Alignment:** When a system like RAG uses sparse retrieval, it becomes necessary to extract keywords from the query for the search. Query rewriting techniques can be applied to improve search accuracy by addressing user terminological inaccuracies or vague descriptions, thereby enhancing the precision of the search results.

**Document Domain Alignment:** Document domain alignment involves generating synthetic answers first and then using these answers to retrieve relevant data. This approach solves the problem of queries and retrieved data not being in the same distribution space. An example of this approach is the work done on **HyDE[12]**.

**Query Domain Alignment:** Query domain alignment involves generating a set of synthetic questions for each atomic unit of text, mapping text segments into the query space, and retrieving the synthetic questions that are closest to the original query along with their corresponding text segments. This method ensures that the most relevant and contextually appropriate segments are selected to respond to the query. SlimPLM[13], for instance, uses a small proxy model to generate heuristic answers, which are then used to predict the knowledge needed to answer the question. This approach effectively aligns queries with the document space.

### 4\. Re-ranking and Correction

Once the top k text blocks are retrieved, RAG systems need to filter and reorder these segments. The ranking of these segments is usually based on the relevance scores provided by the retriever. However, some studies utilize specific metrics like perplexity or perplexity gain as ranking criteria. Another approach is to use language models (LLMs) to assess the credibility and usefulness of the retrieved text blocks. This can involve training a reward-driven contextual adapter to refine the output of the retriever. Additionally, researchers have explored pre-training small language models dedicated to fact verification. These models are used to filter out incorrect retrieved text chunks, thus improving the quality of the retrieved text.

**Recursive Retrieval or Iterative Retrieval:** Considering the inherent limitations of a single retrieval attempt in terms of accuracy, it is beneficial to perform multiple retrievals to gradually address any omissions. For example, Kim et al.[14] introduced a tree-like recursive retrieval method that incorporates pruning strategies. This method breaks down ambiguous questions into disambiguated ones, ultimately arriving at the closest correct answer. Similarly, SEATER[20] uses the k-means algorithm to construct a hierarchical tree structure of items to be retrieved and iteratively recalls nodes within the tree structure. These recursive or iterative retrieval strategies help refine the retrieval process and improve the accuracy of the answers.

### 5\. Enhancing Response Generation

Generating accurate and informative responses requires determining whether the retrieved information is sufficient or if additional external data is needed. It is also crucial to handle conflicts between the retrieved knowledge and the model’s internal prior knowledge. Supervised fine-tuning is an effective method to enhance the generation performance in RAG systems.

When dealing with irrelevant or erroneous information retrieved as context, pre-trained large language models can be easily misled, resulting in incorrect responses. However, studies have shown that by carefully designing training data for RAG systems and applying fine-tuning or pretraining techniques, this issue can be effectively mitigated. RAAT[15], for example, demonstrated through experimental analysis that the detrimental effects of irrelevant retrieval noise, relevant retrieval noise, and counterfactual retrieval noise on RAG models increase progressively. By incorporating these training processes, these methods enable the language model to internally recognize noisy contexts, leading to significant improvements in the quality of response generation even when faced with noisy retrievals.

## Implicit Fact Queries (L2)

Implicit fact queries involve dependencies in data that are not immediately obvious and may require common sense reasoning or basic logical deductions. The required information may be spread across multiple segments or require simple inferencing. These queries often require gathering and processing information from multiple documents, which may exceed the capacity of a single retrieval request. Therefore, the original query is decomposed into multiple retrieval operations, and the results are aggregated to provide a comprehensive answer. Common-sense reasoning is typically used in these queries, and they may include statistical queries, descriptive analysis queries, and basic aggregation queries.

Implicit Fact Queries (Image by Author)

Examples of L2 queries include operations like counting, comparison, trend analysis, and selective summarization. Multi-hop reasoning is often utilized to answer these queries effectively. In summary, L2 queries can be defined as follows:

For any query q and its corresponding answer a, a Q2 fact query is one where:

* There exists a set of explicit fact queries {q1, q2, …, qm} that can be directly retrieved from specific data segments within the dataset D. The relevant data segments necessary to answer each q1, q2, …, qm are identified as rD(qi).
* The union of these segments provides the information necessary to answer the original query q.
* A response generator θ, typically a prompted LLM inference, constructs the answer a to q by aggregating the responses {θ(rD(q1)), θ(rD(q2)), …, θ(rD(qm))} and applying common-sense reasoning to derive an answer that is not explicitly stated in the data.

This definition highlights the reliance of L2 queries on decomposing complex queries into simpler explicit fact queries (Q1) and aggregating their responses to generate the correct response to the original query.

Here are some examples of L2 queries:

* _How many experiments have sample sizes greater than 1000? (given a collection of experimental records)_
* _What are the top 3 most frequently mentioned symptoms? (given a collection of medical records)_
* _What’s the difference between the AI strategies of company X and company Y? (given news and articles about companies X and Y)_

### Challenges and Solutions

At the L2 level, queries require combining multiple facts through common-sense reasoning to arrive at an answer. The challenges faced in L2 queries include:

* **Adaptive retrieval volumes:** Different questions may require varying numbers of retrieved contexts, and the specific number of retrieved contexts depends on both the question and the dataset. Fixed retrieval volumes can result in either information noise or insufficient information.
* **Coordination between reasoning and retrieval:** Reasoning can guide the focus of retrieval, while insights gained from retrieved information can refine reasoning strategies. Addressing these complexities requires intelligent integration and selective harnessing of external data, leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Methods to address these challenges include iterative RAG, RAG on graph/tree, and RAG with SQL, among others.

### Iterative RAG

Implicit fact queries are similar to multi-hop RAG tasks, and iterative RAG methods are used to dynamically control multi-step processes to gather or correct information until the correct answer is achieved. There are two main approaches to iterative RAG:

* **Planning-based:** This approach involves generating a stepwise retrieval plan during the prior-retrieval stage or dynamically within the retrieval process. The plan refines the focus of each retrieval, efficiently guiding the iterative RAG system. Examples of planning-based methods include ReAct[16], IRCoT, and RAT[17], which use strategies like a Chain of Thought to guide the retrieval and decision-making process.
* **Information Gap Filling Based:** This approach focuses on filling in the gaps of existing knowledge through iterative retrieval and generation. Methods like ITRG[18] and FLARE[19] generate answers based on existing knowledge and continue to retrieve and generate for the unknown parts of the response in subsequent rounds.

### Graph/Tree Question Answering

To address implicit fact queries, synthesizing information from multiple references, graphs or trees are commonly used due to their ability to express relational structures among texts. There are two main approaches in this field:

**Traditional Knowledge Graph:** The traditional knowledge graph represents entities as nodes and relationships between entities as edges. Integrating knowledge graphs with large language models can enhance their understanding of acquired knowledge. This integration can be achieved through KG-enhanced LLMs, where KGs are used during pre-training and inference phases of LLMs, LLM-enhanced KGs, which employ LLMs for various KG tasks, and collaborative LLMs+KGs approaches, where both LLMs and KGs complement each other through bidirectional inference. Models like Rigel-KQGA, Think-on-Graph, KnowledgeNavigator, and R3 utilize these approaches to perform question-answering tasks.

**Data Chunk Graph/Tree:** Researchers have also explored using text or data chunks as nodes on graphs or trees. These chunks represent semantic units, and edges can represent relations between these units. Knowledge-Graph-Prompting constructs document graphs and extracts subgraphs to answer bridging, comparing, and structural questions. MoGG uses sentences as nodes and builds edges based on semantic similarity. RAPTOR and GraphRAG employ clustering algorithms to hierarchically cluster text blocks and summarize information within collapsed tree nodes. These methods capture high-level semantic relationships between text blocks and provide answers to implicit fact queries.

### Natural Language to SQL Queries

When dealing with structured data, converting natural language queries to SQL (NL2SQL) can be effective. Tools like Chat2DB translate user queries into database queries, allowing LLMs to retrieve information from structured databases. This integration of text-to-SQL tools with LLMs enhances their ability to generate accurate and contextually relevant responses. It expands the scope of LLM applications, enabling them to perform complex tasks that involve interaction with and interpretation of database content.

## Closing Thoughts

Before diving into building a tailored LLM application, it’s crucial for domain experts to really understand the task. This includes assessing how complex the queries are and choosing the right technology to address them. Injecting external knowledge into the model can make it more stable and easier to interpret, but this isn’t without challenges — limited context windows and the risk of losing key information along the way make careful planning a must. Picking the best approach to integrate data into an LLM involves knowing your data sources inside out and making smart decisions based on that understanding.

In real-world scenarios, things aren’t so simple — queries often come in all shapes and sizes. This means developers need to create a “routing pipeline,” a system that can intelligently switch between different methods to handle whatever comes its way. This approach works especially well for situations where data can be boiled down into shorter explanations, but it also tests the model’s ability to retrieve and make sense of the information.

The world of data-augmented LLMs is both exciting and challenging. To make the most of these powerful tools, it’s all about understanding the problem, knowing your data, and being strategic in your approach. By combining the right methods and crafting thoughtful solutions, we can tackle even the most complex problems.
