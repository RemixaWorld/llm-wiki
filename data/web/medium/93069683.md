## Understanding how LLMs work

To understand how important this is, we need to understand how LLMs work. But not how everyone tells you they work.

How they really work.

### The Universal Task Interface

When training a deep neural network, you must define the task you want the model to optimize.

In the LLM case, **that task is next-word prediction** ; a model receives a set of input words in the form of a text sequence, and it predicts the next token.

> From this point onwards, I’ll refer to tokens as ‘words’, but in reality, they can be words or subwords (similar to syllables).

That token is added to the sequence, and the entire sequence is driven through the model again, generating the next token, and so forth.

To train such a model, as with all neural networks today, we need to find a way to measure each model's prediction and use this signal (how good or bad the prediction was) to reduce the ‘error’ over time.

Luckily, there is plenty of text available on the Internet, which becomes the training data. Thus, to measure how well a model predicts, we simply need to hide the next word for the model and see what probability percentage, out of all possible words the LLM knows, it assigned to the correct word.

Consequently, the model learns to assign the highest probability possible to the correct word.

At this point, you may conclude that the model will always predict the most likely next word, which is the definition everyone gives regarding LLMs.

However, that is not true. These models don’t simply predict the plausibility of one word.

**They model distributions**.

### What do they actually predict?

As we want our model to be capable of modeling the entire written language and to be creative when required, this prediction isn’t just the word the model thinks is most suitable, **but we force it to assign a probability to every single token in its vocabulary.**

In other words (no pun intended), the model predicts not only one word but its entire vocabulary ranked by ‘next word plausibility.’ **And this subtle change to the definition changes everything.**

If we didn’t do this, the model would be entirely deterministic and, based on what we are asking to learn (maximize the likelihood that the next word is the actual word it saw during training) **it would essentially converge into becoming the literal embodiment of its training data**.

While there are reasons to want that, it turns the model into a boring bag of words that imitates Internet data, and that’s not intelligent or interesting. But by forcing it to model the probabilities of other words,**the model learns to generate alternative sequences to the original one that are semantically similar, but not necessarily equal**.

> In fact, if you want a mathematical definition of what a Generative model is (be that ChatGPT or MidJourney), they are multivariate parametric curve functions that find the optimal set of parameters that maximize the likelihood of observing the training data or sequences that are semantically similar to that of the original data.
>
> That was a mouthful, I know, but it’s the most rigorous definition I can declare.

But hold on a second. If the model defines the probability of not just one word but the thousands of words in its vocabulary, it could choose other words besides the most likely one, _right?_

Yes, and that’s exactly what it does. _But how?_ Well… it chooses one at random.

Wait, _what?_

### Parrot or not… they are stochastic

As the model has seen the entire Internet, the model also learns about grammar, synonyms, and so on. Thus, **it has implicitly learned that there are multiple ways of generating text with the same semantic meaning**.

Importantly, it also learns how some predictions matter more than others. For instance, if we ask in what year the Great London Fire was, albeit the model still assigns probabilities to other options, the probability distribution will be extremely skewed toward the token ‘1666’ (or should be).

On the other hand, if you ask it to write a poem, the model has much more ‘creative freedom’. It can combine words in different ways and still meet the user’s request.

Thus, depending on the certainty of the model, the distribution can be very skewed toward assigning extremely high probabilities to one or two words, while in other cases, it may be ‘flat,’ where dozens of words may be correct.

For example, if I tell you the sequence _‘the boy went to the’_ , the possible tokens that could follow that sequence if no additional context is provided are in the hundreds, at least.

As you can see below, the distribution assigns pretty high probabilities to 5 different words that, although some may be ‘more probable’ than others, are all semantically viable.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*CxE1GVTcxYXze-GDIOU3Sg.png)

But if we were to observe the distribution on the previous question on the Great London Fire, I can assure you that ‘1666’ would have a +90% probability, and the rest would be barely above 1%.

That said, we still haven’t answered how the model actually chooses the option. The most popular method is **top-p** , where the model chooses at random from a set of most likely tokens that, combined, reach a probability that surpasses the threshold.

For example, circling back to the Great London Fire question, if the threshold is 0.9 and the probability assigned to 1666 is 0.91, the model will have no choice but to choose that token.

But if, for whatever reason, the probability assigned to 1666 is 0.87, if the threshold is 0.9, the model will include as many tokens as necessary to reach 0.9 and then choose at random.

This is the worst possible situation because the chances of the model's producing an inaccurate result skyrocket. If the model has to fill in with options like ‘1987,’ with 1% likelihood, and ‘1573’ and ‘2010’ with 1% each too, even though ‘1666’ has 87% likelihood of being the correct choice, the model will still choose at random between the four.

> Suddenly, the chances the model shits the bed and outputs a wrong year are as high as 75%.

Tragedy. So, _what if there was a better way to prevent this?_ And that, my friend, is min-p.

## Min-P Sampling, A Performance Booster

In simple terms, Min-p truncates the distribution by setting a dynamic threshold dependent on the highest probability value.

That is, min-p’s final number results from a multiplication between two numbers:

* The base value, which you define as a hyperparameter
* The probability assigned to the most likely token

For example, in the image below (top), the most likely word has a probability of 0.47. With a base min-p of 0.1, the min-p threshold will be 0.047 (or 4.7%). In other words, **any word with a smaller likelihood than that one is automatically rejected**.

Thus, all except two are rejected. This is great because the model’s distribution was already clearly biased toward these two options, which means the best thing we can do is reject all the rest.

On the other hand, if we look at the second distribution, it is observably ‘flatter.’

Therefore, the model is more uncertain about what the next word should be, and thus, the min-p should be moderate in its rejection.

Luckily, as the min-p threshold is dynamic and dependent on the certainty of the model, the truncation threshold barely reaches 0.01 (1%), and thus, **many possible tokens remain available for sampling**.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*K1z77QI4B252QHBPQPFjFg.png)

> \- The top distribution is much more common when facing facts, maths, or events where the options are narrow
>
> \- The bottom distribution is much more common in tasks like creative writing, where we expect the model to be creative.

> Long story short, while top-p sampling chooses ‘one of the most likely,’ **min-p actually takes into consideration the structure of the distribution** , incentivizing the output of highly probable tokens when the result is obvious and without impacting the creativity of the model in situations where the distribution has a long tail.

As you may imagine, min-p is much more versatile and particularly ideal for fighting hallucinations.

If we look at our example of the London fire, a min-p of 0.2 would have automatically rejected all but ‘1666’ even if the model isn’t even sure. Still, min-p is extremely biased toward most likely tokens when one token is considerably more probable than the rest, saving the day.

## An Instant Classic?

This is the best type of research. Simple, to the point, and immediately useful.

When tested in popular benchmarks like GSM8k (maths) and GPQA (multiple-choice questions written by domain experts in biology, physics, and chemistry), **the model instantly achieved a 10/20% higher result just by changing top-p with min-p.**

Although the risk of hallucination will not go to zero (it probably never will unless we find a way to collapse distributions into one single token when required), **min-p is certainly a sampling method that will automatically reduce the hallucinations of your model.**

Nonetheless, I’m convinced this is an instant classic, one that could become as standard as AdamW and MLPs as optimizers and building blocks for neural networks, respectively.

In fact, **Google has already confirmed that it will soon offer support to min-p in its API (it’s already available in HuggingFace’s Transformers library).**

> That’s Min-p in a nutshell. Short, to the point, but unreasonably effective.
