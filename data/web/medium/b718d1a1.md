## How to build confidence in Generative AI by detecting everything except hallucinations.

![Searching for Facts, Amazon Titan Imange Model](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*kS9BzgqjM3BnzDAvaYY76g.png)

In this blog we explore a way to detect hallucinations by doing the opposite; we instead detect the factual support, or attribution, of every statement made by an large language model (LLM). While detecting hallucinations can be challenging, in many cases it is much easier to trace the factual source of each statement. By process of elimination statements which have no factual underpinning tend to be hallucinations. We also provide [sample code](<https://github.com/aws-samples/llm-based-advanced-summarization/blob/main/detect_attribution.ipynb>) to implement this idea using Amazon Bedrock. Just want to see it in action? Skip down to the section of this blog titled “So how does it work?”

## So what is a hallucination?

One of the biggest challenges with all LLM’s is their ability to confidently and convincingly make up completely wrong information. This can cause high profile problems, such as [the attorney in May 2023](<https://www.legaldive.com/news/chatgpt-fake-legal-cases-generative-ai-hallucinations/651557/>) who submitted caselaw that was fabricated by an LLM. It can also cause loss of user trust when a human receives an answer that is clearly false or fabricated. There are many different reasons why an LLM might make something up, including poor prompt engineering, lack of adequate data in the prompt, or perhaps simply low quality models. While there are a number of methods which attempt to detect hallucinations, they each have their own limitations and hallucinations continue to be a problem.

Many different efforts have been made to reduce the amount of hallucinations produced by LLM’s. One of the most effective methods is Retrieval Augmented Generation, or RAG. RAG is the process of looking at a user’s request and then adding data to the prompt which is relevant to the request, then finally sending the extra data and original request together to the LLM. Ideally, this means that all of the information that the LLM needs to respond to the user’s request is now contained inside the prompt to the LLM. For example, consider the question:

> _What is the weather today?_

Without RAG, the LLM has no way to know the answer which may lead to a guess or hallucination. However, a RAG enabled system would look at the same question and add data to it like this:

> _Consider this information: Today’s weather is sunny. Use that to answer this question: What is the weather today?_

With this RAG augmented prompt, our LLM is able to give the correct answer, and therefore RAG is a powerful tool for reducing hallucinations. Because RAG has proven an effective method to control the output of an LLM, it is now often considered a hallucination when the answer is not supported by the data in the prompt, **even in the case** **where the answer is correct**. For example, even if the LLM correctly answered the above question with no context and said: “Today’s weather is sunny.”, we would still consider this a hallucination because it is not factually supported by the information in the prompt. For the remainder of this blog, **we consider all statements not supported by the prompt to be hallucinations** , even if they are factually true. This is often a helpful viewpoint, because it allows you to control and limit the information shared by an LLM by controlling what gets injected at the RAG step.

## Why should we add attributions?

In a legal brief submitted to the court, a lawyer is required to cite her sources. She can not simply claim that the law says “Congress has the power to collect taxes,” but instead must make that claim while citing the source, “Constitution of the United States. Article 1, Section 8, Clause 1”. In this way, the lawyer provides attribution and proves that the claim is not a hallucination. We can follow this same pattern with LLM’s, and ask them to prove to us that each claim they make is not a hallucination. In turn, when an LLM is unable to provide evidence to support a claim, we can have a high suspicion that the claim may be a hallucination, and then develop a separate process for handling such claims with higher scrutiny.

Detecting attribution allows us to build trust and explainability with our users by giving them direct evidence that an LLM’s claims are true. In many current RAG systems, including Amazon’s own managed RAG Knowledge Base product, users are already shown the RAG documents as a reference when a response is generated. However, in many cases the context added by a RAG system can be lengthy, and it can be challenging to understand where in the RAG context the LLM thinks it found support for its output. By using the system of attribution proposed in this blog, we are able to go a level deeper and give direct, sentence level reference verification. This makes it quick and easy for a user to see the connections and trust the result.

## If something has no attributions, is it a hallucination?

Not necessarily. While sentences with no bases in the source documents deserve a higher level of scrutiny, there are some cases where helpful, no hallucinatory sentences are created. A simple example of this are thing like an introductory or transition statement. For example, when asking an LLM for a summary, the first sentence in the response might be “Here is the summary you asked for.” This is not a hallucination, but merely helps with the natural sounding flow or understanding of the actual content. If we just want to show users attributions we can stop here, but if we also want to detect hallucinations, we can also ask the LLM to identify statements like this. In practice, we ask the LLM to score each sentence on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates a helper sentence that requires no support and makes no factual claims, and 100 is a factual claim that requires support from the source document. Finally, if a sentence scores high on requiring factual support, and also has no attributions found, we can have a high level of confidence that we have identified a hallucination.

## So how does it work?

Once we have an input (such as raw data to be summarized or results from RAG) and the output from the LLM, we make a second call to the LLM asking it to find the attributions. Essentially we ask the LLM to go line by line and prove the output using the input. We start by adding numbering the sentences in both the input and output, and then construct a prompt that asks the LLM to list all the line numbers in the input that support each line in the output. Here is an example so that you can see it in action, taken from the [CNN news dataset](<https://huggingface.co/datasets/cnn_dailymail>).
Input: (A short news article from CNN)

> Filipinos are being warned to be on guard for flash floods and landslides as tropical storm Maysak approached the Asian island nation Saturday. Just a few days ago, Maysak gained super typhoon status thanks to its sustained 150 mph winds. It has since lost a lot of steam as it has spun west in the Pacific Ocean. It’s now classified as a tropical storm, according to the Philippine national weather service, which calls it a different name, Chedeng. It boasts steady winds of more than 70 mph (115 kph) and gusts up to 90 mph as of 5 p.m. (5 a.m. ET) Saturday. Still, that doesn’t mean Maysak won’t pack a wallop. Authorities took preemptive steps to keep people safe such as barring outdoor activities like swimming, surfing, diving and boating in some locales, as well as a number of precautionary evacuations. Gabriel Llave, a disaster official, told PNA that tourists who arrive Saturday in and around the coastal town of Aurora “will not be accepted by the owners of hotels, resorts, inns and the like … and will be advised to return to their respective places.” Aldczar Aurelio, a meteorologist with the Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration (PAGASA), said the storm was centered 200 miles southwest of Aurora province as of 5 p.m. (5 a.m. ET) and heading west at a 12.5 mph clip. It’s expected to make landfall Sunday morning on the southeastern coast of Isabela province and be out of the Philippines by Monday. Ahead of the storm. Isabela Gov. Faustino Dry III warned Saturday that residents should act as if this will be “no ordinary typhoon.” Dry told PNA, “We do not know what the impact will be once it will make landfall.”

Output: (In this case, the output is the human written highlights of the news article. I have also added the sentence “I love to eat donuts for breakfast.” at the end to introduce a hallucination.)

> Once a super typhoon, Maysak is now a tropical storm with 70 mph winds . It could still cause flooding, landslides and other problems in the Philippines . I love to eat donuts for breakfast.

Next we add line numbers, which makes it harder for a human to read, but it is still simple for an LLM to understand. Here I’ve used XML tags, which work very well with Claude:
Input with line numbers:

> <line number=1>(CNN)Filipinos are being warned to be on guard for flash floods and landslides as tropical storm Maysak approached the Asian island nation Saturday.</line><line number=2>Just a few days ago, Maysak gained super typhoon status thanks to its sustained 150 mph winds.</line><line number=3>It has since lost a lot of steam as it has spun west in the Pacific Ocean.</line><line number=4>It\’s now classified as a tropical storm, according to the Philippine national weather service, which calls it a different name, Chedeng.</line><line number=5>It boasts steady winds of more than 70 mph (115 kph) and gusts up to 90 mph as of 5 p.m. (5 a.m.</line><line number=6>ET) Saturday.</line><line number=7>Still, that doesn\’t mean Maysak won\’t pack a wallop.</line>…

Output with line numbers:

> <line number=1>Once a super typhoon, Maysak is now a tropical storm with 70 mph winds .</line><line number=2>It could still cause flooding, landslides and other problems in the Philippines .</line><line number=3>I love to eat donuts for breakfast.</line>

Finally, we package this all up in a prompt to Claude using Amazon Bedrock:

>

Human: You are given a reference source, and a statement based on that source. Your job is to find every single line in the reference which helps to support the claims made in the statement.
> Here is the source and statement:
> <source>
> {{INPUT}}
> </source>
> <statement>
> {{OUTPUT}}
> </statement>
> For each line in the statement, please list all the line numbers from the source, if any, which support that line in the statement.
> Your response should be in JSON format, with a key for each line number in the statement, and values which are the supporting line numbers from the source.
>
Assistant: Here is what you asked for:

And the result we get looks like this:

```json { "1": [2, 5], "2": [7, 8, 9, 12, 13], "3": []} ```

That’s not quite human readable, so we use a bit of python to swap out the line numbers for the original text, and we get this:

> Output line 1:
> Once a super typhoon, Maysak is now a tropical storm with 70 mph winds .
> Supporting facts:
>  2: Just a few days ago, Maysak gained super typhoon status thanks to its sustained 150 mph winds.
>  5: It boasts steady winds of more than 70 mph (115 kph) and gusts up to 90 mph as of 5 p.m. (5 a.m.
>
> Output line 2:
> It could still cause flooding, landslides and other problems in the Philippines .
> Supporting facts:
>  7: Still, that doesn’t mean Maysak won’t pack a wallop.
>  8: Authorities took preemptive steps to keep people safe such as barring outdoor activities like swimming, surfing, diving and boating in some locales, as well as a number of precautionary evacuations.
>  9: Gabriel Llave, a disaster official, told PNA that tourists who arrive Saturday in and around the coastal town of Aurora “will not be accepted by the owners of hotels, resorts, inns and the like … and will be advised to return to their respective places.”
>  12: It’s expected to make landfall Sunday morning on the southeastern coast of Isabela province and be out of the Philippines by Monday.
>  13: Ahead of the storm, Isabela Gov Faustino Dry III warned Saturday that residents should act as if this will be “no ordinary typhoon.”
>
> Output line 3:
> I love to eat donuts for breakfast.
> Supporting facts:
>  No supporting facts found.

As discussed in the previous section, here we have identified the attributions for each line, but we still don’t know for sure if those with no attributions, like line 3 above, are actually hallucinations. This is a good place to stop if you are not too worried about hallucinations, and just want to show your users the factual basis of each statement, and let them draw their own conclusions. However, if we also want to detect hallucinations, we can also ask the LLM to identify statements like this. We ask the LLM to score each sentence on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates a helper sentence that requires no support and makes no factual claims, and 100 is a factual claim that requires support from the source document. If a sentence scores high on requiring factual support, and also has no attributions found, we can have a high level of confidence that we have identified a hallucination. Here is what the prompt looks like:

>

Human: You will be given a statement. It may contain false content, and therefore is being reviewed in a two step process.
> The first step is to identify all lines that make factual claims, and the second step is to find sources that support those claims.
> You are a detail oriented expert in charge of the first step of this process.
> You job is to look at each line in the statement, and score how likely it is to require proof from trustworthy sources.
> For example, the sentence “I have a hat.” makes a factual claim, and should score high on the need for support, while the sentence “Here is a summary.” is a helper sentence, and therefore does not need external support and should score lower.
> Here is the statement:
> <statement>
> {{TEXT}}
> </statement>
> For each line in the statement, please score each line on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates a helper sentence that requires no support and makes no factual claims, and 100 is a factual claim that requires support from trustworthy sources.
> Your response should be in JSON format, with a key for each line number in the statement, and values which are the score for that statement.
>
Assistant: Here is what you asked for:

This prompt, when {{TEXT}} is replaced with our example above, produces a JSON similar to the last one. With a bit of python, we can integrate that score into the previous output, and flag any line which has no attribution and a score under 50 as a likely hallucination. Here is the result:

> Output line 1: (Facts required score: 0)
> Here are some highlights.
> Supporting facts:
>  No supporting facts found.
>
> Output line 2: (Facts required score: 100)
> Once a super typhoon, Maysak is now a tropical storm with 70 mph winds .
> Supporting facts:
>  2: Just a few days ago, Maysak gained super typhoon status thanks to its sustained 150 mph winds.
>  5: It boasts steady winds of more than 70 mph (115 kph) and gusts up to 90 mph as of 5 p.m. (5 a.m.
>
> Output line 3: (Facts required score: 100)
> It could still cause flooding, landslides and other problems in the Philippines .
> Supporting facts:
>  7: Still, that doesn’t mean Maysak won’t pack a wallop.
>  8: Authorities took preemptive steps to keep people safe such as barring outdoor activities like swimming, surfing, diving and boating in some locales, as well as a number of precautionary evacuations.
>  9: Gabriel Llave, a disaster official, told PNA that tourists who arrive Saturday in and around the coastal town of Aurora “will not be accepted by the owners of hotels, resorts, inns and the like … and will be advised to return to their respective places.”
>
> Output line 4: (Facts required score: 100)
> I love to eat donuts for breakfast.
> Supporting facts:
>  No supporting facts found. Likely hallucination detected!

You can see that the function is able to point out the correct parts of the source document to support each statement. It is also able to determine that the introduction statement is fine, but the final statement should have had factual support but does not, so the system flags it as a hallucination.

## This seems nice. What’s the catch?

The idea and sample code of this post are just a starting point. For any individual use case, it will likely need to be tweaked to provide appropriate results. Furthermore, this method is primarily focused on attributions, and will not detect all kinds of hallucinations. For example, consider an output that says “I had coffee and donuts for breakfast.” This is a factual statement, so it will get a high score on the need for attribution. However, if the source document only contains the statement “I had coffee for breakfast” this will correctly be cited as a factual source for the output statement. This method does not look at the sub-sentence level, and find that the donut portion of the statement is a hallucination. However, it can still build trust with the users, because a human can glance at the references and determine the truth. If hallucination detection is critical at this level, then the output statement and and source attributions can be further processed to detect hallucinations more precisely. Also note that this method requires the supporting facts to be in the input prompt, and will not work as a method for understanding when an LLM is making a statement based on its training data.

## Conclusion and what’s next

In this blog post, we explored the idea of asking an LLM to cite its sources, and provide attribution to every claim it makes. This allows us to build trust with our users by building in explainability, and allows us to detect some forms of hallucinations by looking for statements that have no supporting facts but shld. It still may be the case that misinformation sneaks in if an LLM misinterprets a source document, but we combat this by listing attributions, and allowing humans to judge for them selves if an output is trustworthy. As a next step, we recommend trying out [the code](<https://github.com/aws-samples/llm-based-advanced-summarization/blob/main/detect_attribution.ipynb>) with your own data, and exploring how tweaking the prompt can allow you to provide a deeper level of confidence to your own users. You may also want to check out our [recent blog post on summarization methods](</@flux07/advanced-generative-summarization-techniques-939605601fba>), which can then be further verified with the technique described here.
