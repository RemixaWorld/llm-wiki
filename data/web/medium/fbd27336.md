Fusing Imitation and Mental Models in Transformers for Rapid Task Mastery

![Photo by Growtika on Unsplash](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*0KqHK8ujGaVnVKMB)

I recently reviewed _“Distilling Reinforcement Learning Algorithms for In-Context Model-Based Planning (DICP)”_ by Jaehyeon Son, Soochan Lee, and Gunhee Kim (OpenReview ID: BfUugGfBE5). The paper proposes a practical idea: train a single Transformer to reproduce an _RL_ agent’s learning trajectory and perform look-ahead planning within the same model.

Traditional _RL_ — think _PPO_ or _DQN_ — needs millions of trials to solve a task. That’s fine in simulation, but not when you want a warehouse robot or trading bot to adapt on the fly. You simply can’t afford endless trial and error.

Algorithm distillation helps by feeding a Transformer the agent’s entire training log so it can imitate moves in-context. But blind imitation comes with baggage: the model also copies slow updates and timid exploration.

_DICP_ fixes that by giving the Transformer an internal “mental model” of the environment. Before choosing an action, it rolls out a few possible futures in its head and picks the best. It’s like copying your coach’s strategy while adding your own foresight.

I’ll unpack _DICP_ in plain English. I’ll cover how it learns dynamics, blends imitation with planning, and outperforms pure _RL_ or distillation alone. Along the way, I’ll share real-world examples — robotics, finance, you name it — and suggest simple tweaks to make in-context planning even more robust.

## DICP and Reinforcement Learning

When a robot first learns to navigate a maze with a traditional reinforcement learning (_RL_) algorithm, it must explore step by step, often bumping into walls (“go left, hit a wall, go back, go right…”).

This trial-and-error process takes a lot of time and effort. Even if we use a typical distillation approach — where a Transformer model simply mimics the RL robot — the result is the same: the Transformer robot still moves slowly and cautiously, trying left then right, and wastes many steps.

The _DICP_ method is different. Before moving, the robot “looks” at the maze and imagines a few possible paths in its “mind.” For example:

* Path 1: left → left → up (hits a wall)
* Path 2: right → right → up (reaches the goal) ✅

Since Path 2 works, the robot chooses it immediately, greatly reducing the number of steps it actually needs to take.

## How DICP Plans Its Next Move

Pure distillation hands a Transformer both the smart moves and the stale habits of its _RL_ teacher — slow policy tweaks, timid exploration, even the same blind spots. That makes it fast to adapt but still prone to repeating suboptimal decisions.

_DICP_ takes distillation one step further by teaching the Transformer a lightweight “world model” alongside imitation. Now, before each action, the model imagines a handful of possible outcomes and picks the most promising path — like a chess player glancing ahead rather than just copying old games.

Think of a delivery drone navigating a new city block. A distilled-only model might retrace every cautious detour it saw before. _DICP_ , however, uses its internal map to simulate three quick routes — avoiding heavy traffic or no-fly zones — and then selects the safest, most direct option without crashing into a lamppost.

Here’s a flowchart of _DICP’s_ decision loop:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*YXRGI-5bv-ZeNGdtfxY15w.png)

In benchmark tests like the Darkroom maze and robotic grasping in Meta-World, _DICP_ slashed required interactions — down to roughly a quarter or a third of what classic _RL_ needs — while matching or exceeding success rates. That means huge savings in training time and resources.

Beyond robotics, you can plug _DICP_ into any scenario where fast, reliable adaptation matters: automated trading systems spotting market anomalies, smart grids adjusting to load spikes, or even personalized tutoring software tailoring lessons on the fly.

## Demystifying DICP: From Core Equations to a Worked Example

_DICP_ marries _RL’_ s tried-and-true policies with on-the-fly mental simulations, so you get:

* fewer real-world trials
* instant adaptation without repeating past blunders
* one Transformer that imitates and plans — no extra environment model needed

_DICP_ teaches a Transformer to learn from history and imagine its next moves in its head — tackling new tasks smarter and with way less effort.

Here’s the algorithm’s flowchart:

``` Generate reinforcement-learning trajectory data (rollouts from an RL algorithm) │ ▼Define a custom Transformer architecture: • Trajectory encoder • Dynamics-prediction module (environment simulator) • Internal planning mechanism (rollout simulation) • Action-decision head │ ▼Train the new Transformer on the RL trajectories │ ▼Evaluate via environment interaction (test on new tasks) ```

Below is how the internal Transformer leverages reinforcement learning:

```sql (Transformer Internal Structure)┌───────────────────────────────────┐│ Transformer ││ ││ Past experience (RL trajectories)││ │ ││ ▼ ││ Imitate RL policy behavior ││ │ ││ ▼ ││ Internally “imagine” next-step ││ outcomes ││ │ ││ ▼ ││ Compare possible actions (plan) ││ │ ││ ▼ ││ Select the optimal action │└───────────────────────────────────┘ │ ▼ Execute action in the real world ```

We need a single summary for planning. Two common choices:

This code shows (i) where the Transformer is built, (ii) how the observation / action shapes needed by the encoder are registered, and (iii) how every training batch is ultimately fed through the encoder when `model(batch)` is called.

**How Trajectory Encoding Works Efficiently?**

1. Factorized tokens let the model treat “−1 reward after Right” differently from “−1 after Left”.
2. Attention can skip over low‑value actions and focus on meaningful cause‑and‑effect links (e.g., _two steps ago we hit a wall_).
3. No extra training loop at test time — the Transformer simply ingests fresh context and re‑computes _h_t_ ​.

Once _h_t_ ​ is ready, the Policy‑Imitation and Dynamics heads (Steps 2–3) can operate with the _full_ history distilled into one dense vector.

### 2\. Policy Imitation Head

The main task is to take the context vector _h_t_ ​ from the encoder and turn it into a probability distribution over actions. Below is the full math — followed by a concrete maze read‑out.

We use a single linear layer plus _soft‑max_ :

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:568/1*rBXp8P3YLE2QuzXGgKn67g.png)

Dimensions:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:621/1*RI5Y0sFkzKAS4oJ5mpfNkA.png)

So the policy head is just the ‘ _logit_ ’, followed by a _soft-max_ to get probabilities over those four moves.

**Training Loss**

A supervised cross‑entropy with behavior‑cloned targets:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:567/1*qu8Zo74qk0eOJkfMySx2PA.png)

Then, the model learns to mimic the demonstrator’s next move.

Here are the codes that function policy-imitation head:

The _ad.py_ implements the **Action-Decision Transformer** : a single-level model that turns an encoded trajectory into a continuous action prediction (plus loss) at each step.

``` # setupif config['learn_var']: self.pred_actions = nn.Linear(config['tf_n_embd'], 2 * config['dim_actions']) self.loss_fn_gaussian = nn.GaussianNLLLoss(full=True, reduction='mean')else: self.pred_actions = nn.Linear(config['tf_n_embd'], config['dim_actions'])# forward pass predicted_actions = self.pred_actions(transformer_output[:, self.n_transit-1])if self.config['learn_var']: dist = predicted_actions mean = dist[:, :self.config['dim_actions']] var = torch.exp(dist[:, self.config['dim_actions']:]) result['loss_action'] = self.loss_fn_gaussian(mean, target_actions, var)else: result['loss_action'] = self.loss_fn(predicted_actions, target_actions) ```

The _idt.py_ implements the **Inverse-Dynamics Transformer** — a hierarchical model that first predicts latent high-level decisions-to-go, then decodes them into low-level continuous actions with a corresponding imitation loss.

``` # setupif config['learn_var']: self.pred_action = nn.Linear(config['tf_n_embd'], 2 * config['dim_actions'])else: self.pred_action = nn.Linear(config['tf_n_embd'], config['dim_actions'])# forward passpred_actions = self.pred_action(transformer_output[:, :, :, 1])# loss in top-level IDTif self.config['learn_var']: mean = pred_actions[..., :self.config['dim_actions']] var = torch.exp(pred_actions[..., self.config['dim_actions']:]) output['loss_action'] = self.loss_fn_gaussian(mean, actions, var)else: output['loss_action'] = self.loss_fn(pred_actions, actions) ```

With this head in place, the _DICP_ model always has a first‑cut guess for the next action, even before doing any look‑ahead planning.

## 4\. In‑Context Roll‑out Planner

This part is the “mental simulator” that turns one‑step predictions into a short look‑ahead search — _inside the Transformer, no environment calls_. We will derive every formula, then walk through a two‑step maze rollout with real numbers.

**Branch Generation Loop**

For each branch _k ∈ {1..K}_ :

1. **Seed action** _a_{t+1}^k=A_t[k]_
2. **Autoregressive unrolling** for _ℓ = 1..L_ :

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:490/1*2fAUvN6ysUgNjB3UMy4T4Q.png)

where _h(s^)_ is the Transformer embedding of the _imagined_ state.

We can repeatedly imagine the next state/reward by the Dynamics heads, feed that imagined state back through the encoder, and greedily pick the next action using the policy head. Greedy choice is common, but a _soft‑max_ sample works too.

**Return Estimation**

For each branch _k_ :

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:259/1*iZfFyejGhgK0hBCSDEEM8A.png)

In matrix form for batched roll‑outs:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:607/1*Qt5Q-x3fz5oMBFJgKWZOng.png)

**Action Selection Rule**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:466/1*P718OvngD9fBYXyh7vPfWw.png)

Here, only the **first** action is executed; the rest of the branch is discarded.

**Example: Maze Walk‑Through**

Settings _:_ _K=3, L=2, γ=0.99_.
Candidate pool: _Right, Up, Left_.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*68fLKi2f6sAeNLVtjt8mkg.png)

**Time Complexity**

* Each branch uses _L_ calls to state & reward heads and _L_ encodings → _O(KL)_.
* In practice _K=5, L=5_ is plenty; latency fits <5 ms on a single _GPU_.

**Why This Beats Plain Model‑Free RL?**

Since one forward pass now gives:

* A policy prior, **plus**
* A mini‑ _MPC_ (model predictive control) plan out to depth _L_.

That slashes real‑world sample use: instead of experiencing _50_ bad moves, _DICP_ can _imagine_ them and steer clear.

So the In‑Context Roll‑out Planner turns the Transformer into a tiny, differentiable simulator — letting the agent “dream” several futures before its next step.

Here are codes codes that realizes the _in-context roll-out planner_ (“mental simulator”):

1 _ad.py_ (Action-Decision Transformer):

```python # ---------------- in class AD ----------------def greedy_search(self, x, sample_size=5): batch_size = x.size(0) # one forward pass to get mean / std of action distribution output = self.transformer(x, max_seq_length=self.max_seq_length, dtype="fp32") if self.config['learn_var']: dist_actions = self.pred_actions(output[:, -1]) # W_a · h_t mean_actions = dist_actions[:, :self.config['dim_actions']] std_actions = torch.exp(dist_actions[:, self.config['dim_actions']:] / 2) else: mean_actions = self.pred_actions(output[:, -1]) # deterministic head std_actions = torch.ones_like(mean_actions) # sample K-1 extra candidate actions around the mean mean_actions = mean_actions.unsqueeze(1) # (b,1,a) std_actions = std_actions.unsqueeze(1) # (b,1,a) sampled = torch.randn(batch_size, sample_size-1, self.config['dim_actions'], device=self.device) * std_actions + mean_actions sampled, _ = pack([mean_actions, sampled], 'b * a') # include mean as 1st sample # append the candidate action tokens to the context embed_act = self.embed_query_action(sampled) # (b,k,h) embed_act = embed_act.unsqueeze(2) # (b,k,1,h) x_rep = repeat(x, 'b n h -> b k n h', k=sample_size) # tile context x_cat, _ = pack([x_rep, embed_act], 'b k * h') # imagine one-step roll-out with same Transformer imagined = self.transformer(rearrange(x_cat, 'b k n h -> (b k) n h'), max_seq_length=self.max_seq_length, dtype="fp32") imagined = rearrange(imagined, '(b k) n h -> b k n h', k=sample_size) # predict immediate reward for each candidate roll-out rewards = self.pred_rewards(imagined[:, :, -1]).clip(0, 10) # (b,k,1) rewards = rewards.squeeze(-1) # (b,k) # pick arg-max reward ⇒ best next-action _, idx = rewards.sort(dim=-1, descending=True) best = torch.gather(sampled, 1, repeat(idx, 'b k -> b k a', a=sampled.size(2))) # (b,k,a) return best[:, 0, 0] # (b,a) ```

2 _idt.py_ (Inverse-Dynamics Transformer, low-level planner):

```python # ------------ in class DecisionsToGo ------------def greedy_search(self, x, z_embed, sample_size): # run transformer to get action distribution output = self.transformer(x, max_seq_length=4*self.config['low_per_high'], dtype='fp32') if self.config['learn_var']: dist_actions = self.pred_action(output[:, -1]) mean_actions = dist_actions[:, :self.config['dim_actions']] std_actions = torch.exp(dist_actions[:, self.config['dim_actions']:] / 2) else: mean_actions = self.pred_action(output[:, -1]) std_actions = torch.ones_like(mean_actions) # sample candidate actions mean_actions = mean_actions.unsqueeze(1) # (b,1,a) std_actions = std_actions.unsqueeze(1) sampled = torch.randn(x.size(0), sample_size-1, self.config['dim_actions'], device=x.device) * std_actions + mean_actions sampled, _ = pack([mean_actions, sampled], 'b * a') # append action tokens & imagine future with transformer embed_act = self.embed_action(sampled) # (b,k,h) embed_act = embed_act.unsqueeze(2) x_rep = repeat(x, 'b n h -> b k n h', k=sample_size) x_cat, _ = pack([x_rep, embed_act], 'b k * h') imagined = self.transformer(rearrange(x_cat, 'b k n h -> (b k) n h'), max_seq_length=4*self.config['low_per_high'], dtype="fp32") imagined = rearrange(imagined, '(b k) n h -> b k n h', k=sample_size) # reward prediction & best beam rewards = self.pred_reward(imagined[:, :, -1]).clip(0, 10) # (b,k,1) rewards = rewards.squeeze(-1) # (b,k) _, idx = rewards.sort(dim=-1, descending=True) best = torch.gather(sampled, 1, repeat(idx, 'b k -> b k a', a=sampled.size(2))) return best[:, 0, 0] ```

The method _greedy_search_ method in both files plays the similar role:

1. takes the current hidden tokens _h_{0:T}_ ;
2. samples or perturbs candidate next-actions;
3. appends those actions as extra tokens;
4. runs the **same Transformer** forward to _imagine_ future tokens;
5. uses the learned reward head to score each imagined roll-out;
6. returns the best action (beam 1).

**(Offline) Training Signal**

If training data are collected on-the-fly, the new pair supplies fresh labels:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:445/1*xGvZNOfwy1YqCkDmvV-iLg.png)

These losses are averaged into the replay buffer for periodic fine-tuning, though in classic _DICP_ the main policy is fixed and only the context grows.

**Outputs Passed to the Next Cycle**

1. Updated state _s_{t+1}_
2. Reward _r_{t+1}_
3. Done flag (terminates episode if _true_)
4. Extended trajectory _τ_{0:t+1}_

These feed directly back into Step 1 (Encoding).
If _done = true_ , the encoder is reset and a fresh episode starts.

**Why the Action-Execution Module Excels?**

**Reality check:** The planner’s “dream” predictions may be off; only real feedback tells the agent that a wall is actually unpassable or that a reward is lower than expected.

**Low‑latency control:** Because the costly look‑ahead ran beforehand, Action Execution is just one environment call — fast enough for robotic closed‑loop control.

Below are the codes that turn the model’s final latent
vector into an executable action — the _Action-Execution_.

_ad.py_ (Action-Decision Transformer):

```python # Excerpt from class AD# This method is called by evaluate_in_context to obtain an env actiondef act(self, obs, act_prev, rew_prev, deterministic=False): """Return a low-level continuous action for the environment.""" with torch.no_grad(): tokens = self.encoder(obs, act_prev, rew_prev) # trajectory → h_t logits = self.pred_actions(tokens[:, -1]) # policy head if self.config['learn_var']: # Gaussian head μ = logits[:, :self.config['dim_actions']] σ = logits[:, self.config['dim_actions':]].exp() return μ if deterministic else μ + σ * torch.randn_like(μ) else: # deterministic head return logits if deterministic else \ logits + self.action_noise*torch.randn_like(logits) ```

_idt.py_ (Inverse-Dynamics Transformer)

```python # Excerpt from class DecisionsToGo (low-level controller)def act(self, context_tokens, deterministic=False): """Low-level action after high-level latents are fixed.""" with torch.no_grad(): h = self.transformer(context_tokens)[:, -1] # latest hidden logits = self.pred_action(h) # policy head if self.config['learn_var']: μ = logits[..., :self.config['dim_actions']] logσ2 = logits[..., self.config['dim_actions']:] return μ if deterministic else \ μ + torch.exp(0.5*logσ2) * torch.randn_like(μ) else: return logits # (deterministic) ```

_evaluate.py / train.py_ – where the action is executed:

``` # inside evaluate_in_context or the eval block of train.pywith torch.no_grad(): action = model.act(obs, act_prev, rew_prev, deterministic=True)next_obs, reward, done, _ = env.step(action.cpu().numpy()) ```

* Action-Execution is defined to _act_ (_AD_) and _DecisionsToGo.act_ (_IDT_).
* Both take the encoder/roll-out output _h_t_ , run the single linear policy head, add optional exploration noise, and return a continuous control vector consumed by the gym-like environment.

## DICP vs. DeepSeek: Same Bones, Different Brains

I recently dug into _Distilling RL Algorithms for In-Context Planning_ (_DICP_) and was struck by how much its loop looks like the one powering _DeepSeek_ ’s game bots, even though one targets robotics and the other video games. Both lean on the same universal idea — **_RL_ signals + a sequence model** — which is quickly becoming the default recipe for any agent that must learn from streams instead of static data.

### **Shared skeleton**

Both systems run the loop:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:532/1*4AdPDWEe65PyP03WA4oRLw.png)

They encode the full history _τ_{0:t}_ ​ into a vector _h_t_ ​, use heads to predict one-step dynamics, roll out _K ⁣× ⁣L_ futures, and pick:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:397/1*KBpoo3k3g9NQrlWJzLIwTw.png)

A drone, a game hero, or a stock bot — each follows that blueprint.

### **Where They Split**

_DICP_ uses classic multi-head attention; _DeepSeek_ replaces it with a state-space backbone (_Mamba / MLA_) and layers _GRPO_ actor-critic gradients on top.

DeepSeek scans ten-times longer logs at _O(Ld)_ cost, but has no attention heat-maps and needs extra tuning for tricky credit assignment.

### **Can We Swap _Mamba_ into _DICP_?**

Yes — almost like changing a Lego block.

_DICP_ has three parts: encoder → policy head → dynamics head.
A standard version uses a Transformer encoder. If you unplug that encoder and snap in _Mamba_ (a state-space–style model), the policy logic, the rollout math, and the loss functions stay exactly the same. Nothing else changes.

Imagine a warehouse robot that must process a _2000_ -step log of barcode scans and shelf moves:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Uur0zisj8_fZDrESphQtQg.png)

**Good:** Mamba keeps latency low and memory tiny — great for edge devices or smart meters that stream long sequences every second.

**Not-so-good:** You lose the clear “attention heat-map” that helps explain why a Transformer picked action A over action B, and accuracy can dip on tasks that truly need every token to talk to every other token.

**Quick rule-of-thumb:**

Pick _Mamba + DICP_ when sequences are huge or hardware is tight.
_Example:_ an energy sensor sending 10000 readings per day to a micro-controller.

Stay with Transformer +_DICP_ when you care about saliency plots, have mid-length data, or want to plug into the rich Transformer RL libraries you already use.

Different encoder blocks, same RL backbone — that reusable pattern is the main story here, no matter which sequence model you bolt on.
