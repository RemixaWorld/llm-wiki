Imagine trying to solve a puzzle with 400,000 pieces, where most pieces have no picture on the box, many are written in ancient scripts that are difficult to read, and the pieces are scattered across libraries on different continents. This is the reality facing scholars of the Cairo Genizah, one of history’s most significant documentary archives. This series of articles examines how multi-modal deep learning might serve as a research tool to support scholars in transcribing medieval texts, connecting related fragments, and searching this vast collection more effectively, while examining the underlying principles of cross-modal representation learning.

Multi-modal deep learning (MMDL) is a rapidly expanding area of machine learning aimed at creating models that can ingest multiple modalities of data such as text, images, audio, video, graphs, etc. MMDL is vital as many real world applications of AI require understanding and synthesizing several types of data.

For instance, a clinical decision support model might ingest patient X-Rays, the doctor’s notes from the electronic health record (EHR), and time series data of the patient’s vital data; an AI app on your phone that helps you fix problems with your car might need to synthesize image of your vehicle and tabular diagnostic codes; a stock trading application prices might take historical time series data and recent textual news articles; or a shipping company might need to analyze satellite images, numerical logistics data, and weather data to suggest the best route. Pretty much every industry has problems that require some form of multi-modal reasoning. Additionally, human intelligence is predicated on using our various senses which are somewhat analogous to multi-modal inputs. In a previous article I described multi-modal deep learning[ for time series forecasting and classification](</p/8033c1e1e772>).

Here, however, I want to return to understanding and applying multi-modal deep learning on text and images. In many ways the research/implementation of MMDL on text/images is much more mature than with respect to time series and other modalities. We have a number of foundation models and LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, Gemini) that can handle text and images out of the box. We also have more research on the alignment of the embedding space (e.g., Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training [CLIP]). However, as we will see, there are a number of limitations, particularly with respect to “out-of-distribution” text image pairs and long sequences of text. Furthermore, although a lot of effort/analysis has gone into English versions of these models, relatively few have evaluated these in a multi-lingual context.

In this series of articles, we are going to look at the task of “document understanding” with LLMs. Specifically, we will see if we can effectively search and cluster documents in the Cairo Genizah Archive using multi-modal embeddings; and if optical character recognition free models (more on what this means later) can accurately transcribe/translate any of these documents. We will explore both off the shelf models as well models that we will fine-tune for this specific task. Finally, we will look at how we might “productionalize” a search/transcription system for academics/scholars using the models we train.

From a technical perspective hope to leave with a much deeper understanding of how high dimensional embeddings of text/images are aligned in vector space; the current state of document understanding models; fine-tuning/adapting LLMs to low-resource languages; the limits of prompt engineering; and the computation costs/challenges of leveraging such large models. Along the way, we may uncover some new insights about the Cairo Genizah itself as it is a fascinating set of texts.

## Dataset, goals, and challenges

The Cairo Genizah is a trove of over 400,000 documents dating all the way from the 6th to the 18th centuries. The documents are in various languages including Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, and others. Of those 400,000 documents, at least 75,000 have been digitized and are available online. There are multiple collections of Genizah documents scattered across different libraries. The primary set we will be using in this article is the Princeton Cairo Genizah collection (PGP). The PGP contains roughly 39,000 documents. Of these, academics have transcribed about 5,000. Even fewer of the documents have translations. The PGP is useful because it contains rich metadata about the documents such as a description and estimated dates. This will be useful for our embedding models and search system.

The second set of documents that we will use is from Cambridge University. Since the PGP focuses exclusively on transactional/marriage documents, not religious texts, we need to also leverage the Cambridge collection of Genizah documents. Documents (ketubahs, business contracts, letters, etc.) make up roughly 10% of the Cairo Genizah while the other 90% are religious texts. The Jewish Theological Seminary also has a small collection that we may end up leveraging as well. Due to the different format of these archives there is a lot of code/scraping that went into acquiring structured data from the different collections in the correct format. I will not detail those efforts here, but you can view the code in the repository. I’ve open-sourced the raw JSON/image files as well on Kaggle for others to use for their own research purposes.

With that said, for our purposes we want to focus on doing two things:

**_(1) Facilitate searching, clustering, and question/answering about the documents using multilingual and multimodal RAG._**

Although the documents are searchable using a basic search on the Cairo Genizah, the search is limited in scope and tedious. For instance, we could not easily find a document that contained lines from the “shema” or find all documents “similar” to an existing document based on textual content and age.

More complicated questions like what documents cite Rashi are currently impossible. There is also an image search component where someone might want to input the image of a document and similar style documents (in terms of parchment, ink, writing style, etc). In order to improve the search mechanism we need to form multi-modal and multi-lingual representations of the documents that we can use for semantic searches. These representations may shed some insights into the documents in their own right, as we will see below.

**_(2) Directly transcribe/translate the un-transcribed documents_**

Our second task is to directly transcribe the documents from the image. In this case we need to transcribe the Hebrew/Arabic text and make it directly searchable for the documents with missing transcriptions. There has been a lot of work on Optical Character Recognition models and this is likely what the Princeton team is working on. Currently, however there is no good automated solution for transcription. Several have noted that Tessaract fails to work reliably even on block print in the Cairo Genizah (Wolf}. Moreover, open source right to left deep learning models, particularly multimodal ones, are still severely lacking. Therefore any research publication in this area could significantly accelerate a number of projects.

We are going to take a different approach from the traditional OCR models though. We are going to use OCR “free” models like Donut to learn to transcribe without the time consuming process of writing bounding boxes. We will train a general use “Hebrew/Arameic/Arabic” model that can be used for other document tasks, then fine-tune it specifically for use on the Genizah Documents. I will cover this step in the second article of this series.

(3) **_Learn and understand the fundamental principles of cross modal alignment and how that might transfer beyond our current use case._**

While this project is about the Cairo Genizah and document understanding, we can also get a better sense of how alignment of multiple modalities for search/retrieval works in practice. This knowledge could provide the framework for designing other multi-modality retrieval systems. For instance, as someone working with time series forecasting for climate purposes, I am interested if we could create time series embeddings of streams/rivers that could then be queried with an expression like “Colorado River at flood stage,” to see if it would return all the relevant time series embeddings.

Fundamentally, CLIP seems to work well at aligning short text sequences with simple images in the same vector space for retrieval. We need to understand the reason for this. Second, how do we extend that to longer more complicated versions of text and more nuanced images (e.g. The Cairo Genizah). What are the challenges? What worked, what didn’t, and why? What adjustments do we have to make in terms of architecture and fine-tuning? Third, how do we expand this beyond just text and image alignment? How do we expand these ideas to other modality pairs? Fourth, how do we expand this to many modalities (text, audio to time series, video or some other many combos)? Thinking about the future, we need retrieval systems and models that can effectively synthesize many modalities.

This touches on idea of learning disentangled representations as well. With disentangled representation learning we generally want to separate different factors of variation into independent components of the representation space. Interestingly, models like CLIP already demonstrate some implicit disentanglement — as evidenced by text-to-image generation in Stable Diffusion, where CLIP embeddings can guide the creation of images with specific visual attributes, styles, and semantic content.

This suggests that different dimensions of the CLIP embedding space do capture distinct factors of variation of text and images, even without explicit disentanglement training. [Materzynska et al for](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07835>), instance, “find that a simple setup and an orthogonal projection can in fact separate the two capabilities.” In our Cairo Genizah clustering results, we observe similar implicit disentanglement: documents naturally separate by language (Hebrew vs. Arabic), content type (religious vs. secular), and document genre. The clear separation between Talmud/piyyut clusters and letter/legal document clusters indicates that the embeddings have learned to distinguish between religious and administrative textual domains — a form of semantic disentanglement that aligns with medieval scholarly categories. However, these representations remain largely entangled, mixing visual features (script style, material properties), textual semantics (content meaning, language), and contextual factors (time period, geographic origin) within the same embedding dimensions. The key research question becomes: how can we leverage and enhance the implicit disentanglement already present in foundation models like CLIP?

Building on approaches like those of Materzynska et al, we could potentially learn orthogonal projections that separate visual paleographic features from textual semantics. For example, one subspace might capture script style and material composition (useful for dating and provenance analysis), while another captures semantic content and document type (useful for thematic research). This would enable queries like “find documents with similar handwriting but different content” or “retrieve texts with similar material composition regardless of language.”

### **Current Challenges in Genizah Scholarship and the role of AI**

Although a lot of progress has been made with respect to Cairo Genizah scholarship there remains a lot of challenges.

**Identifying Join Candidates**

One of the large hurdles in Cairo Genizah scholarship is identifying different document fragments that together form one full/entire document. There are many fragments of the same document that were separated from each other and that do not make sense without the accompanying fragment. Attempting to determine which fragments fit together is called “identifying join candidates.” Wolf et al in “ _Identifying Join Candidates in the Cairo Genizah_ ” wrote about automated methods of joining fragments. However, only about 24% of their suggested joins turned out to be verified joins. Additionally, their method had several key limitations that affected its effectiveness. The approach required significant manual preprocessing, including manual identification of written sides, coarse alignment, and removal of non-relevant components like labels and rulers. The method’s performance was heavily dependent on image quality and varied across different collections due to varying imaging conditions, while many fragments remain difficult to analyze due to faded ink or discolored pages. Most importantly they focused almost entirely on visual/handwriting analysis rather than textual content.

By creating multi-modal embeddings in our analysis we can hopefully suggest even more joins. The power of modern embedding systems far surpasses what Wolf et al employed in 2010. We can also utilize a unified representation that combines transcription (where available), document image, and the document meta-data. This should make it much easier to search for similar documents and potential joins.

**Lack of Transcriptions/Translations**

One of the biggest challenges with searching and studying the Cairo Genizah archive is the lack of transcriptions. As mentioned above only a small minority of documents have been transcribed. This will mainly be a focus of part two of this article series. However, obviously having less than 10% of the entire collection transcribed makes research very difficult. Surprisingly, not much research has explicitly looked at applying ML to Genizah transcription.

**Composition of Writing Materials**

In terms of images themselves another area of interest is deciphering the type of parchment/paper and the inks used. In the book _“Composition Analysis of Writing Materials in Cairo Genizah Document”_ Zina Cohen goes into great detail about the types of inks and parchments. She verifies the inks using reflectology and X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) Spectrometry to identify carbon and non-carbon based inks. Based on her findings she classifies three major types of writing materials:

* Paper: 257 manuscripts (majority)
* Parchment: 113 manuscripts
* Leather: Small number (exclusively non-biblical religious scrolls from Babylonian community)

and four major types of inks:

1. Carbon inks (soot/charcoal-based)
2. Plant inks (tannin-based from tree bark/gallnuts)
3. Iron-gall inks (iron + gallic acid reaction)
4. Mixed inks (combinations of the above)

For our purposes this is useful when creating “negative pairs” for the NOMICs model. In order for the model to learn visual differences between documents we can create negative pairings based on the ink/parchment types verified by Zina Cohen. Over time hopefully our model could then generalize its knowledge to unseen documents and understand the underlying ink composition. This could shed new light on these documents without the need for invasive techniques like spectroscopy.

**Authorship Attribution**

Another challenge with Genizah research is authorship attribution. In many documents in the Cairo Genizah we do not know who the original author was. By examining the script, writing style, vocabulary, inks, etc of certain documents academics can make guesses about the author. With a successful embedding model we can likely do something similar by examining the clusters and the similarity of documents. HaCohen-Kerner et al for instance discussed using K-Means and Expectation Maximization to cluster respona in Rabbinic Literature. Whereas more recently Bauersfield et al proposed using a transformer (BERT) architecture to de-annoymize double blind papers in the peer review process. In this scenario they explicitly trained the model for document classification rather than creating clusters. An obvious limitation of this approach is the ability to generalize to unknown/unseen authors.

For our purposes clustering makes much more sense. We could embed multiple documents from the same author and a target document. We could measure the cosine similarity between the author’s known documents and the “target” document. We could also test additional documents and see if they are closer.

**Lack of centralized search/dispersed (physical and online collections)**

Finally, an overall end-to-end search/chatbot that incorporates the Genizah documents as sources along with academic literature could help power many other research investigations. Currently it is a painstaking and manual process to search Genizah fragments and their associated existing academic literature. Documents are spread over several different collections Cambridge, Princeton Cairo Genizah, Jewish Theological Seminary and several others. Academic literature mainly exists on JSTOR or other websites. By extracting and tying fragments to academic articles we could allow scholars to quickly survey the space.

All of these challenges are somewhat inter-connected as improving one can help the other areas. For example building a better search engine can link more documents with their academic transcriptions this could provide more training data for OCR Free models. Likewise, better OCR free models could improve the search engine performance. The improved embeddings from incorporating ink characteristics could also improve clustering and location origin identification plus inspire more manual verification of documents. We can see the interplay of how AI can supplement the humanities research and how likewise the humanities research can improve the AI systems.

## Understanding multi-modal image/text retrieval

Now that we have identified some academic challenges with respect to Cairo Genizah scholarship we will begin our discussion of how AI might assist humanities researchers. But before we can do we need to truly understand how CLIP, COPALI, and semantic search operates.

**Understanding CLIP and how it works under the hood**

When talking about multi-modal embeddings we invariably return to Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training (CLIP). Despite being an older model (2021) in the rapidly moving world of AI, it provides the foundation for understanding how we can align textual and image embeddings in vector space. I’m not going to dive into every technical aspect of how CLIP works. For that you can read the original paper, but we are going to briefly revisit contrastive pre-training as we will be using variations of this to fine-tune our models for the Cairo Genizah.

(*Note for non-technical humanities readers: we are going to get very technical in this next section. But it is not necessary to understand everything. This section is mainly for other AI researchers to understand how these models work.)

With contrastive pre-training, the model is supplied with both an image and its accompanying description. The model takes several image/text pairings at once. For instance, in this case we might have

“Pepper the aussie pupper”:

“A Boeing 737 aiplane on a runway”

“Men eating hummus”

“A Macbook pro”

![Overview diagram of CLIP.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*qW7FNCcUzA2U_5Xh)

The text encoder creates a high-dimensional representation of the image and image encoder creates a high dimensional representation of the image. In the next stage both representations are mapped to the appropriate dimension:

* _Image features_ : The image encoder outputs are passed through a linear projection layer W_i that maps from the image representation dimension d_i to a shared multimodal embedding dimension d_e
*  _Text features:_ Similarly, text encoder outputs go through a linear projection W_t that maps from text dimension d_t to the same shared embedding dimension d_e

After this both projected embeddings are L2-normalized:

* I_e = l2_normalize(I_f @ W_i)
* T_e = l2_normalize(T_f @ W_t)

This normalization is crucial as it means the similarity computation becomes purely about the cosine similarity between embeddings. The actual alignment happens through the computation of a similarity matrix:

* A similarity matrix is computed as: logits = I_e @ T_e.T * exp(t)
* This creates an N × N matrix where N is the batch size
* Each element (i,j) represents the similarity between image i and text j
* The similarities are scaled by a learned temperature parameter t

Through the loss function the model learns :

* **Maximize** cosine similarity for the N correct (image, text) pairs (diagonal elements)
* **Minimize** cosine similarity for the N² — N incorrect pairings (off-diagonal elements). So for instance in our sample above

So in Python this would something like this:

``` # labels are just [0, 1, 2, …, N-1] indicating correct pairs are on diagonallabels = np.arange(N)# Loss from image perspective (each image should match its text)loss_i = cross_entropy_loss(logits, labels, axis=0)# Loss from text perspective (each text should match its image)loss_t = cross_entropy_loss(logits, labels, axis=1)# Final symmetric lossloss = (loss_i + loss_t) / 2 ```

CLIP uses a large batch size, therefore the model will see a large number of “negative pairings.” Over time the model begins to learn how to form effective similar representations between the concepts. Specifically, the authors trained on a single batch size of 32,768 image/text pairs and 400 million total images.

One drawback of this approach is that since batches are randomly sampled the negative pairings might not always be negative. This is somewhat mitigated by training on a huge dataset. But this is obviously something we need to keep in mind if we end up fine-tuning CLIP on a relatively small dataset like the Cairo Genizah.

Now that we have seen a bit about how CLIP works let’s move onto multi-lingual embeddings and document embeddings. For this we will use **NOMIC,** but before we do that let’s discuss a bit about how NOMIC Copali works and why CLIP fails for documents.

**NOMIC CoPali**

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*heGp1lZaabuUOSPK)

One downside of CLIP is that it doesn’t work well for long text sequences like you might find in a document. It is mainly trained/fine-tuned with simple phrase/image pairs (e.g. “A golden retriever running in the grass.”) With a long complex document there are multiple things at play from the page structure, to the text itself, to headers, etc. Additionally, the semantic content of a document often spans multiple sentences or paragraphs, requiring understanding of longer textual contexts than CLIP’s typical training pairs. We can see how a specialized model might be necessary — one that can handle document-level understanding rather than simple image-caption pairs. This is where CoPali comes in, as it was specifically designed for document retrieval tasks.

ColPali comes from the paper [_“ColPali: Efficient Document Retrieval with Vision Language Models_](<https://openreview.net/forum?id=ogjBpZ8uSi>) _.”_ Now the biggest difference with CoPali is that Copali was made specifically for document retrieval instead of just general images. Second, in training, CoPali actually is trained using “hard-negatives.” For instance, if we had a set of documents this what a training batch might look like:

```json training_batch = [{"query": "What is the total revenue for Q3 2023?","document_image": financial_report_page_image, # PNG/JPEG of PDF page"positive_pair": True},{"query": "How does machine learning improve healthcare outcomes?","document_image": medical_ai_research_paper_image,"positive_pair": True},{"query": "What are the side effects of ibuprofen?","document_image": pharmaceutical_drug_label_image,"positive_pair": True},{"query": "Show me the project timeline for the construction phase","document_image": engineering_blueprint_image,"positive_pair": True}] ```

So CoPali expects query/image pairs and negative ones in the same batch. Negative in the sense all of these documents are very different.

I will discuss what that means for the Cairo Genizah in the next section. But the basic idea is that in each training batch we would want as dissimilar as possible types of documents/queries. After these pre-processing/embedding these images/text encodings would take on the following format

``` document_representations = [financial_report_patches, # Shape: [1024, 128] - 1024 image patchesmedical_paper_patches, # Shape: [1024, 128]drug_label_patches, # Shape: [1024, 128]blueprint_patches # Shape: [1024, 128]]# Query becomes token embeddingsquery_representations = [revenue_query_tokens, # Shape: [15, 128] - ~10 query tokens + 5 unusedhealthcare_query_tokens, # Shape: [18, 128]ibuprofen_query_tokens, # Shape: [12, 128]timeline_query_tokens # Shape: [16, 128]] ```

Next after the query and the documents are embedded the representations are run through the model and a matrix is computed:

``` # Compute similarity scores for all combinationsscores_matrix = [ # Query 0 (revenue) vs all documents [ LI(revenue_query, financial_report), # 0.85 - HIGH (correct pair) LI(revenue_query, medical_paper), # 0.12 - low LI(revenue_query, drug_label), # 0.08 - low LI(revenue_query, blueprint) # 0.15 - low ], # Query 1 (healthcare) vs all documents [ LI(healthcare_query, financial_report), # 0.18 - low LI(healthcare_query, medical_paper), # 0.92 - HIGH (correct pair) LI(healthcare_query, drug_label), # 0.31 - medium (false negative risk!) LI(healthcare_query, blueprint) # 0.11 - low ], # Query 2 (ibuprofen) vs all documents [ LI(ibuprofen_query, financial_report), # 0.05 - low LI(ibuprofen_query, medical_paper), # 0.28 - medium LI(ibuprofen_query, drug_label), # 0.88 - HIGH (correct pair) LI(ibuprofen_query, blueprint) # 0.07 - low ], # Query 3 (timeline) vs all documents [ LI(timeline_query, financial_report), # 0.22 - low LI(timeline_query, medical_paper), # 0.14 - low LI(timeline_query, drug_label), # 0.09 - low LI(timeline_query, blueprint) # 0.79 - HIGH (correct pair) ] ```

We can then see how the model learns the correct similarity pairs and dissimilar documents. In computation of the pairwise cross entropy loss only the hardest negative is used. Therefore the model learns which documents are truly dissimilar. Altogether this works very well to query documents based on short strings.

![The CoPali Architecture for document and query encoding.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*KN3DK3T9J9OjX9b2sMZgOA.png)

One downside of the Colnomics approach is that it is not symmetric meaning that an image would not necessarily have a high cosine similarity with a similar image. Only the query image patch pairs have a high similarity. This means if we wanted to cluster documents based on images or create an image based search that would likely not work well with COLPALI as a full image embedding is never created. Therefore as we will see the image clusters with COPALI don’t really make that much sense. Instead we will likely need to train Hebrew version of CLIP then fine-tune on the Cairo Genizah documents in order to have effective image search/clustering.

**How would we want document embeddings to work for the Genizah?**

I hope our discussion has been interesting thus far, but we haven’t specifically delved into how this relates to the Cairo Genizah. Before we start embedding Genizah documents, let’s think about what sorts of representations would actually be useful for a retrieval/question answering system. As we discussed with CoPali, ideally we would want “similar” Genizah documents to be close to each other in the embedding space. We also want both English/Hebrew search queries to be close to their respective relevant documents in terms of cosine similarity.

With document retrieval, it is also important to remember that we don’t just want the transcription of the document and the image to align in embedding space. Instead, what we really want is for the document description/summary to align with the image in embedding space. For instance, say we have the following document:

![Page of the Babylonian Talmud from the Cairo Genizah. https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-TS-MISC-00012-00022-00002/1](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*vVRALXI_K2AS_41e)

The actual document transcription is long and would likely not match our/user queries directly. Instead we probably want the image embedding to be more representative of the document description, key phrases, location, language and date attributes in the JSON, while also containing information about the document/fragments physical condition and overall aesthetic (like missing characters partition, script style, etc.). **Experiments/Evaluation**

Traditionally retrieval systems are evaluated using metrics like precision, recall, and F1 score. However, for the Cairo Genizah that remains difficult due to us not having the ground truth answers for any specific query. Particularly recall is difficult to compute for this reason as we don’t know what all the relevant retrieved instances are for a query. Precision is a bit easier but still relevance can be a bit subjective. For our preliminary evaluation we classified documents into broad categories such as legal documents, Talmudic (prints), piyyut, and non-legal letters. We then used K-Means to create four clusters based on the embeddings.

In terms of evaluating the clusters we can then use silhouette scores, however these too have their limitations. For those unfamiliar let’s briefly review what silhouette scores are and what their strengths/weaknesses are for evaluating the K-Means clusters we just generated.

Fundamentally silhouette scores are a value between -1 and 1. Generally the higher the number the better the clustering results. A positive number broadly means items are assigned to the right cluster whereas negative means items are assigned to the wrong cluster. A number close to zero indicates overlapping clusters.

![Plotting the four document types four distinct clusters do emerge. Piyyut and Talmud are in the left hand side of the image. On the right side letters and legal documents. There is some lap of letters and legal documents as some legal documents are sent as letters. This qualitatively makes sense but results in lower scores.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*hPtwd7ujpx77BfwXkTf9zg.png)

Overall the clustering results demonstrate both strong category separation (purity = 0.832) and semantically meaningful overlap. Talmud and piyyut documents form highly distinct clusters, while letters and legal documents show expected overlap, reflecting the historical reality that many letters contained legal content such as contracts and business agreements.

Standard clustering metrics like silhouette score (0.206) suggest moderate performance, but qualitative analysis reveals that our embeddings capture sophisticated semantic relationships that reflect medieval Jewish textual culture. The apparent ‘confusion’ between letters and legal documents reflects historical reality, while the separation of religious from secular texts demonstrates culturally meaningful clustering that pure metrics cannot capture.

This highlights a broader challenge in applying AI to humanities: success requires evaluation frameworks that incorporate domain expertise alongside quantitative metrics.

Evaluation is something that we hope to further improve. Our preliminary results reinforce the idea that NOMICs can create good embeddings. Ultimately evaluation will need to leverage expert knowledge of the documents and the corpus.
