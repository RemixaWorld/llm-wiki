## The Reasoning Problem

Large Language Model’s (LLMs) capabilities are often explained as dark magic but they are actually due to a much simpler phenomenon: **pattern matching**.

### Fake it till you make it.

Simply put, they are regurgitating the data they’ve seen during training, establishing a clear correlation between the quality of the data and the expected outcomes.

Therefore, you can’t expect behaviors they have not seen during data. Sadly, when it comes to reasoning, their capacity to plan or elicit complex thoughts or execute complex actions is tremendously limited as such data patterns are unequivocally vacant in most available data.

While most text on the open Internet may express intelligent thoughts or conclusions, that data usually misses the most critical part: **the thought process.**

> Experts expose their conclusions to a certain problem but rarely, if ever, how they reached such a conclusion. Therefore, our frontier AI models can only hope to imitate the quality of previously seen reasoning conclusions, but we can’t expect them to reason the entire thought process to reach that known thought, let alone reason a new one.

That’s why the current elicitations of intelligence of models like GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet are not what they seem.

**They are nothing but an illusion of intelligence** , an instance of a model that has seen so much data that it can interpolate knowledge points into new ones that are, in reality, **simple regurgitations of known thoughts**.

> Don’t get me wrong, the fact that we have created ‘intelligent databases’ that can convincingly imitate intelligence, and compress so much knowledge into such a small digital file, is nothing short of extraordinary and already very useful, but still an illusion of raw intelligence nonetheless.
>
> In his seminal piece that birthed the field of AI, ‘[Computing Machinery and Intelligence](<https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf>),’ Alan Turing defined Artificial Intelligence as ‘The Imitation Game’ (hence the film's name).
>
> Today, more than seventy years later, **that definition stands as true as ever.**

This reality, **rarely accepted by incumbents explicitly** , has undoubtedly been accepted implicitly, as all these labs have resorted to the same conclusion: if we can’t expect LLMs to develop reasoning capabilities out of thin air, we must develop the reasoning data so that they can imitate not only quality thoughts but also the reasoning process that leads to them.

And that leads us to synthetic data.

### To Copy or Not to Copy, the Question is

A few days ago, [The New York Times released an article](<https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2024/08/26/upshot/ai-synthetic-data.html>) explaining the ‘model collapse’ problem.

If we continuously train models using their own data, they eventually collapse their distribution of known words, even reaching a point of outputting the same text sequence repeatedly.

![Source: NYTimes](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:500/1*U_UpyUPU5xv3Ukv4bbItpA.gif)

While this is undoubtedly true, this article falls short in acknowledging that synthetic data is already being vehemently used as a form of training, even giving birth to entire models trained solely on artificially generated data, with prominent examples such as GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Grok-2-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, or the Microsoft Phi family.

All these models have been trained using synthetic data, at least partially. Nonetheless, synthetic data has become a fundamental piece of the training puzzle in all circumstances, as Llama 3.1 models were also heavily trained on synthetic data, too.

Specifically, Meta used a very interesting method: They checkpointed previous instances of the model during training, fine-tuned those instances on task-specific data like coding, and used those models to generate new coding data to feed the more advanced version.

It’s what the NY Times article cites as using the outputs of an LLM to train itself but with innovative techniques that, far from collapsing the model knowledge, **amplify its performance in certain tasks of interest**.

But we can even take it a step further.

> We can train a mini version of a model to imitate its larger model to 90% of performance for 20% of the cost, a process known as distillation.

### Compression and Distillation

Since the release of GPT-4 back in March 2023, to today’s GPT-4o mini, the difference in pricing is astonishing:

* [GPT-4o-mini](<https://openai.com/api/pricing/>) is **400 times cheaper** than [GPT-4’s March 202](<https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7127956-how-much-does-gpt-4-cost>)3 version for a million tokens of input prompt processing,
* and **200 times cheaper** for million tokens of output

_And the best thing of all?_ GPT-4o-mini is a way better model in terms of raw performance, too. _But how is that possible?_ The answer is distillation.

_But what is distillation?_ Distillation is a training process in which __ the objective function isn’t cross-entropy like in a standard LLM (maximizing the probability assigned to the most likely word) but including a distribution divergence loss ([usually using the KL Divergence formula](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence>)).

In layman’s terms, while an LLM is given a piece of text and has to predict the next word in a sequence, a distilled model (the student) is trained to not only match the probability assigned to the correct token **but essentially match the teacher model’s entire distribution** , aka learning to behave just like the parent model (the teacher).

Therefore, for that exercise to work, you don’t train the model on an open dataset of publicly available data, **but essentially on data generated by the larger model (fully synthetic)**.

_But how is this mathematically implemented?_

> Skip the following section if you can live without knowing this; it’s not required to continue reading.

### **How is this mathematically implemented?**

LLMs maximize the **cross-entropy** , which is nothing more than paying attention to the probability the model assigned to the correct next token in a sequence and using the loss gap as a signal to improve the prediction.

But as you know, LLMs predict a distribution over the next token, not just one token. As we want to match the teacher model’s style and behavior, what we want is for both distributions to match so that the student model becomes the ‘embodiment’ of the teacher model.

Thus, we add an extra term, the **KL Divergence loss**. This term takes the logits of both models (the values of each token in the vocabulary before computing the last softmax layer) **and penalizes the student model whenever it diverges from the teacher’s distribution**.

In other words, we aren’t only focusing on matching the most likely token; we intend to match the entire distribution (naturally, both models share the same token vocabulary). This might be complicated to process without visuals, so we are doing just that.

Looking at the image below, each area under the red and blue curves represents the distribution over the next token for the teacher (red) and the student (blue)

> Gaussian (normal) distributions are rarely the case; this is an illustrative example.

Hence, **the KL divergence loss incentivizes the blue distribution (the student) to merge into the red distribution** , so that the probabilities assigned to every single token in the vocabulary for the next prediction match.

Finally, we combine both losses and evaluate the total loss. Therefore, the model does not only learn to perform next-word prediction correctly, but also to generate text in the teacher's style.

> To balance learning, we normally add a constant that increases the weight of the cross-entropy loss or KL divergence, respectively, adapting learning if necessary, in the form **Loss = C x Cross-entropy loss + (1 — C) x KL Divergence Loss** which is the loss that the model actually optimizes against.

_But why are we discussing all this?_ Well, because this is all you need to know to understand OpenAI’s strategy.

## From Strawberry to Orion

OpenAI’s unveiled strategy can be divided into two steps, which unsurprisingly represent two new model families.

### Strawberry, the reasoning standard

Heavily speculated for almost a year, OpenAI has been working on a new model that showcases considerably improved reasoning capabilities.

Although we can’t fully claim to know how it works, several published OpenAI research papers give us quite an idea.

* Back in 2021, [they presented the concept of verifiers](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14168>). These surrogate models learn to evaluate or critique the quality of the model’s thoughts, helping it navigate the space of possible solutions and settle for one, improving a model’s performance considerably in reasoning-required tasks like maths.

> The intuition is that allowing models to search and sample multiple possible solutions increases the likelihood that at least one is correct.

* Furthermore, last year, they presented ‘[Let’s verify step by step](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050>),’ another seminal piece that studied process-supervised reward models (PRMs, which they did not invent, though). These models, instead of simply looking at the model’s final output to evaluate the correctness, looked at every step of the model’s thought process, **identifying critical mistakes that may lead to wrong conclusions or outputs**.

![Source: OpenAI](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*EZKRKOIbbGgcLyhOKYU2FA.png)
* Finally, this year, [they presented Prover-verifier games](<https://openai.com/index/prover-verifier-games-improve-legibility/>), a paper where they helped stronger models generate easier-to-understand answers that weaker models, or humans, could properly evaluated. Formally considered an alignment-focused paper envisioning a future where AIs are superior to humans, **this is also an obvious next step in their path to using verifiers that are smaller and cheaper to run than the generator**.

_And what do all these papers indicate?_ Well, our first new set of models, the Strawberry family, will encapsulates all this research into one model:

A powerful LLM that can search the solution space (combined with a search algorithm like Monte Carlo Tree Search, as we recently saw with [AgentQ](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/agentq-a-human-beating-ai-agent-85353bfd1c26>)) **that will use verifiers to validate each thought in a thorough** (step-by-step) manner but also in an **economically feasible way** (using weaker verifiers that don’t require humongous amount of compute).

But as hinted by The Information piece, this probably won’t be enough in terms of unit economics (running models for a long time to generate multiple solutions is very expensive).

Therefore, [as OpenAI is already losing billions](<https://finance.yahoo.com/news/report-claims-openai-burned-8-105046378.html#:~:text=Report%20claims%20that%20OpenAI%20has,make%20a%20%245%20billion%20loss>), they may be looking to create a **mini Strawberry model** , obtained through distillation from a larger Strawberry model, but renouncing its active search functionality while having a considerable portion of the reasoning capabilities of the teacher.

Then, by adding this new model to the ChatGPT product, you get an efficient-to-run model boasting supercharged reasoning capabilities.

> **But how would that solve the unit economics problem?**
>
> Instead of doing an active search during inference, this model would be trained through data augmentation. By imitating a model that performs the active search, we maximize the likelihood that the smaller model performs well-reasoned outputs without actively searching for them.
>
> In other words, they would be looking to improve the model’s intrinsic reasoning capabilities, or System 1 of Daniel Kahneman’s thinking modes, without dealing with the huge costs of active search.

However, as we mentioned at the beginning of this piece, Strawberry may be just a means to an end, **an extra step required to build the next generation of frontier AI models, a model codenamed Orion**.

### Synthetic supercharged data

Based on the article, OpenAI may implicitly suggest that to achieve our next leap in AI intelligence,**available human data is not only not sufficient, but we might even require an additional surrogate model that acts as a synthetic reasoning data generator on steroids (Strawberry)** , which is then used to create the truly powerful model, Orion.

According to The Information, this model has already been shown to the US Federal Government, which could also lead to tighter regulation around future powerful AI models (but that’s a story for another day).

> Consequently, this model codenamed ‘Orion’ is most likely GPT-5.

So, all things considered, _how do we pack this dense article into bullet points?_

1. First, OpenAI might have been using instances of GPT-4o (and, quite probably, GPT-4o-mini for better cost scaling)**to generate a huge dataset of level-one reasoning data**.
2. With that, and leveraging the aforementioned published research, such as "Let’s verify step by step" (and surely unpublished research, too), **they have trained powerful reward models that excel at evaluating reasoning chains**.
3. Then, knowing that the Prover-verifier research proves that they don’t really need huge reward models for this task, use GPT-4o/mini combined with verifier-led active search **to create an even more advanced level-two reasoning dataset** with strong reasoning chains in the thousands or millions of datapoints.
4. With that dataset they trained **Strawberry Large** , which is then distilled into ‘Strawberry mini’ which might be the enhancement that the ChatGPT product sees this very fall, which would be just a little taste from OpenAI to the world of what’s to come.
5. Finally, **they replicate steps 1 to 4 but using Strawberry Large instead of GPT-4o** , yielding our next frontier model, Orion (GPT-5).
