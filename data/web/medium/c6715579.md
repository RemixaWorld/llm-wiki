This article explores what the Transformer Model architecture has enabled — the magic of unsupervised learning, which gives capabilities like reasoning and generalization bordering on human-level understanding to large LLMs.

[Ilya Sutskever](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Sutskever>), the CTO and the man behind the GPT fame, arguably, one of the smartest men when it comes to LLMs, talks about Unsupervised Learning as the most important thing in Machine Learning, essentially the Holy Grail.

> At OpenAI .. the hope was that if you have a neural network that can predict the next word, it’ll solve unsupervised learning. So back before the GPTs, unsupervised learning was considered to be the Holy Grail of machine learning.…

What he means here is **not** about self-supervised learning, that is the idea of using the next word as the implicit label in training, instead, he alludes to something far more profound and transformative, hinting at the deeper potential of unsupervised learning to unlock new frontiers in AI. He continues on how it was finally solved using the Transformer Neural Network.

> But our neural networks were not up for the task. We were using recurrent neural networks. When the transformer came out, literally as soon as the paper came out, literally the next day, it was clear to me, to us, that transformers addressed the limitations of recurrent neural networks, of learning long-term dependencies. …And that’s what led to eventually GPT-3 and essentially where we are today. -[Ilya Sutskever](<https://www.forbes.com/sites/craigsmith/2023/03/15/gpt-4-creator-ilya-sutskever-on-ai-hallucinations-and-ai-democracy/?sh=3d1c37cb1218>)

The excitement, optimism, and wide-ranging applications surrounding Generative AI stem from the remarkable capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models, trained on trillions of word tokens, have evolved to do much more than predict the next word. They have learned to model the world itself, enabling them to generalize across various tasks. This remarkable ability is a product of **unsupervised learning**.

In this article, I aim to explore this phenomenon through the lens of **compression theory** and **Kolmogorov complexity**. While alternative interpretations exist, such as Claude Shannon’s **Information and Coding Theory** (focusing on entropy and information content) or the **PAC-Bayes framework**([connecting generalization to probability density functions](<https://simons.berkeley.edu/talks/studying-generalization-deep-learning-pac-bayes>) via Kullback-Leibler divergence), these approaches are mathematically dense and less intuitive. Compression theory, on the other hand, provides a more intuitive and relatable framework for understanding how these models achieve their remarkable results.

## Beyond Statistical Probability Distribution Calculators

Large Language Models (LLMs) are often regarded as statistical systems that compute probability distributions over their vocabulary, predicting the next token based on context using their learned attention mechanisms.

This is how they work, and this fact is so commonplace that most people and many experts still dismiss all capabilities of LLMs as just a glorified probability density function picker. This was especially true of the older and smaller models like GPT-2. But as the scale of the models started to increase exponentially along with the training data, emergent properties of generalization and reasoning happened which cannot be explained away without unsupervised learning.

The ability of these later and larger models GPT3/4, Gemini, LLAMA2/3, etc., to generate creative, reasoned, and contextually rich responses goes beyond mere calculations of probability density functions. These abilities stem from the **emergent properties** of their training process — properties that we might liken to human-like reasoning and understanding. There are multiple papers regarding this also from AI researchers. Still, the subject is usually discussed emprically and evidence is also highly subjective that it is still debated.

It is true that LLMs, such as GPTs and LLaMAs, operate by predicting the next token using probabilities derived from their auto-regressive architecture. But focusing solely on this mechanism by which and only by which they can generate output — akin to how vocal cords generate human speech — misses the essence of their capabilities. Understanding the mechanics of vocal cords does not fully explain the meaning or creativity of the words spoken. Similarly, the real intrigue lies not in the probabilities themselves but in how the model determines, from an infinite range of possibilities, the most contextually appropriate probabilities.

To explore this, we aim to investigate the technical underpinnings of these emergent properties, even if they are often described with inherently ambiguous terms like “reasoning” and “understanding.”

### Supervised learning

Supervised Learning is simple: it is learning from labelled data. The best example of one of the largest labelled datasets is **ImagNet**. The huge amount of labelled image data collected as part of the ImageNet project helped [**AlexNet**](<https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf>) introduced in 2012 (Ilya Sutskever, Alex Krizhevsky and Geoffrey Hinton) revolutionised computer vision and started the Deep Neural Network revolution.

> Supervised Learning works well. This is the bedrock of almost all machine learning used in production today.
>
> [“All the impressive achievements of deep learning amount to just curve fitting..”](<https://www.quantamagazine.org/to-build-truly-intelligent-machines-teach-them-cause-and-effect-20180515/>). The Truing Award winner Judea Pearl critique of AI/ML system. He is the one associated with formalising Causal reasoning.

Given enough labelled data, AI/ML systems will learn to approximate any complex multivariate functions. They are excellent **_universal function approximators_**. They become** _very good feature extractors_** of the data, associating features to the target label.

### Limitations of Supervised Learning

The problem with Supervised Learning is that labelling huge amounts of datasets for training models needs a lot of time and effort.

The larger issue is that this approach doesn’t align with how humans learn, and it’s impractical — if not impossible — to label every possible intent, intention, or generalization.

The limitations of supervised learning become evident when considering an analogy: a system trained on labelled images of dogs and cats, even using the most advanced Convolutional Neural Network, will struggle to generalize and associate both categories under the broader concept of “animal,” even if explicitly labelled. This highlights the inherent challenge of capturing higher-level abstractions through supervised methods alone.

### Unsupervised Learning in ML

Continuing from the understanding and usage of Supervised learning, many inadvertently associate unsupervised learning with Clustering via algorithms like K-Means clustering or PCM. However, in the case of Neural Networks, there is universally a cost or loss function to optimise via computing the difference between the expected value and generated value. So there always needs to be an expected or target value. Algorithms like Clustering, Principal Component Analysis etc are classic ML algorithms and have nothing to do with Neural Network Learning.

### Self-supervised Learning

Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) is a type of machine learning where the model learns from unlabeled data by generating its own labels from the data itself.

Assume that the Neural Network has to predict the preceding sentence in this passage. It can be given the first word “** _Those_** ” and ask it to predict anything next. Say it predicted “monks”, but the expected is “** _with_** ”. So using this concept the loss function can be computed with expected labels, without explicitly having to label the data, backpropagated and weights of attention layers as well as the final feed-forward layers adjusted and learned.

### Self-supervised Learning unlocks Training Data scale

What this truly unlocks is the whole **fifteen trillion token** or more of the World Wide Web, including code in Github, blogs, Quora, articles, and anything without a paywall are legitimate “training sets” — <https://www.educatingsilicon.com/2024/05/09/how-much-llm-training-data-is-there-in-the-limit/>

### Tokens, Vocabulary and Probabilities in LLMs

In GPT-3 and similar language models, **vocabulary** refers to the set of all tokens that the model can recognize and use to generate or process text. GPT-3 has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. It is much larger in bigger models.

**Tokens** are the basic building blocks of text that the model processes. Tokens can represent: A single word (e.g., “apple”); a part of a word (e.g., “ap”, “ple”); a single character (e.g., “a”, “b”); special symbols (e.g., punctuation or control tokens like [ENDOFTEXT])

When GPT-3 generates text. At each step in the sequence, the model predicts the **next token**. The prediction is based on probabilities over all 50,257 tokens (its vocabulary). The token with the highest probability is chosen (or sampled, depending on settings like temperature).

For example: Suppose the text so far is “I love”.

GPT-3 calculates the probabilities for the 50,257 tokens to determine the next token. It might decide: “you” (most probable), “,” (second most probable), “Python” (less probable).

The **probability is not uniformly distributed** (i.e., not 1/50,257 for each token). Instead, GPT-3 uses its trained knowledge to assign higher probabilities to tokens that make sense contextually. For example:

Tokens like “you,” “Python,” or “,” might have higher probabilities, as they are common continuations of “I love.”

Tokens like “qzx” (if it exists) or random characters would have much lower probabilities because they are unlikely to fit the context.

To calculate the total number of possible combinations of three tokens that can be generated following the initial input “I” using GPT-3’s vocabulary of 50,257 tokens, we need to consider the following:

**After “I,” there are 50,257 possible tokens for the next word. and so on for each word**

The total number of possible combinations is 50,257×50,257×50,257. This means there are approximately **127 trillion possible combinations** of three tokens following the initial token “I”, though the probable combinations will be much lower.

Using this understanding lets us go to Sobieszek, Price paper

_When GPT continues a text, it picks one of these probable tokens, appends the text with it, and then recalculates the probability of continuations for the new prior text elongated by that token. This is the autoregressive quality of GPT-3 … Two things should be noted here: (a) many possible strategies of traversing this tree of possibilities exist (e.g. always picking the most probable token), ._**_.b) The exponential increase in the number of possible sentences that stems from this process is enormous (given GPT-3’s vocabulary of 50,257, the number of different possible outputs becomes greater than the number of atoms of the universe at a length of just 18 tokens)_**_._

**_..in order to produce coherent continuations, GPT-3 must’ have saved into the weights of its connections a massive amount of information._**

**_However, considering the exponential growth of the number of conditional probabilities that must be stored, this information must first be somehow compressed, and such compression usually cannot be lossless_**

> ** _What we’ll claim is lost during this compression and the generalizations made during training are the source of both the limitations and supposed intelligence of such models_**

### Compression as a means of understanding Unsupervised Learning.

> “prediction leads to compression, compression leads to generalisation, and generalisation leads to computer intelligence” [3]

There are many concepts related to compression. One way to conceptualize this is through **Kolmogorov Complexity** , which measures the minimum length of a program required to describe a dataset. It is best explained by an example:

Consider a dataset with 10,000 repeated instances of the number 1:

x=”111111…1111" (10,000 ones)

**High Compressibility** : A program `print("1"*10000)` is much shorter than the data itself. Kolmogorov Complexity K(x) is low here

**Low Compressibility** : For a truly random string of length 10,000 (e.g., sensor noise), there’s no shorter representation. Kolmogorov Complexity K(x) is approximately the size of xxx.

This means that if you can Compress data, the algorithm should know the structure of the data. Example ZIP. A file with non-repeatable data is harder to compress than one with repeatable data.

A text with a [Jabberwocky poem](<https://www.scottishpoetrylibrary.org.uk/poem/jabberwocky/>) can be compressed at only 40% but a file with “a” repeated can be compressed 90%.

``` alex@pop-os:~$ ls -lh jabberwocky.* -rw-rw-r-- 1 alex alex 941 Nov 18 20:08 jabberwocky.txt-rw-rw-r-- 1 alex alex 657 Nov 18 20:07 jabberwocky.zipalex@pop-os:~$ ls -lh aa.* -rw-rw-r-- 1 alex alex 899 Nov 18 20:28 aa.txt-rw-rw-r-- 1 alex alex 210 Nov 18 20:28 aa.zip ```

It may surprise you that the most efficient text compressor is based on the Transformer model and LLM by [Fabrice Bellard](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrice_Bellard>) which used a language model for very high compression. Here is the paper <https://bellard.org/nncp/nncp.pdf> and <https://bellard.org/nncp/nncp_v2.pdf> and NNCP leading in text compression leaderboard <https://www.mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1072>, though the exact mechanism is not open-sourced and hence may not be relevant in this context.

Another example is human vision. We take in so much information/data through our eyes and senses. But our brain can compress it enough to generalise on the essentials.

### LLMs and Reasoning

There are many logical and reasoning questions online and it is difficult to pick a prompt and check if the model has reasoning or if it is just repeating from a similar training dataset for that prompt.

The rigorous way would be to check the score on benchmarks like [MLMU](<https://paperswithcode.com/paper/llama-2-open-foundation-and-fine-tuned-chat/review/?hl=106287>) (Multitask Reasoning) <https://www.vellum.ai/llm-leaderboard> where it is clear that LLMs have substantial reasoning capabilities.

Still, for the sake of an example, I went with something simple and absurd hoping that the model (LLAMA2 13b Chat) has not seen this yet just for illustration.

``` my Cow has jumped over the barn,said Thomas. Can Thomas be trusted----Thomas is a person who is making a statement, but the statement itself is impossible. Cows cannot jump over barns, as they are large animals that do not have the ability to fly or jump over tall structures. Therefore, Thomas cannot be trusted to make accurate statements about his cow's abilities. ```

Assuming that the model has not seen this type of prompt in Training, as there are infinitely different ways to make unique text, still, it has pieced together the context _“that Thomas states his cow has jumped over a barn_ ” and from the instruction prompt -” Can Thomas be trusted”, it has not statistically just picked out a probable answer like _Yes/No Thomas can/cannot be trusted,_ but it has reasoned if it is “possible” for a “large animal like a cow” to jump or fly and hence deduced that the statement is “incorrect/unlikely” and that “Thomas statement cannot be trusted”.

### Initial empirical proof of Unsupervised Learning GP2 and GPT3

The paper **_Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners [6]_**_introduced_**GPT-2**(a 1.5 Billion parameter model )**t** o the world based on Transformer architecture. It did something very extraordinary. It proved empirically that a sufficiently large LLM, when trained with a sufficiently large dataset (CommomCrawl / WebText- cleaned up internet data) starts to generalise so well that it is able to successfully execute many tasks better or at par on the level of State of the Art supervised systems at that time.

_“GPT-2 displays a broad set of capabilities, including the ability to generate conditional synthetic text samples of unprecedented quality, where we prime the model with an input and have it generate a lengthy continuation. In addition, GPT-2 outperforms other language models trained on specific domains (like Wikipedia, news, or books) without needing to use these domain-specific training datasets. On language tasks like question answering, reading comprehension, summarization, and translation, GPT-2 begins to learn these tasks from the raw text, using no task-specific training data. While scores on these downstream tasks are far from state-of-the-art, they suggest that the tasks can benefit from unsupervised techniques, given sufficient (unlabeled) data and compute.”_<https://openai.com/index/better-language-models/>

This was the first practical demonstration of the capabilities of unsupervised learning, emprically verified with tests. The ranking in the tests can seen here — <https://paperswithcode.com/paper/language-models-are-unsupervised-multitask>

GPT-3 is a 175 Billion parameter model (Language Models are Few-Shot Learners[7] and it was trained almost exactly like GPT2 but on more tokens/ tain datasets and it improved considerably on the GPT2 benchmarks for NLP tasks- <https://paperswithcode.com/paper/language-models-are-few-shot-learners>

### Explaining Unsupervised Learning through Emergence

Other than compression, another way of examining the property of unsupervised learning is through the concept of Emergence. This is not universally accepted as of now as there are counterarguments here. But this is the case with most things in AI and LLM now.

The 2022 Google/DeepMind research paper _Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models_ [8]describes this phenomenon

>  _Emergence is when quantitative changes in a system result in qualitative changes in behavior._
>
> _An ability is emergent if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models. [8]_

It notes that the score in the benchmarks for various NLP tasks increases spontaneously beyond a model scale, though it offers no explanation as to the behaviour of emergence. The paper and [others](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.05335>) partly suggest that phase transitions in the internal representations or mechanisms could underlie emergent abilities. While metrics like cross-entropy loss show steady improvements during training, these phase transitions manifest as sudden, qualitative improvements in task performance, often without clear changes in the loss curve itself. These explanations are not intuitive.
