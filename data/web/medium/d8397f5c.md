## From Move 37 to Web Navigation

When **AlphaGo** defeated the world champion, Lee Sedol, in a best-of-5 game of _Go_ , a Chinese board game, [it was a defining moment in AI history](<https://youtu.be/WXuK6gekU1Y?si=QMAt5zXmtcnbyYqL>)[.](<https://youtu.be/WXuK6gekU1Y?si=QMAt5zXmtcnbyYqL>) Google Deepmind achieved it by training a model on a**Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm** , which is a crucial part of the agent we are looking at today.

_But what is MCTS?_

### How MCTS Works

In a nutshell, MCTS works by helping a model make the best decision possible in every state (in AlphaGo’s case, the state would be the current board state).

To do so, it performs ‘Monte Carlo simulations’ or ‘rollouts,’ in which **the algorithm ‘looks into the future’** by simulating the different possible outcomes going down a specific action trajectory.

For instance, in _Go_ , AlphaGo (or AlphaZero, a better version) simulates what events would unfold for each possible action it chooses, simulating until reaching a terminal state (winning or losing the game).

Based on that simulation, it measures the **expected cumulative reward** of that rollout (or the likelihood that going down that path will be a positive outcome for the model, aka winning the game). In other words, **the model chooses its following action based on the highest expected cumulative reward at any given time**.

But what makes MCTS work so well is that it also incentivizes exploration.

In other words, the model doesn’t always choose the decision with the highest expected reward, **but also explores actions with smaller expected rewards** that could**,** in the future, yield higher returns by unveiling unexpected new strategies.

_But how do we incentivize a model to explore?_

Whenever you are faced with a neural network, you need to ask yourself the following question: _what will this neural network learn?_

What an LLM—or any neural network, for that matter—learns **largely depends on the task and the objective function**.

In this case, while the task remains the same (next-token prediction), the objective function computes how well or poorly the model has performed, using this as feedback to direct what the model should learn or avoid.

In this case, the objective function used is **MCTS UCB1** , shown below, where:

* _Q(ht,a)_ refers to the Q-function based on the current environment and the proposed action.

> This function calculates the expected cumulative reward over the rollout of that action. That is, if the model chooses that action, what is the expected accumulated reward it will obtain going down that trajectory of actions?

* The other term includes a constant that amplifies or minimizes exploration (_cexp_), multiplied by a square root term.

> Without going into much detail, this term incentivizes exploration by giving a greater score to actions that enter the model into a ‘lesser-visited’ state, as both N(ht) and N(h+1) refer to the visitation frequency at a specific node (in this case, a particular page on a website).

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:488/1*-jGAkrgWLRX6AoxX-5zq8w.png)

_So, what does the agent learn with this objective function?_ Well, that it should choose actions that **maximize the likelihood of good outcomes** while also going through **unexplored paths** that could lead to greater results.

> Thus, an action may have a very high expected reward but may be very common, while an action that yields less potential reward may have a larger exploration incentive.
>
> This allows the algorithm to be creative and explore, choosing what appears to be worse actions for the benefit of more remarkable future outcomes instead of what seems to be the obvious option every time, **which can lead to suboptimal training**.

> And with exploration comes a great unlock: creativity.

### The First Creative Model

This exploration capacity, coupled with its rollout simulations, **led to AlphaGo performing one of the greatest moments in AI history:** the infamous ‘[move 37](<https://www.wired.com/2016/03/two-moves-alphago-lee-sedol-redefined-future/>)’.

This seemingly dumb movement at first (low immediate reward) ultimately **proved crucial to winning the game against the best human at the time**.

> If you don’t know the rules of Go (like me), a comparison to ‘Move 37’ in chess would “The Inmortal Game” between Adolf Anderssen and Kieseritzky back in 1851, when Anderssen sacrificed the queen, a seemingly stupid move, only to checkmate its rival a few moves later.

> But hold on a minute. If MCTS is so good, _why isn’t every LLM using it?_

The problem with MCTS, and Reinforcement Learning techniques in general (models that learn by measuring the reward over their actions)**is that the world is full of sparse rewards**.

In layman’s terms,**it’s hard to implement MCTS when measuring whether the robot’s action was good or not isn’t obvious**. Without going into robotics, where measuring rewards has become an AI field in itself ([as I’ve covered](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/p/circus-robots-first-ai-videoclip-opensource-catching>)), measuring a reward is sometimes a matter of significant subjectivity.

> For instance, considering two summaries of a given text, which is a prevalent task for an LLM, how do we choose which is best?

This underscores the biggest problem with Reinforcement Learning today: outside constrained environments like a board game with a clear signal of who’s right or wrong, it barely works…

_Or does it?_ Because that’s precisely what AgentQ has done in LLM web navigation.

## Autonomous Web Navigation

In simple terms, [**AgentQ fuses the worlds of LLMs and MCTS**](<https://multion-research.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/AgentQ.pdf>)**(and DPO, more on that in a second) to create an agent that can**[**surf the web and perform actions on it**](<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Pl_wJQ8JA5M>).

For instance, in the image below, the model can book a table for its user, even correcting it after making a mistake on the first search.

![Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*-kUi_o8TlFlptLDo)

Technically speaking, the model does the following:

* Having access to the past actions and the current website state (DOM), and the user’s request, generates a set of possible actions (the plan).
* It then measures the cumulative reward expected from choosing each action (the Monte Carlo rollouts we mentioned earlier), balancing if that trajectory has been explored enough (it only explores trajectories that have not been thoroughly explored).
* Then, it executes the chosen action,
* and in addition to the action, it explains why it decided the action it did, acting as an ‘inner dialogue’ that can be leveraged to ‘reason’ future predictions.

But an unanswered question remains: _How does the model measure the reward in a scenario like this? Aren’t we in one of those cases where measuring the model’s actions is hard?_

Yes, which leads us to the biggest contribution from AgentQ’s researchers: **the ‘AI critic.’**

### Combining AI and rollouts as signal

In order to deal with the uncertainty of open-ended tasks common in web navigation, the actual process the model undergoes is this one:

* When tasked by the user, AgentQ first uses its LLM to propose an **action plan** ,
* Then, **it verbalizes (literally writes down) the reasoning** behind the plan. In other words, it writes down why it will perform each step of the plan.
* Next, **it lays out the possible actions it can perform** at a given step, sending them to the AI critic (image below),
* The **AI critic then reorders the actions** according to what it considers the best potential outcomes (again, as seen in the image below)
* Then, **the model chooses an action** based on a combination of the critic’s feedback and the expected reward for each rollout (the expected reward of taking a specific direction we discussed earlier).
* Finally, the model **verbalizes an explanation** as to why each chose the action, reasoning over it in future actions.

Consequently, what would otherwise be a highly complex problem with hard-to-measure rewards can be solved with an additional AI that judges the outcomes of the model’s actions with pretty solid accuracy, giving birth to AgentQ.

![The AI Critic process. Source](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/0*zCFlIk9XRjBh5fyn)

_But how do we factor in the AI critic’s feedback?_

If we trace back to the equation we saw earlier, the Q-function is actually broken down into two terms as shown below.

* One is the AI critic’s feedback,
* The other is the actual reward obtained by the model after backpropagating the rollout (calculating the reward at each state based on whether the rollout ended in a positive outcome or not).

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:410/1*2A1wy_6ua2efmODvOJDdzg.png)

This result is then inserted into the first equation we saw earlier to find out which action is chosen.

> An important aspect of all this process is that, unlike a standard optimization procedure for a neural network, **MCTS is non-differentiable**. This means that to optimize the network we aren’t computing gradients, as we usually do when training neural networks, but updating two things:
>
> 1\. how well the model computes the expected reward on a trajectory (the first Q in the equation above)
>
> 2\. The visitation frequency on a node. In this use case, how many times has the model visited a specific page on a website (the N(ht) and N(h+1) terms we saw in the first equation).
>
> As we cannot optimize through gradients, this method requires online interaction, meaning that, in order to learn, the agent has to actively interact with websites. This may seem inocous, **but non-differentiable equations are much harder to optimize (and much more expensive).**

However, to further enhance the agent’s capabilities without the constraints of online training, researchers added a step that pushed the agent’s capacities further:

DPO.

### Improving decision making

As a final step, the researchers used the different explored trajectories to build a preference dataset to train the model on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to improve decision-making (a differentiable objective).

> **But what is DPO?**
>
> DPO is a very popular alignment method that enhances the model’s decision-making capabilities, **very commonly used in LLM training as an alternative to Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** , or RLHF, to align models into a particular behavior (improving their decision-making capabilities).
>
> The difference is that DPO optimizes the policy that maximizes the reward (that ensures the best outcome) **without having to materialize the reward** , assuming that by finding the optimal policy directly, **we are indirectly optimizing the reward**.
>
> As not having to calculate the reward of the preference alignment avoids having a reward model, **DPO is much cheaper than RLHF** and thus much more common amongst open-source efforts (although combining both is something that has been done too, with examples like Meta’s Llama models).

DPO considered, the actual pipeline used to train AgentQ is more complex than what you should expect from agent training but highly effective, in what could soon become standard.

1. **Initial Online Training with MCTS:**

* **Pre-trained LLM as a Base Model:** To avoid having to train a base model from scratch, they used a pre-trained model, Llama 3 70B.
* **MCTS for Exploration:** MCTS helps the model explore various possible actions and outcomes, particularly in environments where the sequence of actions significantly impacts the final result (e.g., web navigation or game-like scenarios).
* **Online Interaction:** During this phase,**the model interacts with the environment directly (online training)**. The MCTS algorithm helps balance exploration (trying new actions) and exploitation (using known good actions) to gather a diverse set of trajectories while improving the model’s ability to navigate effectively (choose better trajectories)

2\. **Further Fine-Tuning with DPO in an Offline Setting:**

* **Offline Data:** After the initial online training, the model has accumulated a set of trajectories (both successful and unsuccessful). These trajectories serve as the basis for offline fine-tuning.
* **DPO for Policy Refinement:** DPO operates on the collected offline data by comparing pairs of trajectories and optimizing the policy based on preferences (e.g., which trajectory led to better outcomes). This step refines the model’s decision-making process without needing further online interactions, which is expensive and difficult to optimize, as we mentioned beforehand.

Despite the overall complexity, AgentQ’s impressive results show it was highly worth it. _But does this agent take us any closer to this ‘God AGI’ that people are so desperate for?_

Of course not, but it pulls us closer to autonomous narrow agents, and that’s a remarkable achievement in itself.

## It’s an improvement… but a narrow one

I can’t hide my excitement on this.

[I have been all over LLMs+search in my newsletter for quite some time](<https://thetechoasis.beehiiv.com/subscribe>), as the new, super-powerful generation of AI models, **so it’s nice that we are finally seeing their inevitable emergence**. However, no matter how impressive AgentQ is, it still has limitations.

Although it’s shocking to see an LLM achieve above-human results in complex tasks like web navigation that require extensive planning, reasoning, and search, we are talking about a model that, once again, **has been optimized for a particular use case.**

In a nutshell, the only difference between this method and traditional RL methods is that this time, **we can leverage LLMs' better ‘intelligence’ to turn open-ended tasks like web navigation into optimizable problems**.

In other words, it’s a net improvement, but no, **this doesn’t take us anywhere close to AGI** , as the most significant unsolved problem remains:

* _How do we train an LLM with RL to optimize for hundreds of tasks, not just one?_

However, seeing that once again, the base model’s limitations limit what AgentQ can do, _could a better LLM model (think GPT-5 or Grok-3) be the unlocker we are waiting for?_
