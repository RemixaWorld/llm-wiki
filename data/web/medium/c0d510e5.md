Since 2004, we as Americans have gradually lost faith in the news industry, a trend that has largely gone unnoticed, because — well, who’s going to cover it?

This year’s [Gallup poll](<https://news.gallup.com/poll/651977/americans-trust-media-remains-trend-low.aspx>) reveals another record low — 69% of Americans say they trust the media “not very much” or “at all,” while only 31% say they trust the media a “great deal” or “fair amount”. This marks a dramatic shift from the poll’s inception in 1972, when trust in the media was more than twice that of distrust.

So, what’s happening here? Why have we lost faith in a historically trusted institution? There are now more outlets than ever to choose from, and journalists have a seemingly endless vat of resources at their disposal. Where is the disconnect?

## Why Trust in Media is at an All-Time Low

Like others in the Gallup poll, I too have lost a bit of faith in the American news system. From my view, outlets are dedicating more front page space to opinion pieces, while the remaining stories are fraught with cherry-picked facts and circular references. It also bugs me that media companies not only endorse political candidates, but also write openly about who their readers ought to endorse as well.

So, armed with my own preconceived bias about media bias (derp), I decided to do a bit of research on what has been happening inside our once esteemed news industry.

### Problem 1: Farewell to the Ombudsman

Season 1 of Michael Lewis’ _Against the Rules_ podcast outlines what he calls the “decline of the American referee” — or, for non sports fans, the decline in arbitration and oversight roles across numerous American industries. One such example he gives is the ombudsman within our news industry.

<https://youtu.be/otU-1Il7GfM?si=mguf9fYAQgsdCGRc>

I had to Google it too…

> **Ombudsman** — an independent representative within a news organization who addresses reader complaints, ensures journalistic integrity, and acts as a mediator between the public and the newsroom. Their role is to uphold transparency, accountability, and trust in reporting practices.

Since the late 2000s, the New York Times, Los Angeles Times, Chicago Tribune, NPR, and countless other outlets have either cut or significantly reduced the role of their ombudsmen citing financial constraints. While I don’t doubt that many media outlets have fallen on tough times, the lack of accountability and oversight within some of our biggest newsrooms is deeply concerning.

### Problem 2: Ideas of a Select Few

Ever wonder who ultimately owns the article you’re reading or the program you’re watching? Over the past few decades, there is a much higher likelihood of it being the same person. As the below infographic suggests, a majority of our news industry is owned by a select few.

While consolidation may bring efficiencies and resources to national outlets, its effects at the local level have been far from ideal. Economic pressures have led to the closure of over [**2,500 local newspapers since 2004**](<https://pressgazette.co.uk/news/news-deserts-research-newspapers-closed/>), creating “news deserts**”** — areas with little to no access to local journalism. This decline has drastically reduced resources for investigative reporting and critical community-level coverage, leaving local audiences with fewer reliable options.

As local journalism disappears or becomes reliant on syndication from national outlets, where do people turn for trustworthy information?

### Problem 3: Social Media

According to [Pew Research](<https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/social-media-and-news-fact-sheet/>), 54% of U.S. adults get at least some of their news from social media. While these platforms have made information more accessible, they’ve also made misinformation far more prevalent.

With the sheer volume of content and the rise of engagement-driven algorithms, it has become incredibly difficult to distinguish truth from hearsay.

## A Techno-Optimist Rose-Colored AI Glasses Argument for AI & News

But what if we took a different approach? One that uses AI as a content _organizer_ rather than content _creator_. What if we made ChatGPT the ombudsman?

To illustrate how this might work, let’s use an example.

Over the past few months, immigration has, once again, become a major topic of debate in American political circles. Trump’s mass deportation plan and recent nomination of Tom Homan has made it clear that there is a massive policy shift on the horizon. So, how is immigration being covered by different news outlets?

Immigration headlines from left-and right-leaning media

If you follow more conservative-leaning media outlets like Fox News, the New York Post, or the Washington Examiner, you’re likely to encounter stories emphasizing issues like migrant-related crime, domestic job competition, or the challenges faced by sanctuary cities like San Francisco, New York City, and Los Angeles

On the other hand, liberal-leaning outlets like CNN, MSNBC, or the Los Angeles Times often focus on stories highlighting the violence and persecution migrants face in their home countries, as well as inspiring accounts of Dreamers and other immigrants contributing positively to the economy and society.

Unless you have time to read both sets of outlets every morning, it’s likely that your opinion on immigration (and many other contentious topics) will reflect that of the publications you choose. This is where I believe AI can have the biggest impact.

### News Triangulation with AI

Let’s further boil down the immigration topic to one particular story — Trump’s appointment of Tom Homan as “Border Czar”. Homan is likely to make lots of headlines in 2025, so it feels appropriate to focus here.

In a quick Google search, we find that both CNN and FOX News have written articles on Mr. Homan. For simplicity, let’s assume CNN encapsulates the left-leaning take, and FOX News the right.

* CNN: [Trump announces Tom Homan, his former acting ICE director, will be administration’s ‘border czar’](<https://www.cnn.com/2024/11/10/politics/tom-homan-border-czar-ice-donald-trump/index.html>)
* FOX News: [Trump to appoint former ICE Director Tom Homan as next ‘border czar’: ‘nobody better at policing our borders’](<https://www.foxnews.com/politics/trump-appoint-former-ice-director-tom-homan-next-border-czar-nobody-better-policing-our-borders>)

Now, if you copy and paste the content from these articles into ChatGPT giving it context on each outlet’s bias, you can generate a summary of this story that includes both partisan perspectives. You can also ask the AI to look for _overlapping points_ from both articles on the assumption that these represent what both sides agree on — i.e. the main facts of the story.

When you hit enter, the output might look something like this:

**Summary (overlapping points)**

> Following his election victory, President-elect Donald Trump appointed former ICE chief, Tom Homan as ‘Border Czar’ to execute extensive deportation plans. Homan’s role, lacking direct oversight from Congress, emphasizes his ability to influence immigration policy significantly. Trump’s focus remains on mass deportations, framing it as key to national security.

**Left Perspective (from CNN)**

> Tom Homan’s appointment intensifies concerns about inhumane immigration practices reminiscent of the previous term, with immigration advocates fearing a rapid escalation towards mass deportation without due process. Homan’s public rhetoric, including aggressive threats to undocumented immigrants, signals a troubling disregard for the complexities surrounding immigration, raising alarm bells about potential human rights violations within immigrant communities if policies are enforced without compassion.

**Right Perspective (from FOX News)**

> Homan’s selection is a strategic move to enhance national security and restore integrity to immigration enforcement, reflecting a clear mandate from voters for stricter border policies after years of perceived laxity under the Biden administration. Homan’s previous experiences and established credibility within ICE are expected to galvanize border authority morale, suggesting a more effective response to immigration threats and a promise to prioritize the removal of individuals posing public safety risks without the chaos feared by some critics.

In my view, this approach is extremely powerful, because it allows readers to put partisan framing into context, while also highlighting the key facets of a story. By combining perspectives from across the spectrum, we can reduce the influence of bias and build a foundation of shared truths, fostering more informed and balanced discussions.
