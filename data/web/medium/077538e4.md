The AI startup arena is witnessing extremely high competition, as the advent of large language models has led to an incredibly large number of such startups. Despite this surge, [only 17% of Generative AI startups have already reached mid-stage](<https://www.cbinsights.com/research/generative-ai-funding-top-startups-investors-2023/>). The majority remain at an early stage or have not received venture capital funding.

On the one hand, GenAI promises high profits, giving LLM-powered applications impressive capabilities at minimal development costs. On the other hand, using LLM also introduces significant risks for a product. Here are two of them:

Firstly, **most users are not yet ready** to adopt the novel UX approaches that most developers are implementing in GenAI products and features. In the previous article, I described these challenges and explored strategies to avoid or overcome them:

## 6\. Short Lifespan of Applications Enhancing LLM Capabilities 🚫

Consider the scenario: an application is designed to enhance the quality of GPT model output when solving problems of a narrow class. For instance, it might employ a multi-agent approach to do it.

Another example: imagine an application that curates data for the model’s context (utilizing Retrieval-Augmented Generation or similar technologies) and intelligently fine-tunes LLM parameters (such as temperature or top_p) for specific task categories.

**Such applications may quickly become obsolete** if LLM vendors like OpenAI or Google integrate these functionalities directly into their services.

> _This may happen because LLMs are not like “application development platforms”. I mean that end users can harness the power of LLMs_** _without_** _necessarily relying on specialized applications._

Put simply, end users can leverage LLMs without our “products”. That is why:

* For instance, MacOS users can easily interact even with a **local** LLM if they have installed a versatile application such as LM Studio (supported on Mac M1 and later).
* Cloud-hosted LLMs are even more accessible through “web console” interfaces like OpenAI Playground, Google AI Studio, and Anthropic Console. These platforms offer **direct** access to LLMs, comparable to API access, i.e. without adding functions or imposing restrictions.
* Naturally, users gravitate towards applications developed by leading LLM vendors, such as ChatGPT or Claude.ai. While these applications incorporate some business-logic layer for file uploading, context management and so on, this layer remains “thin”. Nevertheless, most users are quite happy with ChatGPT, so third-party applications with “higher quality output” can’t compete here.

Indeed, ChatGPT users may still want some enhancements. However, OpenAI is well-positioned to address these gaps more effectively than third-party developers, as the gaps are minor.

✅ Rather than relying on the hypothesis that your product would address user pain points by enhancing LLM capabilities, consider pivoting your product strategy around this alternative assumption:

> **_In the near future, we’ll witness a substantial leap in the quality of LLMs and associated functionalities._**_This advancement will be significant enough to allow your product to gain popularity among the_** _majority_** _of potential users in your niche.
>  Here, I’m referring to a case where your product currently appeals only to innovators and early adopters — those willing to endure initial subpar outputs and manually fine-tune for satisfactory outcomes._

What’s the basis for this hypothesis?

Of course, tying a product’s success to future characteristics of a “platform” (in this case, LLMs) is always risky. However, I see no reason to suggest that GenAI fast development will significantly decelerate in 3–5 years:

1. Admittedly, the world is facing a **scarcity of training data** for models. However, the quality of LLM inference isn’t solely dependent on better data.
2. **Financial** risks, such as dramatic price hikes for commercial LLMs, are mitigated by the abundance of open-source alternatives.
3. The primary reason for LLM progress slowdown might lie in potential **legislative** hurdles. However, these risks are likely to be confined to individual countries rather than presenting global, insurmountable obstacles.

## 7\. Overconstraining LLM: A Recipe for Uncompetitive Applications 🚫

Most GenAI products seem to be aimed at constraining LLM capabilities, effectively diminishing the inherent versatility of LLMs:

* Website support chatbots are designed to address only relevant queries, rather than responding to all conceivable questions. This use case is quite expensive to implement.
* Similarly, corporate internal assistants follow the same pattern.
* The majority of GPTs (an OpenAI term) also serve as tools to constrain LLM capabilities. Their prompts define specific roles and tasks, while their data files make LLM responses narrower and more focused.

These constraints are designed to facilitate end-to-end solutions for specific user problems. While product convenience and completeness are crucial for market adoption, as I discussed in [my previous article](<https://medium.com/ai-advances/what-llm-powered-products-are-worth-developing-ux-and-adoption-perspectives-d9efcf444d50>), achieving these objectives in generative AI applications comes at a significant cost.

> _When an application_** _excessively constrains_** _LLM capabilities, a lot of resources are spent on such constraints’ implementation, while these resources that could be channeled into more valuable product features. This strategy_** _impedes_** _product development, ultimately compromising its market competitiveness. Moreover, customers do not experience the_** _wow factor_** _typically associated with a LLM, as its potential is severely limited by the product design._

✅ To sidestep this pitfall, I recommend shifting perspective: instead of viewing LLMs as tools for executing narrowly defined tasks, recognize their broader potential. In certain scenarios (as illustrated below), GenAI is already demonstrating the capacity to **achieve objectives with relative autonomy** rather than simply performing concrete tasks.

As we progress towards AGI, this ability to achieve broader goals is expected to improve significantly.

### Overconstraining LLM: Example

Let’s examine an educational product that constrains LLM capabilities. GenAI is often employed to evaluate essays and textual responses, providing feedback to students. However, the assignments themselves remain identical to pre-AI era ones, and the feedback quality requirements mirror those of human tutors. To meet these requirements, developers must craft intricate prompts:

* The prompt should handle every conceivable edge case. For instance, a student might request assistance instead of completing the task, or they might copy-paste internet content that’s related to the topic but irrelevant to the course curriculum.
* To replicate traditional human feedback, the prompt needs to incorporate examples of “student response — teacher feedback” pairs. From my experience, curating or crafting these few-shot prompts for numerous assignments is incredibly time-intensive, even when following a standardized template across all tasks.

> _The rationale behind these efforts is to constrain LLM outputs. Paradoxically,_**_resources are being expended on limiting LLM capabilities rather than leveraging them to benefit users_** _._

A large prompt for education (1200+ words) illustrates the effort required to constrain LLM

To reduce the effort, one may consider delegating prompt creation to AI and providing a “prompt template” only (as discussed in section 8 below). However, this approach might remain labor-intensive, as multiple templates are typically required.

✅ Consequently, beyond developing “LLM-powered complex prompt generators”, I advocate for **reimagining the initial problem formulation while preserving the core goals**.

In the above example about education, the primary students’ goal is to reinforce knowledge or acquire skills. This can be achieved through an AI-student dialogue, that is not constrained by specific assignments. Crafting prompts for such interactions is more efficient: often, defining the learning objectives and the AI’s persona are enough. Few-shot examples can primarily guide output style rather than output content.

This approach dramatically reduces the number of distinct “assignments”, with prompts being different in their learning objectives only.

## 8\. “GenAI Squared” Products: Unlocking Unfair Competitive Advantage ✅

1\. The previous section explored a scenario where GenAI products, built on “complex prompts for LLM”, aim to automate human tasks (like tutors grading essays). In such cases, I would propose evolving the product into an “LLM-powered **generator** of complex prompts for LLM”.

2\. Consider another scenario: a typical GenAI feature in an editor of some content (text, slides, images, etc.), such as Copilot in Microsoft Office. The use case for such a feature begins with a user having to enter a prompt that answers the question: “What should be changed, added or removed?”. While just a few words are enough, many users struggle with writing the prompt.

In such a situation, it’s worth thinking about helping the user **by generating the prompt** based on existing content and the user’s editing history. Compared to the previous example, the goal of generating prompts here is not only to save human effort, but also to personalize UX.

> _I call these prompt-generating products_** _“GenAI Squared” (GenAI²)_**_. In general, this category includes any solution employing LLMs_** _at two different “tiers”_** _, where upper-tier LLM outputs_** _influence the context_** _used by LLM at the lower tier. Ideally, the reverse influence also takes place._

In both examples above, the upper tier is about generating prompts. However, it can make sense to **generate any data** , or apply LLM for any purposes related to the lower tier.

3\. For example, the upper-tier GenAI could **model user behavior** , while the lower-tier GenAI guides users through personalized journeys based on the modeling outcomes.

4\. As the last example, let’s revisit the AI copilot for course creators, described in section 2 of the previous article [What LLM-powered Products Are Worth Developing?](</what-llm-powered-products-are-worth-developing-ux-and-adoption-perspectives-d9efcf444d50#031b>). Integrating that copilot (“upper-tier” generator) and LLM at the course delivery level (lower tier) could evolve the copilot into a GenAI² product. The copilot helps course authors **(re)generate prompts** to teach students with, **context** for those prompts, and **course content**. Furthermore, student interactions and feedback at the lower tier could be leveraged to fine-tune the generator in the copilot, creating a dynamic, self-improving system.

The example 4 is the product I am currently developing. It is in the MVP stage, and market experiments are about to begin, so **I cannot yet proof the efficiency of the GenAI² strategy** with my product. For me, this article is a way to adjust and refine my ideas around that product.

Nevertheless, there is a reason why GenAI² concept is helpful to you:

1\. As product managers, we must experiment with our markets ourselves. No one can tell us with certainty whether our products will become successful.

2\. GenAI-based products should go to market ASAP, so they cannot afford **too long** experimentation, as multiple pivots can result in losing momentum. That is why every **a priori** consideration helps us be faster by limiting the scope of experiments.

3\. Therefore, the logic behind the GenAI² strategy can help you even if it fails with my product.

You might find it challenging to **quickly** come up with a novel, valuable GenAI² product idea, even within a familiar market niche. Surprisingly, this challenge is a positive sign!

> _✅ In my opinion, the “GenAI squared” approach gives the product a kind of_** _unfair competitive advantage_** _. This stems from the inherent complexity in designing such a product — a challenge not just for you, but for competitors as well. Moreover, the implementation of such a product design often isn’t obvious. At the same time, the technical implementation does NOT demand big investment, as you’re harnessing the power of LLM across two tiers rather than one._

In essence, we’re investing our ingenuity and systems thinking into the product design, while enlisting creative developers to breathe life into our “ingenious” concept. This strategic approach constitutes our unfair competitive edge. The underlying two tiers are powered by LLMs coupled with **minimal** coding.
