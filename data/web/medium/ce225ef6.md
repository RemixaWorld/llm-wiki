## The uncomfortable truth about fp32 embeddings

Let’s say this out loud.

Most of us default to **float32 embeddings** because that’s what the models give us. We treat them as sacred. Untouchable. High precision or nothing.

But think about what we’re actually doing during retrieval.

We’re not solving physics equations.
We’re ranking documents.

And ranking, especially at the top of the funnel, is surprisingly forgiving.

That’s the crack this entire approach slips through.

## The trick

The setup is almost boring once you strip away the mystique:

**Binary search for recall. Int8 rescoring for precision.**

That’s it.

You don’t throw away quality.
You stage it.

You let cheap representations do the heavy lifting first, then bring precision back only where it matters.

Here’s how the full flow works.

## The inference strategy, step by step

Let’s walk through it slowly, like we’re debugging it together.

## Step 1: Embed the query

We start exactly where you expect.

A dense embedding model.
A standard fp32 vector.

Nothing fancy yet. This is your reference signal. The thing you’ll trust later.

## Step 2: Quantize the query to binary

Now comes the first shift in mindset.

We take that fp32 query embedding and quantize it to **binary**.
Same semantic direction.
32× smaller representation.

This step is fast. Almost suspiciously fast.

## Step 3: Search a binary index

This is where scale suddenly stops being scary.

Binary indices are tiny.
They’re fast.
And they live happily in memory.

Instead of asking,
“Which of 40 million documents is best?”

## Why this works (and why it feels wrong at first)

It feels wrong because we’re taught to protect precision at all costs.

But retrieval isn’t classification.
It’s not regression.
It’s not even generation.

It’s triage.

Binary embeddings are phenomenal at **ruling things out**.
Int8 embeddings are great at **ranking contenders**.
Fp32 is best when you really need nuance — which is rare once you’re down to 40 docs.

So instead of paying fp32 costs everywhere, you stage precision like a spotlight.

Wide beam first.
Narrow beam later.

## Storage and memory

Let’s compare this to a standard fp32 setup.

## Float32 retrieval (baseline)

* RAM: ~180GB
* Disk: ~180GB
* Speed: slow
* Cost: painful

## Binary + int8 retrieval

* Binary index: **32× smaller**
* Int8 embeddings: **4× smaller**
* RAM usage: ~6GB
* Disk usage: ~45GB

And here’s the kicker.

You only keep the **binary index in memory**.

That’s where the real savings compound.

## Performance trade-offs

Let’s not pretend there’s zero loss.

Here’s what the experiments show:

``` | Representation | Index Size | Speed | Performance || ---------------- | ----------- | ---------------- | ----------- || float32 | 1× | 1× | 100% || int8 / uint8 | 4× smaller | up to 4× faster | ~99.3% || binary / ubinary | 32× smaller | up to 45× faster | ~96% | ```

Now the important part.

By retrieving **more candidates** via binary search and rescoring them with int8, you recover **~99% of fp32 quality**.

That last 1% costs you almost nothing.

## What this changes, practically

Honestly? A lot.

It means:

* You don’t need GPUs for massive semantic search
* You can ship serious retrieval on commodity hardware
* You stop paying a “luxury tax” for fp32 everywhere
* You can scale collections without rewriting everything

And maybe the bigger shift is mental.

We’ve been treating embeddings like fragile glass.

They’re not.

They’re tools.
And tools work best when you stop using one hammer for every nail.

## If you take one thing from this

Think in stages.

Don’t ask, _“What’s the most accurate representation?”_
Ask, _“What’s accurate enough at this point in the pipeline?”_

Binary first.
Int8 second.
Precision last.

Once you see retrieval this way, going back to brute-force fp32 everywhere feels… kind of irresponsible.

And maybe that’s the real lesson hiding here.

Not that quantization is clever.

But that **engineering judgment still beats brute force** , even in the age of giant models.
