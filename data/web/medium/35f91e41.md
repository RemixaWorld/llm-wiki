## So, Why Go Multimodal?

In real world often data in spread across multiple modalities like text, images, audio, and video. And most of the time a RAG system could give a far better answer if it could pull information from all modalities. In sentiment analysis, images or emoji patterns often invert the nuance of a message that words alone can’t reveal. Consider dynamic web search, finding the right answer may depend on a map snippet, a chart, or a podcast segment. Only systems capable of understanding and retrieving across modalities can deliver robust, context-rich answers with trustworthy evidence

In practice, it’s about a system that can extract context from all formats and evidence. A radiology report is not simply referencing clinical notes, but is “reading” and understanding the X-ray image. For a complex question on a scientific paper, synthesizing written argument, and information presented on a chart or diagram. Identifying a screenshot a customer has attached to the query and linking it to documentation in the customer support system.

## Anatomy of a Multimodal RAG Pipeline:

To truly appreciate what sets multimodal RAG apart, let’s dissect its core pipeline. What actually happens behind the scenes when an AI system answers a question by drawing from text, images, audio, or even video? Understanding this end-to-end process is very important to grasp the complexity of modern multimodal RAG systems.

For a full view of what differentiates multimodal RAG, let me dissect its core pipeline. So, let’s understand what actually happens behind the scenes when a system’s AI tries to answer questions using text, images, audio or video

Anatomy of a MultiModal system [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.12323>)]

1. **Query Understanding:**

The user query could be a question in text with an attached image, or a combined input such as a snippet of audio plus a written request. The first challenge for the pipeline is to encode all elements of the query irrespective of modality and to use dedicated encoders that translate each format (text, image, audio) into a common semantic space. This multifaceted representation also ensures the next step is not just a description of words, but the entire array of context.

**2\. Retrieval:**

Next, the system searches a dynamically-indexed, multimodal knowledge base for information. This selects candidate documents, images, videos, and other formats based on similarity scoring or learned relevance models in accordance with the user’s intent.

**3\. Fusion:**

The stage is evidence synthesis. The retrieved results from multiple sources are not only brought together but fused through score fusion, cross-attention or projection into embedding spaces. This section aims to bring signals from the two modes together so that downstream generator can identify across them like resembling textual description in a image or in a video segment.

**4\. Augmentation:**
Here, the pipeline refines itself using iterative feedback, user guidance, and self-reflection to filter, supplement, or re-rank the retrieved data. Techniques like context enrichment and iterative retrieval make sure only the most high-quality contexts are used in the final output.

**5\. Generation:**
Finally, the magic of RAG meets generation. An advanced multimodal language model takes the fused, augmented context and crafts a grounded, evidence-based response. This output can itself be multimodal, a textual explanation supplemented by highlighted regions in an image, or a sequence of annotated video frames.

## The Impossible Trinity Challenge of Multimodal RAG

Addressing multimodal RAG isn’t just traditional RAG plus images. It‘s fundamentally more complex, requiring solutions to three interconnected challenges that researchers call the _impossible trinity._

**Challenge 1: Unified Multimodal Representation**

How do you create a single system that handles text, tables, images, and equations while preserving what makes each modality unique?

**Challenge 2: Structure-Aware Decomposition**

Documents are usually carefully structured contents where spatial relationships carry meaning. A figure caption doesn’t just describe an image, it provides the conceptual framework for understanding it. Equations aren’t standalone; they’re linked to surrounding definitions. Break these connections, and you’ve destroyed the knowledge graph.

**Challenge 3: Cross-Modal Retrieval**

The hardest problem is that answers often require reasoning _across_ modalities. An insight might start with a textual claim, reference a table for evidence, and conclude with a visual demonstration. Traditional systems can’t navigate these cross-modal connections because they treat each modality as a separate systems.

## Breakthroughs and Innovations in Multi-Modal Methodology:

### 1\. How to do better Retrieval for MultiModal content?

Real-world queries are rarely fully textual searches. A user might upload a photo and add a short text description. The system should return images that look like the reference photo _and_ respect the details mentioned in the text. Maybe the user wants the same object but in a different color. Or in a different setting. Both inputs matter.

Traditional search systems struggle here. They don’t know how much weight to give each modality. Should the image matter more? The text? or both?

**1\. Efficient Similarity Search:**

Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) is a common approach. It matches queries and documents by measuring how similar their representations are. Models like [_BanditMIPS_](<https://openreview.net/pdf?id=FKkkdyRdsD>) and [MUST](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06397>) use this method to keep results both accurate and fast.

Press enter or click to view image in full size

Overview of MUST [[Source](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.06397>)]

Some common frameworks to solve this problem are:

1. _Multi-streamed Retrieval (MR):_

This method treats each input separately. It runs several independent searches and then tries to merge the results. Though it’s simple, it’s slow and inaccurate. Picking the best items from several large pools is hard when you don’t know which modality carries more meaning.

_2\. Joint Embedding (JE):_

This approach fuses all inputs into one vector right away. A single early fusion embedding. But this can blur important details. The model may lose track of which features matter most. This results in confusing embeddings and weaker retrieval.

_3\. The MUST Framework:_

MUST addresses these issues with a hybrid fusion strategy that works across multiple stages. The idea is to combine signals without losing their individuality.

Here are the core pieces:

**1\. Multi-Vector Representation:** Instead of representing each object with a single vector, MUST use multiple high-dimensional vectors, one per modality. This gives a richer, clearer picture of the data.

**2\. Vector Weight Learning:** MUST include a small model that learns how important each modality should be. These weights improve the joint similarity score between queries and contexts. They capture the _importance of the modality itself_ , not the specifics of the content. This helps the system generalize well across different queries. Once learned, the weights help build a single concatenated vector for search. Users can also override the weights if they want more control.

**3\. Fused Indexing and Joint Search:** Instead of building separate indexes, like MR does, MUST builds one fused proximity-graph index. It uses the learned weights to blend the modalities in a balanced way. This eliminates the need for slow post-search merging. It leads to much faster joint search.

Experiments show significant improvements: more than 10× faster search and roughly 93% better accuracy compared to baseline methods.

**2\. Modality-Centric Retrieval:**

Real-world questions often involve images, videos, or documents, and systems need to be ready for them all.

* Text-centric models like BM25, [MiniLM](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.10957>), and ColBERT focus on finding the best matches in written content. Some work better for short text snippets, others for locating precise details in longer texts.
* **_Vision-centric models_** use tools like CLIP, BLIP, and ALIGN. They can match queries with images, even if those queries are written in text. There are also models like [EchoSight](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.83.pdf>) and [ImgRet](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.771.pdf>) that let you search by using an image as the query.

For visual questions such as “When was the 1st ascent of this mountain?”, visual-only search methods consider image similarity only, ignoring the textual details of the accompanying article. By incorporating multimodal reranking, the correct entry, accounting for both visual and textual information, can be accurately identified.[[Source](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.83.pdf>)]

In [EcoSight](<https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.83.pdf>), given a visual question with an image, the retriever searches the reference image in the knowledge base for top k similar images to get their corresponding Wikipedia Entries. After changing the granularity to sections, all the sections of retrieved entries are then reranked with the maximum pairwise similarity of their textual embeddings and the reference image+question’s Q-Former query tokens. The top reranked section will be utilized as RAG prompt for the LLM to generate the ultimate answer

**Video-focused systems** such as [iRAG](<https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3627673.3680088>), [VideoRAG](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.13093>), and MV-Adapter are built for handling dynamic content. Some are good at dealing with sequences, others can handle longer videos or pick key moments.

For example, the Video-RAG pipeline contains three key phases. In the query decouple phase, the LVLM is prompted to generate a retrieval request for auxiliary texts. Next, in the auxiliary text generation and retrieval phase, the video is processed in parallel to extract three types of textual information (OCR, ASR, and object detection), and the relevant text is retrieved as the auxiliary text. **Document-based retrievers** look beyond simple text. ColPali, [M3DocVQA](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04952>), and mPLUG-DocOwl can also search tables, figures, and even the arrangement of elements on a page.

For example, [M3DOCRAG](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04952>) framework consists of three stages: document embedding, page retrieval, and question answering. In document embedding, visual embeddings are extracted with ColPali to represent each page from all PDF documents. In page retrieval, top-K pages of high relevance (MaxSim scores) are retreived with text queries. In an open-domain setting, we create approximate page indices for faster search. In question answering, we conduct visual question answering with multi-modal LM (e.g. Qwen2-VL) to obtain the final answer.

**3\. Re-ranking and Selection:**

Finding the right items is only half the story. Deciding which results actually matter takes smart filtering.

* **Optimized Example Selection:** Some systems use techniques like clustering or selecting key moments (for video) to make sure the results aren’t just similar, they’re useful and varied. Hybrid RAG and clustering-based selection are examples.
* **Relevance Scoring:** Simple similarity isn’t enough. Metrics like SSIM, cross-correlation, and BERTScore help sort the evidence by how well it actually answers the query. Some systems train neural nets to be even better at ranking results.
* **Filtering:** Not everything retrieved is helpful. Hard negative mining, consensus scoring, and dynamic filters get rid of noisy or irrelevant content.

### 2\. How Do Multimodal RAG Systems Merge Information?

Finding information is only the first step. To answer complex questions, an AI needs to combine facts from lots of different content types like text, images, and even audio clips. So how do these systems put everything together in a way that makes sense?

**2.1 Score Fusion:**
One practical approach is called score fusion. Each piece of evidence, a chunk of text or an image, gets a _relevance score_ showing how closely it matches the user’s question. The system can then combine these scores so that the most helpful pieces, across all types of media, come first.​

**2.2 Cross-Attention:**
Now, consider questions that need more than stacking sources. Sometimes context in an image changes the meaning of a sentence or vice versa. Cross-attention models help by allowing the AI to focus on important parts of each input. It pays special attention to words related to objects in a photo, or aligns sounds with their descriptions. This helps the AI understand and blend information, so nothing gets lost in translation across modalities.

**2.3 Alignment Methods:**
Alignment is about translating different data types into a common language so they can be compared and merged. Methods like CLIP and BLIP create joint spaces where text, images, and even video live together as mathematical points. When the system sees a question or a set of evidence, everything gets projected into this shared space, making it easier to find real connections.

**2.4 Unified Frameworks and Projections:**
Instead of separate pipelines for each type of data, new frameworks let all forms feed into one supermodel. Some use a joint _projection space_ where each modality can be mapped and compared on equal terms. This keeps things consistent and makes the system’s reasoning easier to follow.​ There are three main approaches I see in the way the contents are unified.

**_2.4.1 Unifying by converting all content to Text:(UniRAG):_**

Overall framework of UniRaG [[Source](<https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10535103>)]

UniRAG is a three-stage framework built for multimodal question answering (MMQA). The idea is simple. Bring everything into one format: text.

_Stage 1: Convert all inputs to text._
Images are turned into captions using models like LLaVA. Tables are flattened into linear text. Once this is done, every modality — visual, tabular, or otherwise — shares the same form.

_Stage 2: Retrieve useful context._
A cross-encoder scores each question–document pair. The system then selects the top-k most relevant documents.

_Stage 3: Generate the answer._
An LLM reads the collected text and produces the final response.

By treating all content as text, UniRAG keeps the pipeline simple. Everything moves through the same retrieval and generation flow.

**_2.4.2 Unifying by converting all content to images:_**

[**M3DOCRAG**](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04952>) (Multi-modal Multi-page Multi-Document Retrieval-Augmented Generation) is designed for complex document visual question answering (DocVQA). Many documents contain diagrams, charts, or multi-column layouts that don’t survive OCR cleanly. Turning them into pure text loses important clues. So M3DOCRAG keeps the visual structure intact.

It works in three steps:

_1\. Encode pages as visual embeddings._
Each document page becomes a visual feature vector using a multimodal retriever such as ColPali.

_2\. Retrieve the top-K relevant pages._
The system compares the query against all page embeddings. It uses efficient indexing, like inverted files, to scale to large document sets.

_3\. Generate the answer._
A multimodal model, such as Qwen2-VL, looks at the retrieved page images along with the text query. It then produces the answer by reading both the visual cues and the question.

This approach shines when the answer depends on non-text elements like charts, figures, or spatial layout. It also helps when information is scattered across multiple pages. Text-only RAG often misses these details, but an image-based pipeline keeps them visible.

**_2.4.3 Unifying across modalities:_**

RAG-Anything unifies content as **interconnected knowledge entities** and introduces a **dual-graph construction strategy** to capture both cross-modal relationships and textual semantics within a single unified representation. To retrieve information efficiently, the system utilizes a **cross-modal hybrid retrieval mechanism** that strategically combines structural knowledge navigation (exploiting explicit graph relationships for multi-hop reasoning) with semantic similarity matching (using dense vector representations). This integrated design enables RAG-Anything to seamlessly access knowledge across all modalities, delivering superior performance, especially on challenging long documents where relevant evidence is dispersed across multiple modalities and sections

### 3\. Augmentation, Reasoning, and Generation

It’s not enough to simply gather and merge data. Getting truly helpful answers takes more.

**Context Enrichment and Adaptive Retrieval:**

AI models often need more than what they pull up at first glance. New systems use feedback, either from the user or the model itself, to keep looking for better context when answers are weak or incomplete. This is called iterative retrieval, and it ensures the information is not just close, but specifically chosen to clarify tricky or multi-part questions.

**Source Attribution:**

Source attribution means the model doesn’t just answer, it shows where each part of the answer came from (like linking a sentence to a chart or naming the document it used). This boosts trust and makes it easier for users to verify facts.

**In-Context Learning, Instruction Tuning, and Loss Functions:**

Modern systems are constantly learning from the way people interact with them. By rewarding the model for correct steps (loss functions), helping it follow instructions more closely (instruction tuning), and letting it learn from examples presented in the current conversation (in-context learning), these models are getting better at reasoning and explaining, not just retrieving.

## How do we check if a multimodal RAG system actually does what we want?

That’s where benchmarks and metrics come in. Benchmarks are like test tracks for AI. They challenge models with tough tasks: visual reasoning, finding facts outside training data, or answering multi-step questions from mixed media. The [M2RAG](<https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17297v1>) benchmark is designed to measure how well a model can handle both text and images at once. Others, like [MRAG-Bench](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08182>) or [VisDoMBench](<https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10704>), focus on visual problem-solving. Dynamic benchmarks such as [Dyn-VQA](<https://huggingface.co/datasets/zhzhen23/DynVQA/blob/fab64c195099a550956b3bf5a0edebea1cc0278c/README.md>) and ScienceQA look for smart multi-hop reasoning, where the system needs to connect several clues to answer right.​

Evaluating whether our AI application really gets the question and answer relevantly is not easy. You need fair metrics for every type of information. For text, things like BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, and Exact Match give a sense of how close the AI’s answers are to what people expect. For images, there’s CIDEr, SPICE, and CLIP Score, which test if the system’s labels or captions match what’s actually in the picture. FID and KID check image quality, while BERTScore helps with semantic alignment (making sure the meaning is preserved). Audio can be checked by metrics like Fréchet Audio Distance. There are even special domain metrics, for example, geodesic distance for maps or clinical relevance in healthcare.

The tough part about evaluation in Multimodal systems is juggling text, images, audio, and video all at once. Each type has different standards for accuracy. Sometimes, a model gets the words right but misses details in an image. Other times, it draws connections across formats but struggles with details. As researchers add more modalities, testing gets harder, but it matters. Without careful evaluation, it’s easy to miss mistakes or subtle gaps that multiply in real-world use.​

## Approach 2: RAG Anything Framework

Instead of relying on several separate tools, RAG-Anything handles all content types in one place. Text, images, tables, equations, they all flow through the same system.

Traditional RAG pipelines often fall short when dealing with non-text elements like images and charts. RAG-Anything avoids this by supporting true multimodal retrieval from the start.

Users can search across documents that mix text with diagrams, tables, or mathematical expressions. Let’s see how we could implement it.

## What are the Open Challenges in Multi-Modal RAG?

Even with so much progress, multimodal RAG is far from solved. Some of the toughest problems are just coming into focus as systems get deployed into production.

**Cross-Modal Alignment:**
Connect the dots between text, images, video, and audio to answer user question is harder than it sounds. Each modality has its own structure and way of representing meaning. Aligning an X-ray with its written report or matching a section of a podcast to a news article takes more than basic similarities. Even the best models sometimes misinterpret or completely miss clues hidden in other formats.​

**Relevance:**
Finding something relevant in a search is only step one. Knowing if it’s the _right_ evidence takes much more. Filtering out distracting or misleading snippets, and ensuring only the most useful information stays is a big hurdle. Spurious matches still trip up top models today.

**Explainability:**
Users want answers they can trust. They want to know why did the model pick that image or citation? Can we trace an answer back to its source, especially when data is blended from multiple types? Right now, multimodal RAG often struggles to make its choices clear. Improving transparency remains a key area for research.

**Evaluating Real-World Performance:**
Benchmarks can help you to test and assess your AI system to some extent, but real life is messy. Inputs are noisy, mixed, and often ambiguous. Production-deployed MultiModal system needs to be monitored relentlessly through a strong monitoring framework.
