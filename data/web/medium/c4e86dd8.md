Despite undeniable progress, the principles of AI training have remained consistently stagnant over the last few years. Now, a group of researchers proposes an innovative approach that modifies the principles of Generative AI learning:

> **Training models to reorder their future instead of predicting it as a way to improve model “intelligence”**.

This probably doesn't make sense to you right now, but Token Order Prediction (TOP) could be the next big thing in large-scale AI training.

But to understand this, we need to answer why most models these days, from ChatGPT to Nobel-worthy AlphaFold, **have a fundamentally identical mechanism at heart.**

And, funnily enough, we’ll be using Rick Astley for all this.

## LLMs in First Principles

At this point, you already know that ChatGPT is a Large Language Model (LLM) and that these models work by _“predicting the next word in a sequence.”_

Well, funnily enough, let’s clarify this because it’s a wrong statement.

### Handling Uncertainty

LLMs work with probability distributions, something I’ve tackled more deeply recently [in this piece](</@ignacio.de.gregorio.noblejas/the-one-mistake-everyone-makes-in-ai-0d5a44df6c74>), so I’ll cut to the chase in this one.

They don’t actually predict the next word, **but the probability distribution over the next word**. In layman’s terms, they rank the entire vocabulary of known tokens (words or subwords) based on their statistical likelihood as next words in the sequence. This means that, **in every prediction, they consider all possible words they know as the next one, not just one**.

[Source](<https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-do-language-modelsllm-work-we-call-chatgpt-mishra-fdqsc/>)

This is an important distinction because it not only prevents the model from collapsing constantly on identical sequences (e.g., predicting ‘am’ every time it sees ‘I’), **but also incentivizes model creativity** , helping us see how “certain” the model is about the prediction.

> The idea that models “consider” all possible next words in every prediction is an idea that will become important later, so hold that thought.

The thing about this process is that it can be very expensive, computationally speaking, and the larger the model is, the more compute requirements it has, **driving up not only costs but also latency** (the model takes longer to give an output).

However, the primary reason AI models can be costly is that the Transformer, the underlying architecture of most modern models, **has computationally taxing inductive biases** , prompting us to seek alternatives, such as the original one we are discussing today.

So without further ado, let’s start defining this bizarre sentence you’ve just read.

### _What assumptions are we making?_

When training AIs, your job has two main tasks:

1. Choosing the data you will train the model on
2. Choose the model’s architecture

Focusing on the latter, each model architecture introduces certain “inductive biases”, **which are implicit assumptions we are making with the choice of architecture** (or assumptions the model makes to learn).

> In other words, depending on the architecture, the model learns different stuff because it assumes different hypotheses about the data.

> We call them “inductive biases” because, in AI training, we go “against” the original scientific method of deductive reasoning (from the general to the specific), and instead use the power of Big Data to go from the specific to the general.
>
> That is, seeing a lot of data to find the patterns that generalize, like seeing a lot of things falling to the floor as a way to infer laws of gravity. However, to make these inferences, to make this problem tractable, we must make some assumptions about the data, which we term inductive biases.

For example, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which are historically used for computer vision (processing images), exhibit a ‘locality inductive bias’.

By the use of a filter, they identify local patterns in the image, as shown below, where the ‘eye’ filter identifies where the eyes are located (this is obviously a simplified example).

[Source](<https://www.vizuaranewsletter.com/p/classical-filters-and-convolution?hide_intro_popup=true>)

We say this has locality bias because the model is assuming that, by the use of a small filter, it must concentrate on identifying filter-sized patterns. In plain English, **by opting for a CNN, we are implicitly assuming that patterns are ‘local’,** meaning pixels that are closer together matter more to each other than pixels placed in other parts of the image; as every pixel’s ‘**direct attention-to-patterns span** ’, the span of pixels that the pixel can identify patterns with, is the size of the convolutional filter.

This makes CNNs a really bad choice for text for example, which can have very long-range dependencies.

* In the example above, if a pixel has a hidden pattern with a pixel that is outside the filter (like a pixel from the nose and a pixel from the eye in the example above), this model won’t notice the pattern at first, or at least not that effectively.
* In examples like books, the name of a particular character in the story might only be mentioned in Chapter 2, but it is an important fact to recall in Chapter 13. CNNs are not a good option here because they are most likely to miss this relationship (they lack global recall).

> **Technical clarification:** As every layer of the CNN takes as input the output of the previous layer, and the convolution filter aggregates the information from a previous layer (unless the very special case of 1x1 filters with no dimensionality reduction), **there is some pixel-to-pixel communication across all pixels in the image.** Put simply, **despite the locality bias, CNNs can identify larger-than-the-filter patterns.** However,but the connections are not direct, which reinforces the locality bias assumption; **the most important patterns are local**.

However, this choice of architecture has become irrelevant these days, **as almost every model on the planet is a Transformer** , another type of architecture. ChatGPT, Gemini, image models, video models, audio models; every model has the same underlying architecture currently, **_even the Nobel-worthy AlphaFold!_**

_But what does the Transformer have that makes it so powerful?_

> And the answer is probably the most important discovery in AI over the last ten years: **the attention mechanism and its global attention inductive bias** , which is equally a blessing and a curse.

### The global attention bias

The main reason one single architecture, the Transformer, has taken the world by storm is that the attention mechanism underlying these models has a **global attention bias**.

Put simply, a Transformer is what we call a mixing operator that makes the different parts of the input sequence ‘talk’ to each other.

> We call the attention mechanism a “mixing operator” because it allows words in the sequence (in the case of text) share information with each other, which allows the model to build an understanding of the sequence’s meaning.

The convolution filter discussed earlier is also a mixing operator, but what makes Transformers unique (and superior) is that they make the implicit assumption **that all parts of the sequence** (the entire sequence of words from beginning to end) **matter in order to predict the next word.** This is the global attention bias I mentioned earlier.

Unlike other architectures that would be tempted to just look at the most recent parts of the sequence to predict the next word to save up compute effort (i.e., recency bias, the assumption that recent words matter most), **Transformers treat every single word in the sequence as equally important** , an assumption that has proven essential to the performance of models like ChatGPT.

> Recouping the book example we mentioned earlier, the Transformer’s global recall would allow it to remember the character’s name from Chapter 2 even when approaching the ending chapters.

_But what do we mean by this? Why is this such a crucial and powerful feature of Transformers?_ Besides ‘universal recall’ (i.e., remember everything) we have just mentioned, it also identifies long-range patterns like no other architecture.

For instance, the sequence below describes a really famous city without explicitly mentioning it:

> “This city, straddling a river whose islands nurtured a medieval university and whose banks now glow under lamplight, rose from royal palaces and revolutionary barricades into a capital where a once-reviled iron tower became a global emblem, a Gothic cathedral set the standard for soaring buttresses, a hilltop basilica crowns a quarter of painters and poets, and a palace turned museum houses the world’s most visited treasures, all linked by boulevards carved in the 19th century into geometric order, lined with cafés facing the street, bakeries cracking fresh bread, and wine flowing from every region, while its thinkers shaped democracy, its artists redefined beauty, its language carried diplomacy, and its nightly reflections on the river made it synonymous with light and romance,”

It’s packed with crucial information from beginning to end, but in a rather elusive way, and it’s the overall description that gives away what city it is, Paris. Here, **reading a single sentence is hardly enough to know what city it’s talking about.**

> However, a Transformer would still predict Paris easily because **it reads the entire sequence with equal effort** , enabling it to capture all Paris-sounding facts; that’s the magic (and the curse) of Transformers.

Conversely, recency-biased architectures like Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), the previous state-of-the-art, which are more inclined towards the latter parts of the sequence, would struggle much more in this particular instance.

> Many pundits interpret the Transformer’s superpowers from the perspective of ‘The Bitter Lesson’, a highly influential essay by legendary researcher Rick Sutton. This essay basically translates to **compute increases being the ultimate driver of progress**. As the Transformer is more computationally intensive than other architectures (due to the global attention bias we have described), this is another framing to explain its superiority.

_And why am I telling you all this?_ Well, although Transformers are awesome, **they are unsurprisingly incredibly expensive**.

### It works… but at what cost

**The main drawbacks of this impressive performance are equally impressive cost and latency**. As the Transformer assumes no words can be spared, meaning the entire sequence is relevant, a lot of computation is required, **and this computation grows a lot as the sequence grows** (because there is an increasing amount of words to ‘attend to’).

> Most AI Labs today use some variant of the original attention mechanism, mainly [Grouped-query attention](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13245>). But these variants are primarily to save up memory; they are still strongly compute-intensive.

But perhaps even more importantly, **these hefty computation requirements slow down the inference phase**. When the model needs to respond to a user, **it can take a considerable amount of time to answer, with latency growing proportionally to sequence length** , significantly worsening the overall experience.

To mitigate this, in addition to caching, which we won’t be discussing today, we use **multi-token prediction** , a technique in which we teach models to predict not only the next word but also those that follow at the same time.

In practice, our model has multiple “heads” (think of this as a hydra) where each head is in charge of predicting one of the next words. If the model has four heads, it can predict up to four tokens for every inference round, considerably speeding up the entire process (despite making the overall results worse, more on that in a minute).

[Source](</@techsachin/multi-token-prediction-improves-over-next-token-prediction-for-faster-and-better-llms-bd5c4f303b3d>)

In practice, though, the trade-off is so significant (model performance falls if we increase the number of heads too much)**that we only use this multi-token prediction system during training** , disabling the other heads during inference, **with the model essentially behaving as it always has (one word at a time)**.

_But what’s the intuition?_

[Pioneered by DeepSeek](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19437>), the idea was simple: by introducing an “oracle bias”, **the assumption that by forcing a model to predict multiple tokens** , not just the next, but the ‘k’ next ones, **we are forcing it to “look into the future”** , or plan ahead of the immediate prediction, making the current prediction better by making the model also consider the words that are coming next.

> A good example of why this could be helpful is poetry. If we want verses to rhyme, we usually think of the last word first, ensuring it rhymes with the last word of the previous verse, which, in effect, influences the rest of the verse, even though the other words come before.

Fascinatingly, non-MTP models have also been proven to ‘plan ahead’ in poetry tasks, as proven by Anthropic’s attention circuits.

And while MTP has become a staple of modern AI training, with illustrious examples like the mentioned DeepSeek latest models,**it does have a performance tax if we increase the number of prediction heads too much**.

> The reason why multi-token prediction struggles with many heads is that the model has to predict not only the present but also the future. Imagine yourself having to predict not only the immediate word you are about to say, but an entire paragraph at once. You may be able to predict the next four words you are about to say, but predicting 20 is another story.

> Therefore, we found ourselves at a crossroads. MTP helps models _“think better”,_ but it is a very hard learning objective for the model, which severely limits its scale.

_But what if there were another way to make models that focus less on the next prediction, without paying too much of an accuracy tax?_

Enter Token-Order Prediction, a technique that rethinks how models learn.

## From Looking into the Future to Relative Rankings

Token-Order Prediction, or TOP,[ is a novel technique](<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19228>) that proposes a two-way learning approach for models.

### NTP & TOP

To recap, until now, we have had two main ways of training autoregressive LLMs like ChatGPT:

* **Next-Token prediction (NTP).** The standard approach involves the model predicting a single token every time.
* **Multi-Token Prediction (MTP).** As with DeepSeek’s models, the model is trained to predict the next ‘k’ tokens in every prediction.

TOP’s researchers introduce a third option, in which the model, during training, has two objectives instead of just one (one of the two above).

1. **NTP.** The model still has to correctly predict the exact next token, as always.
2. **Token-Order Prediction.** But the model also has to correctly guess the relative order of its token vocabulary in upcoming predictions.

I know, that makes literally zero sense. But it will in a minute, drawing inspiration from Rick Astley. Yes, you read that right.

### Rick Astley’s AI Guest Appearance

Let’s discuss a very small case of an LLM that is a bit too obsessed with Rick Astley, to the point that it can only speak seven words of its famous song _Never Gonna Give You Up_ : ‘down’, ‘give’, ‘gonna’, ‘let’, ‘never’, ‘up’, and ‘you.’

As with any LLM, each of these tokens is assigned a vocabulary index, in this case from 0 to 7. Then, we give the model the first four words of the famous chorus, _“never gonna give you”_ , and the model has to predict the next one.

Up until now, everything has been the same as before. But here’s where changes emerge:

* On the one hand, the model has to predict what the next word is, in this case assigning the highest probability to ‘up’. This is the same learning objective as always.
* Simultaneously,**the model is tasked with reordering all the tokens in its vocabulary in ‘proximity’,** with proximity being how close they are to the current prediction.

The image above illustrates this, but it can be a little bit hard to understand. Let me clarify.

On the top-right-hand side, the model correctly identifies ‘up’ as the next token. In the bottom-right-hand side, **the model is simultaneously signaling preference for the next tokens too** , assigning a proximity score (the ‘bluer’ the score, the higher it is) to the next four words (including ‘up’), and infinity to the rest (it doesn’t bother reordering all words, just the more likely ones).

For example, if we look at the prediction at the bottom, it has the following proximity score vector:

* _[-∞, -∞, 1, 0, 2, 3, -∞]_ , where each number is the proximity score assigned to each word in the model’s vocabulary, listed below:
* _[‘down’, ‘give’, ‘gonna’, ‘let’, ‘never’, ‘up’, ‘you’]._ As the model chooses the sixth, fifth, third, and fourth words as the closest (highest proximity scores), this means the model is defining a preference for **_“up, never gonna let”_** as continuation to the original sequence **_“never gonna give you”,_** which if I’m not mistaken, is precisely how Rick Astley forever hooked our brains to the song.

> To put this in plain English, TOP is just a learning goal for the model in which it not only has to be a good next-word predictor, but also being able to suggest possible next words by **reordering candidates based on proximity**.

At first, it sounds very similar to MTP, but has a crucial difference: **MTP forces models to give absolute preference, the model is saying exactly what word comes next** , next, and next, **giving it zero margin for error** (the training regime will only reward the model if it gets all the tokens correct), **while TOP is a softer learning demand** , we aren’t asking the model to nail the exact ‘k’ word that’s coming next, **but reward it whenever it develops a ‘taste for what comes next’.**

At first, this may read as a worse solution… **_we are making things easier for the model!_**

> And while that is true, that’s the entire point; a crucial lesson in AI is that you have to find a learning objective that is challenging but not impossible, otherwise the model doesn’t learn because the objective is too hard.

In the paper’s results, the model outperforms both NTP and MTP across all scales, even for the larger 7-billion-parameter model, a great sign that this technique is scalable.

## Algorithmic Improvements Still Matter

To summarise, today we have taken a deep look at Token-Order Prediction, a new _auxiliary training objective_ that helps the model learn better internal representations by ranking upcoming tokens instead of exactly predicting them.

The method is more straightforward for the model, leading to greater understanding and better overall performance.

Questions remain, though. For starters, **this new learning mechanism needs to sustain the test of time**. Labs will surely give it a try and adopt it if it makes sense for them, and drop it otherwise.

Additionally, **it remains a question whether this method can be used to accelerate inference**.

If the ranking system is truly powerful and usually adheres to the eventual order of next tokens, **we could potentially automatically decode all the ranked next words** , allowing us to predict several tokens in each prediction. We can’t say for sure, but this is an exciting question to answer.

The key takeaway for me is that we still have significant room for improvement in the algorithmic model layer. Moreover,**the principle in AI that you get what you optimize for remains very much alive** , and these types of choices continue to matter.
