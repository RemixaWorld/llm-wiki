**Scaling Test-Time Compute: Unlocking Efficient Performance in Language Models**

**Introduction** The demand for more powerful language models has traditionally been met by increasing their size, resulting in models that require extensive computational resources during both training and inference. However, recent research suggests that **smaller models** can achieve **competitive performance** by **increasing computational effort during test-time inference**. This shift emphasizes **optimizing how resources are allocated during inference rather than solely scaling up model size.**

This blog explores how techniques like _compute-optimal scaling_ and _Diverse Verifier Tree Search (DVTS)_ enable smaller models to punch above their weight. Additionally, we integrate findings from DeepMind’s comparative study of _process-based_ and _outcome-based supervision_ for language models, highlighting key techniques and outcomes.

**Understanding Test-Time Compute** Test-time compute refers to the computational resources allocated during the inference phase, where a pre-trained language model generates outputs based on user input. Traditionally, both training and inference compute have been tightly coupled, but decoupling them opens the door to optimizing resource allocation differently for these two stages.

**For example:**

* A small model might generate multiple candidate responses.
* An external verifier or scoring mechanism can then evaluate these responses.
* The final answer is chosen based on the highest score or consensus.

This approach avoids the need for excessively large models while still delivering high-quality outputs.

**Compute-Optimal Scaling** Compute-optimal scaling focuses on maximizing the efficiency of inference by strategically increasing test-time compute. Research has shown that smaller models can match or even surpass larger models when given sufficient computational resources at inference.

For instance:

* A 3-billion parameter model, when paired with robust verification mechanisms, can match the performance of a 70-billion parameter model on tasks such as mathematical reasoning.
* Techniques such as _majority voting decoding_ and _reward model reranking_ play a key role in achieving this efficiency.

In this blog post, we’ll concentrate on search-based methods as they represent a practical and scalable solution for test-time compute optimization. In particular, we’ll examine the three strategies illustrated below:

**Best-of-N:**

* Generate multiple responses per problem and assign scores to each candidate answer, typically using a reward model. Then select the answer with the highest reward (or a weighted variant discussed later). This approach emphasizes answer quality over frequency.
* Best-of-N is a simple, but effective extension to majority voting that uses a reward model to determine the most plausible answer. This method comes in two main variants:
* **Vanilla Best-of-N:** Generate N _N_ independent responses and select the one with the _highest RM reward_ as the final answer. This ensures that the most confident individual response is chosen, but it doesn’t account for consistency across answers.
* **Weighted Best-of-N:** Aggregate scores across all identical responses and select the answer with the _highest total reward_. This approach prioritises high-quality answers by boosting their scores through repeated occurrences. Mathematically, the weighting across answers (a _i)_ ​ is performed as follows:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*InIQKd74YEB8G7eJh9nwnA.png)

Typically, one usually uses an outcome reward model (ORM) to get a single, solution-level score. But to allow for fair comparison with the other search strategies discussed later, we will use the same PRM to score the solutions from Best-of-N. As illustrated below, PRMs produce a _cumulative_ _sequence of step-level scores_ per solution, so we need to perform a reduction over the steps to obtain a single solution-level score:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*JD6f66MDACAGRXY-Y24tDg.png)

In the literature, the most common reductions are the following:

* **Min:** use the minimum score across all steps.
* **Prod:** use the product of step-level scores.
* **Last:** use the final score in the steps. This score contains the cumulative information from all prior steps, so treats the PRM effectively as an ORM that is able to score partial solutions.

**Beam search: Beam search with process reward models**

* A systematic search method that explores the solution space, often combined with a [_process reward model (PRM)_](<https://huggingface.co/papers/2211.14275>) to optimise both the sampling and evaluation of intermediate steps in problem-solving. Unlike conventional reward models that produce a single score on the final answer, PRMs provide a _sequence_ of scores, one for each step of the reasoning process. This ability to provide fine-grained feedback makes PRMs a natural fit for search methods with LLMs.
* Beam search being a structured search method that systematically explores the solution space, makes it a powerful tool for improving model outputs at test-time. When combined with a PRM, beam search can optimize both the generation and evaluation of intermediate steps in problem-solving. The way it works is as follows:

1. Generate multiple candidate solutions _iteratively_ by maintaining a fixed number of “beams” or active paths N _N_ .
2. In the first iteration, sample N _N_ independent steps from the LLM with temperature T _T_ to introduce diversity in the responses. These steps are usually defined by a stopping criterion like terminating on a new line `
` or double new line `

`.
3. Score each step with the PRM and select the top N/M steps as candidates for the next round of generation. Here M _M_ denotes the “beam width” of a given active path. As in Best-of-N, we used the “last” reduction to score the partial solutions at each iteration.
4. Expand the steps selected in step (3) by sampling _M_ next steps in the solution.
5. Repeat steps (3) and (4) until the EOS token is reached or the maximum search depth is exceeded.

By allowing the PRM to evaluate the correctness of intermediate steps, beam search can identify and prioritize promising paths early in the process. This step-by-step evaluation is particularly beneficial for complex reasoning tasks like mathematics, where verifying partial solutions can significantly improve final outcomes.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*oSJAbwWSNeWNs1ZNRx-RPA.png)

* **Diverse Verifier Tree Search (DVTS)** DVTS is an advanced search technique designed to improve the accuracy and reliability of model outputs. It works by generating diverse candidate solutions and verifying them through a tree-like evaluation structure. This increases the likelihood of finding a correct and well-reasoned solution.

**Key benefits of DVTS include:**

* Increased diversity in candidate solutions.
* Systematic verification to reduce errors.
* Improved reasoning capabilities, particularly in complex tasks such as multi-step math problems.

## Experimental setup

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*TWTG0UA3G0sLjf88-LWdUQ.png)

As illustrated in the diagram above, the experimental setup involves a pipeline with the following steps:

1. You begin by feeding a math problem to an LLM, which generates N _N_ **_partial solutions_** , e.g. an intermediate step in a derivation.
2. Each step is scored by a PRM, which estimates the probability of each step to eventually reach the correct final answer.
3. The steps and PRM scores are then used by a given search strategy to select which partial solutions should be further explored to generate the next round of intermediate steps.
4. Once the search strategy terminates, the final candidate solutions are ranked by the PRM to produce the final answer.

In order to compare various search strategies, **HuggingFace** reports using the following open models and datasets:

**Model:**
HuggingFace states that they used _meta-llama/Llama-3.2–1B-Instruct_ as the primary model for scaling test-time compute. The 1B parameters keep the model lightweight for rapid iteration, and its unsaturated math performance demonstrates the potential gains of scaling.

**Process Reward Model:**
HuggingFace mentions using _RLHFlow/Llama3.1–8B-PRM-Deepseek-Data_ , an 8B reward model trained with process supervision, to guide the search strategies. Process supervision is described as providing feedback on each step of the reasoning process rather than just the final result. HuggingFace also points out that this model belongs to the same family as its policy model and performed better than other PRMs in the same weight class, such as Math-Shepherd.

**Dataset:**
HuggingFace reports that evaluation took place on the MATH-500 subset of the MATH benchmark, originally released by OpenAI as part of research on process supervision. These math problems span seven subjects and present significant challenges to both humans and most LLMs, and HuggingFace recommends viewing the dataset to appreciate the level of difficulty.

**Supervision Strategies: Process-Based vs. Outcome-Based Feedback** DeepMind’s comprehensive study on supervision techniques sheds light on two primary approaches:

1. **Outcome-Based Feedback:** Supervises models based on whether the final answer is correct. This approach requires less annotation effort but may allow reasoning errors to go unnoticed.
2. **Process-Based Feedback:** Supervises each step of the reasoning process, ensuring that intermediate steps are logically sound.

**Findings:**

* Models trained with process-based feedback exhibit significantly lower _trace error rates_ (errors in reasoning steps) compared to outcome-based models.
* Outcome-based reward models often approximate process-based supervision when fine-tuned correctly.
* Hybrid approaches, combining process-based reasoning traces with outcome verification, deliver the best results.

**Practical Implications and Applications**

1. **Resource Efficiency:** Smaller models require less training data and hardware but can still perform at a high level with optimized test-time computing.
2. **Accessibility:** Reduced compute requirements during training democratize access to state-of-the-art performance.
3. **Scalability:** Decoupling inference from training compute allows organizations to scale inference resources dynamically based on task complexity.

**Future Directions** The intersection of process-based supervision, compute-optimal scaling, and advanced inference techniques like DVTS marks a paradigm shift in language model research. Future exploration may focus on:

* Expanding these techniques to diverse datasets beyond GSM8K.
* Improving verifier architectures for better traceability.
* Further optimizing reward models for efficiency and accuracy.

**Conclusion** Scaling test-time compute represents a transformative approach to enhancing language model performance without the relentless growth of model size. Techniques like DVTS, process-based supervision, and compute-optimal scaling provide powerful tools to achieve efficiency and accuracy simultaneously. By adopting these strategies, smaller models can deliver results that rival their larger counterparts, paving the way for more sustainable and accessible AI solutions.
